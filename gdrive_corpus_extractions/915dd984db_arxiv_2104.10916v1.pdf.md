The source is a proof-of-concept computer-vision pipeline, not a validated injury-prediction system. The multi-hop chain is: in-game video frame \(I_t\) → YOLO ball detection and YOLO person/player detection → Kalman-filtered ball track → ball-carrier association by Euclidean distance from player box centre to ball → tackle-frame selection by the final boundary-box overlap between “spectator” players and ball-carrier → tackler identification by vertical-range condition plus closest horizontal distance to ball-carrier → OpenPose key points → averaged head-centre → binary high-risk/low-risk decision by a head-centre interval → accuracy, recall, precision, F1, and Cohen’s Kappa. The paper states the problem clearly: “Video analysis in tackle-collision based sports is highly subjective and exposed to bias” [PAGE 1], and it claims the system “achieved an evaluation accuracy of 62.50%” [PAGE 1]. That number must be audited because it is not universal: Table 1 shows the 15% high-risk region with \(n=64\) gives 62.50%, but 5% gives 67.19% and 25% gives 51.56% on the same evaluated subset; on \(n=109\) the 15% region gives 36.70% [PAGE 7]. Thus the headline 62.50% is conditional on the high-risk-region width, denominator, and inclusion rules.

The gray-zone begins before modeling. The analyst labels are qualitative and are not referee rulings: “The ground truth data was not based on the referee’s outcome or ruling for the given tackle” [PAGE 4]. That is a strength for avoiding referee bias, but it means the target is an analyst-defined head-proximity heuristic, not a biomechanical injury outcome. The paper is explicit that it is “limited to focusing on the position of the player’s heads in determining the tackle risk” [PAGE 3]. Therefore any claim that the system measures concussion risk directly would be an overreach; it measures a geometric proxy. The proxy is made operational through a high-risk region around the ball-carrier’s head-centre; the tackler’s head-centre inside that region gives high-risk, otherwise low-risk. The region widths tested are 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25%, and 15% is selected post hoc because it gives “the highest F1 Score (0.50), and the highest CKC (0.28)” [PAGE 8]. That selection is a gray-zone optimization on a small sample, not an external validation.

The pipeline has multiple failure surfaces. First, detection: YOLO confidence is an intersection-over-union-scaled objectness score, so missed balls and false balls are possible; the paper notes a frame where “YOLO network incorrectly identifies the ball” and the Kalman filter corrects it [PAGE 6]. Second, tracking: the Kalman filter uses a constant-acceleration model with six states; outlier rejection uses a five-standard-deviation innovation gate and a confidence floor of 0.1 [PAGE 5]. That gate can fail when outliers are consecutive; the paper says consecutive outliers “convinced the algorithm that the outlier points were part of the true dataset” [PAGE 6]. Third, association: ball-carrier is nearest player to ball, and tackler is closest qualifying player in the tackle frame; occlusion can make both assignments wrong. Fourth, pose: OpenPose can mislabel or omit head key points, and the paper warns it is “subjected to potential errors when generating inferences (mislabelling key head points or not being able to generate a label when analysing a video)” [PAGE 5]. Fifth, classification: a small vertical shift in either head-centre can cross the region boundary. Sixth, reporting: accuracy, F1, and Kappa are computed on 64 successfully evaluated tackles out of 109, with 58 running without manual adjustment and six requiring manual parameter changes [PAGE 6-7]. Manual adjustment is itself a gray-zone intervention because it changes the decision rule per clip.

The most important audit is the denominator. The paper reports “a total of 64 tackles were successfully evaluated (73.4% classified as low-risk tackles by the analyst)” [PAGE 7]. That means 45 of 109 clips did not produce a successful automated evaluation. The reported 62.50% cannot be treated as an end-to-end success rate over all supplied tackles; the end-to-end accuracy at the same 15% region over 109 clips is 36.70% [PAGE 7]. The non-evaluated clips are not random missingness: they are likely enriched for occlusion, low resolution, unusual zoom, or tracking failure. Thus the gray-zone conclusion is that the system is a feasibility demonstration of markerless pose estimation in rugby, not a deployable referee aid. A valid deployment claim would need: prespecified high-risk-region width, prespecified inclusion rules, no per-clip manual tuning, calibration on held-out competitions, and separate reporting for all attempted clips versus successfully processed clips.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source operators are detection, tracking, association, pose extraction, head-centre comparator, and metric evaluation. I formalize them with the paper’s stated quantities; units are given explicitly. Assumptions are stated where the paper does not fully specify geometry.

1. YOLO detection operator. For frame \(I_t\), YOLO returns boxes \(\mathbf b_i=(x_i,y_i,w_i,h_i)\) in pixels and confidence \(c_i\in[0,1]\). The paper defines:
\[
c=\Pr(\text{Object})\cdot \frac{A_{\text{overlap}}}{A_{\text{union}}},
\]
where \(A_{\text{overlap}}\) and \(A_{\text{union}}\) are areas in \(\mathrm{px}^2\), and \(c\) is dimensionless [PAGE 4]. The paper states the confidence score is set to zero if no object is detected [PAGE 4]. Assumption: the detector is run per frame; the paper uses a single-class ball detector and a separate person/player detector [PAGE 2-3]. Singularity: if \(A_{\text{union}}=0\), the ratio is undefined; the paper’s no-object rule sets confidence to zero [PAGE 4].

2. Training-size operators. The paper gives:
\[
N_{\text{iter}}=2000\,N_{\text{classes}},\qquad F=(N_{\text{classes}}+5)\cdot 3,
\]
where \(N_{\text{iter}}\) is iterations (count), \(F\) is filter count, and \(N_{\text{classes}}\) is class count [PAGE 4]. For one class, these yield 2000 iterations and 18 filters, matching the paper’s stated single-class setup [PAGE 4]. Units are counts, not physical units. Breakdown: these are heuristics from the cited Darknet guidance, not general laws; they do not guarantee convergence for domain-shifted rugby imagery.

3. Kalman tracking operator. The state vector is:
\[
\mathbf x_k=[p_x,v_x,a_x,p_y,v_y,a_y]^\top,
\]
with position in \(\mathrm{px}\), velocity in \(\mathrm{px\,s^{-1}}\), and acceleration in \(\mathrm{px\,s^{-2}}\); measurement is \(\mathbf z_k=[p_x,p_y]^\top\) [PAGE 4-5]. The innovation is:
\[
\tilde{\mathbf y}_k=\mathbf z_k-H\hat{\mathbf x}_{k|k-1}.
\]
The paper rejects a measurement when the YOLO confidence is below 0.1 or the innovation falls outside five times the square root of the innovation covariance \(S_k\), and then sets the innovation to zero [PAGE 5]. Formally, the gate is:
\[
\text{outlier if } c_k<0.1 \quad \text{or} \quad \|\tilde{\mathbf y}_k\|>5\sqrt{S_k}.
\]
Units: \(\tilde{\mathbf y}_k\) in px; \(S_k\) in \(\mathrm{px}^2\); the threshold \(5\sqrt{S_k}\) in px. Breakdown: the paper reports that “a large jump in the measured position would cause the filter to diverge and become unstable” [PAGE 6]. If the innovation covariance is singular or near-singular, the gate is ill-conditioned. Consecutive outliers can corrupt the track because the filter updates toward the outlier sequence [PAGE 6].

4. Association operators. Ball-carrier selection is:
\[
p^*=\arg\min_{p\in P_t}\|\mathbf c_p-\mathbf c_{\text{ball}}\|_2,
\]
where \(\mathbf c_p\) and \(\mathbf c_{\text{ball}}\) are box-centre coordinates in px [PAGE 5]. Tackle frame is the final frame with boundary-box overlap between “spectator” players and the ball-carrier; the paper says “the final overlap was labelled as the tackle frame” [PAGE 5]. The tackler is the player whose box-centre \(y\)-coordinate lies within the ball-carrier’s box height range and whose horizontal box-centre distance to the ball-carrier is smallest in the tackle frame [PAGE 5]. Assumption: one ball and one ball-carrier; the camera follows the ball-carrier [PAGE 4]. Breakdown: occlusion and box overlap before true contact can make the final-overlap rule select an early frame [PAGE 8].

5. Pose and head-centre operator. OpenPose yields 18 key points [PAGE 5]. The head-centre is:
\[
\mathbf h=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{j\in\{\text{LE},\text{RE},\text{LEye},\text{REye},\text{N}\}}\mathbf p_j,
\]
where LE/RE are left/right ear, LEye/REye are left/right eye, N is nose, and \(\mathbf p_j\) is in px [PAGE 5]. For the ball-carrier, the final three frames are averaged after Kalman filtering [PAGE 5]. For the tackler, because prior-frame tracking is unavailable, the paper averages OpenPose’s head \(y\)-coordinate with an estimate from the head-to-body ratio:
\[
y_T^{\text{est}}=y_{\text{top}}+0.075H_{\text{bbox}},\qquad \hat y_T=\frac{1}{2}\left(y_T^{\text{OP}}+y_T^{\text{est}}\right),
\]
where \(y_{\text{top}}\) and \(H_{\text{bbox}}\) are in px and the 0.075 factor comes from the stated head-to-body ratio of 1:8 and the assumption that head-centre is the middle of the head [PAGE 5]. Assumption: the vertical body axis is approximately aligned with image \(y\); this is not guaranteed in side-on or rotated tackles. Breakdown: OpenPose can merge two players into one skeleton under occlusion [PAGE 8], and low resolution reduces key-point mapping [PAGE 8].

6. Classification comparator. Let \(y_B\) be the ball-carrier head-centre \(y\)-coordinate, \(y_T\) the tackler head-centre \(y\)-coordinate, \(L\) a normalized body-height scale in px, and \(r\in\{0.05,0.10,0.15,0.20,0.25\}\). A formal symmetric implementation of the paper’s high-risk region is:
\[
\hat R_r=1 \quad \text{if} \quad y_T\in[y_B-rL,\;y_B+rL],
\]
and \(\hat R_r=0\) otherwise. The paper states the high-risk region is a “boundary limit with the head-centre of the ball-carrier located in the middle” and tests limits relative to the ball-carrier’s head-centre at 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% [PAGE 5]. Assumption: the region is treated as a symmetric vertical interval around \(y_B\); if the actual implementation uses asymmetric limits, the same logic applies with two endpoints. Units: \(y_B,y_T,L\) in px; \(r\) dimensionless. Singularity: when \(|y_T-y_B|=rL\), the decision is exactly on the boundary and needs a tie rule; if a measurement error \(\delta_T-\delta_B\) exceeds the margin
\[
m=rL-|y_T-y_B|,
\]
the classification can flip. Thus the risk label is unstable when head-centre estimation error is comparable to \(rL\). At \(r=0.05\), the window is narrowest and most sensitive to pose error.

7. Metrics. Accuracy is:
\[
A=\frac{N_{\text{correct}}}{N},\qquad N\ge1,
\]
dimensionless [PAGE 7]. F1 is:
\[
F_1=\frac{\mathrm{CHD}}{\mathrm{CHD}+0.5(\mathrm{HLL}+\mathrm{LLH})},
\]
where CHD is correct high-risk detections, HLL is high-risk labelled low-risk, and LLH is low-risk labelled high-risk; all are counts, and \(F_1\) is dimensionless [PAGE 7]. Cohen’s Kappa is:
\[
\kappa=\frac{P_o-P_e}{1-P_e},\qquad P_e\ne1,
\]
dimensionless, where \(P_o\) is observed agreement and \(P_e\) is random agreement; the paper reports a 15% CKC of 0.28 as “a fair level of reliability” [PAGE 8]. Breakdown/singularity: if \(N=0\), accuracy is undefined; if the F1 denominator is zero, F1 is undefined; if \(P_e=1\), Kappa is undefined. The paper’s Table 1 shows strong metric dependence on \(r\): at 5% for \(n=64\), accuracy 67.19%, recall 29.41%, precision 35.71%, F1 0.32; at 15%, accuracy 62.50%, recall 70.59%, precision 38.71%, F1 0.50; at 25%, accuracy 51.56%, recall 82.35%, precision 33.33%, F1 0.47 [PAGE 7]. Therefore any single accuracy value without \(r\), \(N\), recall, precision, F1, and Kappa is not a complete report.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from injury epidemiology and video analysis to markerless computer vision. The paper situates tackle injury as the highest-incidence injury category in Rugby Union and notes concussion incidence across rugby union, rugby sevens, and rugby league [PAGE 1-2]. It then links prevention to technique and law: World Rugby implemented stricter high-tackle rulings and trialled lowering the legal tackle height from above the line of the shoulders to above the line of the armpit [PAGE 2]. Because enforcement depends on referee judgment, and because “Referee decisions however, are influenced by a number of factors such as decision-making time, home advantage and crowd” [PAGE 2], the paper proposes an objective CV aid. The retrocausal direction is clear: the future goal of consistent, objective referee assistance shapes the present dataset selection, label definition, and output threshold. The system is deliberately scoped to head proximity, not legal/illegal ruling, because “This paper is limited to focusing on the position of the player’s heads in determining the tackle risk” [PAGE 3]. Future work proposes predicting legal/illegal rulings from full pose [PAGE 8], which would be a different target with a different label genealogy.

The prior technical lineage includes sensor-based accelerometry, marker-based motion capture, and markerless pose estimation. The paper notes that accelerometers alone do not provide enough information to identify tackle risk, and that markers can be invasive or confined to controlled environments [PAGE 2]. It cites OpenPose and YOLO as markerless tools and claims “to our knowledge, there is currently no previous study which utilized computer vision for tackle risk evaluation” [PAGE 2]. That claim is historically situated: the paper is a 2021 proof-of-concept using YOLOv4, TensorFlow OpenPose, and a Kalman filter on broadcast rugby video. Its genealogy is therefore not a direct line from biomechanics to validated injury prediction; it is a line from observational video analysis to automated geometric proxy extraction.

Multi-week traps are substantial. First, label drift: the ground truth is analyst-defined and “not based on the referee’s outcome or ruling” [PAGE 4]; over repeated match weeks, analyst criteria for head proximity can drift, especially at boundary cases. Second, domain drift: training images came from open-source sources with keywords “sports”, “rugby”, and “ball”, while test clips were Rugby World Cup and Super Rugby front-on one-on-one tackles [PAGE 3-4]. A model tuned to that distribution may fail under different leagues, cameras, kits, lighting, or angles. Third, camera drift: the camera follows the ball-carrier and is not stationary [PAGE 4]; zoom and resolution changes alter bounding-box scale and OpenPose key-point quality. Fourth, law/rule drift: high-tackle law changes alter what counts as dangerous, so historical labels may not transfer across seasons [PAGE 2]. Fifth, threshold drift: the 15% region was chosen because it had the highest F1 and CKC on 64 clips [PAGE 8]; a later dataset could move the optimum, making the deployed threshold stale. Sixth, feedback drift: if future systems are trained on referee decisions, the model inherits the same inconsistencies the paper seeks to reduce. The paper’s own warning about “intra-competition and inter-competition inconsistencies” in high-tackle sanctioning [PAGE 2] is therefore also a warning about label provenance.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The system sits inside a multi-agent loop: analyst, referee, assistant referees, television match official, players, coaches, and spectators. The paper’s motivation is that human video analysis is subjective and time-constrained [PAGE 1], and that referee decisions are influenced by decision-making time, home advantage, and crowd [PAGE 2]. A recursive model must represent not only what the classifier outputs, but what each agent believes the classifier is for. If referees treat a 62.50% system as authoritative, the system can become an automation-bias anchor. If coaches learn that the classifier keys on head-centre alignment, they may train players to manipulate posture in ways that satisfy the geometric rule without reducing actual injury risk. If analysts know the system’s threshold, their labels may drift toward the system’s output, creating a circular label trap. The paper partly guards against this by not using referee rulings as ground truth [PAGE 4], but it does not eliminate analyst feedback.

Crisis habit regression is the first failure mode. Under match time pressure, a referee or analyst may regress to a habitual head-proximity judgment, which is exactly the heuristic the model encodes. The paper notes that video analysis is “highly subjective and exposed to bias” [PAGE 1], and that referee decisions are influenced by crowd and time [PAGE 2]. A 62.50% model on 64 selected clips is not strong enough to override that habit; it can only flag a case for review. The counter-trap is overcorrection: if referees defer to the model, they may miss high-risk tackles that the model labels low-risk, or penalize low-risk tackles that fall inside the 25% region. The paper’s own class imbalance matters here: 73.4% of evaluated tackles were low-risk by the analyst [PAGE 7], so a model biased toward low-risk can appear accurate while missing high-risk events. At 5%, recall is only 29.41%; at 25%, recall rises to 82.35% but precision falls to 33.33% [PAGE 7]. A referee using the 25% region would see more high-risk flags but more false alarms; using 5% would see fewer flags but more misses. The “clock” is therefore both literal and procedural: tackle frames occur within a second before contact [PAGE 5], video is 25-30 fps [PAGE 4], and the system averages the final three frames for the ball-carrier [PAGE 5]. That short temporal window limits how much evidence can be integrated, and it makes late or early tackle-frame selection a decision-confidence issue.

The recursive theory-of-mind conclusion is that the system should be framed as a review aid, not a referee replacement. The paper itself says “A system that can objectively classify tackle head injury risk may assist referees with their decision-making” [PAGE 2], not replace it. A safe interaction protocol would require the referee to see the raw head-centre overlay, the high-risk-region width, and a confidence/validity flag; if occlusion or resolution vetoes the input, the system should abstain. That abstention is a theory-of-mind safeguard: it prevents the human from treating a machine abstention as a negative judgment.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A valid risk label must pass four layers: physical input validity, biomechanical validity, environmental/weather validity, and statistical/decision validity. If any layer vetoes, the label should be withheld rather than downgraded to “low-risk.”

Physical input layer. The paper omits clips when “players’ boundary box were &lt;14% of the video frame pixel height” and/or “too much occlusion occurred” [PAGE 4]. That is an explicit scale veto. Occlusion is also a pose veto: “OpenPose tends to generate key points for one player across two people” [PAGE 8]. If two players share one skeleton, the tackler and ball-carrier head-centres are not independently measured; the classification equation \(y_T\in[y_B-rL,y_B+rL]\) is undefined in practice because \(y_T\) and \(y_B\) may come from the same merged skeleton. Low resolution is another physical veto: the paper states “higher resolution indicates an improved key point mapping” [PAGE 8]. If key-point error exceeds the margin \(m=rL-|y_T-y_B|\), the binary label can flip. Proof: the classification depends only on the vertical ordering and distance of two estimated head-centres; any error larger than the remaining margin crosses the boundary. At \(r=0.05\), the margin is smallest, so the veto threshold is strictest.

Biomechanical layer. The paper’s target is head-centre proximity, not impact mechanics. It states the study is “limited to focusing on the position of the player’s heads in determining the tackle risk” [PAGE 3]. It also notes that acceleration alone does not provide enough information to identify tackle risk [PAGE 2]. Therefore a valid injury-risk claim would require at minimum impact velocity, acceleration, body orientation, neck posture, and contact vector. The present system has none of these as primary inputs. A biomechanical veto is triggered when the question is “will this tackle cause injury?” rather than “are the head centres vertically proximate in this frame?” The system can answer the latter; it cannot answer the former. This is not a criticism of the proof-of-concept, but it is a hard adjudication boundary.

Weather/environmental layer. The paper includes “various light intensity images” in ball-detection training and limits them to the ability for the user to see the rugby ball [PAGE 3]. It does not report rain, shadow, glare, or mud robustness. Video quality is a documented challenge: the network is “not familiar with low quality images (OpenPose), making it much harder for the network to identify key points” [PAGE 8]. A weather/environmental veto is therefore triggered when lighting, rain, shadow, or broadcast compression degrades key-point detection below the classification margin. The proof is the same error-propagation argument: if pose error is comparable to \(rL\), the label is not identifiable from the video. The system should abstain, not output low-risk.

Statistical/decision layer. The reported 62.50% is conditional: it is the 15% region on 64 successfully evaluated tackles [PAGE 7]. The paper states 64 were successfully evaluated and 73.4% were low-risk by the analyst [PAGE 7]. The 15% region was selected because it gave the highest F1 (0.50) and CKC (0.28) [PAGE 8]. A statistical veto is triggered when a claim omits \(r\), \(N\), the 109-to-64 inclusion filter, or the manual adjustments used for six clips [PAGE 6-7]. Also, the high-risk class is small: 17 of 64 evaluated clips are high-risk, so recall and precision are unstable. The Kappa of 0.28 is “a fair level of reliability” [PAGE 8], not strong agreement. Therefore the decision layer vetoes any statement that the system is deployment-ready or that 62.50% is a general accuracy.

Prediction: changing the deployed high-risk region from 15% to 5% will increase apparent accuracy on the same evaluated set but will sharply reduce high-risk recall; changing to 25% will increase high-risk recall but reduce precision and accuracy. Therefore the correct report is a metric table with \(r\), \(N\), recall, precision, F1, and CKC, not a single accuracy. The paper’s own conclusion selects 15% as “the most reliable” because it is a midway point with the highest F1 and CKC [PAGE 8]; that selection is a design choice on this dataset, not an invariant of rugby tackle risk.
