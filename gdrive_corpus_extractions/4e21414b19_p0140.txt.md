Historically, this era created a multi-week (or multi-generational) trap: the "Biometrician-Bateson" debate. By reducing selection to the multiplication of two error curves, Galton entrenched the belief that evolution was solely a matter of shifting means and variances of continuous traits, effectively blinding early 20th-century biologists to the discrete, particulate nature of Mendelian alleles. The assumption that $M$ remains completely unchanged under selection became a dogmatic trap; when mutation pressures or genetic drift introduced asymmetrical variations, the elegant symmetry of the $y \times y'$ equation collapsed. It took the advent of Fisher’s fundamental theorem in the 1930s to retrocausally rescue Galton's intuition by proving how continuous variance could emerge from discrete alleles—a reconciliation Galton himself never achieved.
