The source is a machine-learning optimization paper, not a sports, physical, biomechanical, or weather source. The central multi-hop derivation is: standard adversarial training starts from the true robust 0-1 risk \(\min_\theta \mathbb E \max_{\|\eta\|\le\epsilon} \{\arg\max_i f_\theta(X+\eta)_i\ne Y\}\) [PAGE 3]. Because the indicator is discontinuous, practitioners replace it with a differentiable surrogate \(\ell\), giving the surrogate zero-sum game \(\min_\theta \mathbb E \max_{\|\eta\|\le\epsilon} \ell(f_\theta(X+\eta),Y)\) [PAGE 3]. The paper’s first key move is to show that this replacement is not guarantee-preserving for the attacker: maximizing an upper bound on the 0-1 loss does not guarantee increasing the 0-1 loss. The paper states this sharply: “the RHS of (9) can be arbitrarily large while the left hand side can simultaneously be equal to zero” [PAGE 4]. Example 1 makes the failure concrete: with \(z_A=(1/K+\epsilon,1/K-\epsilon,\dots)\), \(z_B=(0.5-\epsilon,0.5+\epsilon,0,\dots)\), the cross-entropy of \(z_A\) diverges as \(K\to\infty\) and \(\epsilon\to0\), while \(z_B\) is the adversarial case; hence “an adversary maximizing the cross-entropy will always choose zA over zB and will therefore fail to identify the adversarial example” [PAGE 4].

The paper’s repair is to decouple attacker and defender objectives. The defender may still minimize a differentiable upper bound, because that is exactly what surrogate minimization is for supervised learning [PAGE 2]. The attacker must instead maximize the true misclassification event. The paper introduces the negative margin \(M_\theta(X,Y)_j \triangleq f_\theta(X)_j - f_\theta(X)_Y\) [PAGE 5]. Proposition 1 says that if \((j^\star,\eta^\star)\in\arg\max_{j\in[K]-\{Y\},\|\eta\|\le\epsilon} M_\theta(X+\eta,Y)_j\), then a positive maximal margin \(M_\theta(X+\eta^\star,Y)_{j^\star}>0\) certifies misclassification, while a non-positive maximal margin means no adversarial example exists inside the strict ball \(\|\eta\|<\epsilon\) [PAGE 5]. This converts the discontinuous inner maximization into \(K-1\) differentiable targeted margin maximizations, yielding the non-zero-sum bilevel problem in eqs. (16)-(20) [PAGE 5]. BETA is the algorithmic realization: initialize one perturbation per class, optimize \(\eta_j\) against \(M_\theta(x+\eta_j,y)_j\), project onto \(B_\epsilon(X)\cap[0,1]^d\), select \(j^\star=\arg\max_{j\ne y}M_\theta(x+\eta_j,y)_j\), and return \(\eta_{j^\star}\) [PAGE 6]. BETA-AT then trains on the selected perturbed point [PAGE 6].

The gray-zone audit matters. First, the paper’s core guarantee is conditional on the inner optimizer actually finding a maximizer or a sufficiently positive margin; it is not a global convergence theorem for the full nonconvex bilevel game. Second, the margin-zero boundary is delicate: \(M=0\) is a tie, and the strict positivity condition in Proposition 1 does not resolve argmax ties or boundary points at \(\|\eta\|=\epsilon\) without additional convention [PAGE 5]. Third, the smoothing appendix contains a sign/inequality gray zone. The source writes \(\max_{\lambda}\langle\lambda,M\rangle\ge \max_{\lambda}[\langle\lambda,M\rangle-\frac1\mu\sum_j\lambda_j\log\lambda_j]=\frac1\mu\log\sum_j e^{\mu M_j}\) and says “The inequality here is due to the fact that the entropy of a discrete probability \(\lambda\) is positive” [PAGE 16]. But with \(\mu>0\) and \(\lambda_j\in[0,1]\), \(-\sum_j\lambda_j\log\lambda_j\ge0\), and the log-sum-exp term is generally an upper bound on the hard maximum, not a lower bound. The paper later says “the smoothing is obtained through a lower bound of the original objective” [PAGE 17]. A literal reading cannot have both the displayed inequality and the lower-bound claim in general. Operationally, SBETA-AT is a soft-max reweighting that recovers the hard maximum only in the \(\mu\to\infty\) limit or when margin positivity and class selection are audited; finite-\(\mu\) behavior should be treated as a regularized approximation, not an exact equivalence. Fourth, the empirical claim is CIFAR-10 \(\ell_\infty\) robustness, not physical, biomechanical, or weather robustness: the paper explicitly says “we evaluate the performance of BETA and BETA-AT on CIFAR-10” [PAGE 7], with \(\epsilon=8/255\) and step size \(\alpha=2/255\) [PAGE 7]. The lineage is a sequence of attempts to make adversarial training optimizable, followed by corrections to what the surrogate actually guarantees. Szegedy et al. (2013) and Biggio et al. (2020) identify robust overfitting, where robust test accuracy peaks after the first learning-rate decay and then falls [PAGE 7]. Croce and Hein (2020) introduce AutoAttack and expose overestimation by weaker attacks [PAGE 3, PAGE 8]. RobustBench standardizes leaderboard evaluation [PAGE 7].

The paper’s retrocausal claim is that the field’s heuristic drift—multiple restarts, variable schedules, stopping conditions—is a symptom of the surrogate zero-sum formulation, not an independent empirical nuisance [PAGE 2, PAGE 3]. The source says “robust overfitting is an artifact of the use of improper surrogates in the original AT paradigm” [PAGE 2]. It also says “surrogate-based adversarial attacks are known to overestimate robustness” [PAGE 3]. The lineage trap is therefore long-horizon: one can spend many training epochs and many leaderboard cycles optimizing the wrong inner objective. The source gives epochs, not weeks; any “multi-week” extrapolation is out of distribution. The defensible long-horizon trap is epoch-level: PGD displays robust overfitting after the first learning-rate decay, while BETA-AT does not [PAGE 7].

A second lineage branch is calibration and consistency. The paper cites Bao et al. (2020), Meunier et al. (2022), and Awasthi et al. (2021) on whether convex surrogates are calibrated for adversarial 0-1 risk, and it cites Frank and Niles-Weed (2023) as a future margin-consistency direction [PAGE 9]. The trap here is transferring clean-classification calibration intuition to adversarial classification without checking the inner maximization. The paper’s negative-margin construction is a direct attempt to avoid that transfer error.

A third branch is bilevel and multitargeted attack work. Gowal et al. (2019) found multitargeted attacks estimate robustness more accurately, but their theorem is limited to linear functions; the present paper claims Proposition 1 holds for arbitrary \(f_\theta\) [PAGE 5, PAGE 9]. Mianjy and Arora (2024) study a related reflected cross-entropy bilevel problem in binary classification [PAGE 9]. The multi-week trap is to assume that any bilevel rewriting preserves the same attack semantics; the source distinguishes its margin-based rewriting from FastAT linearization and from reflected cross-entropy.

The final lineage trap is evaluative: AutoAttack uses four attacks, 100 iterations, and five restarts [PAGE 8], while BETA uses 30 iterations and a single restart [PAGE 8]. The source reports BETA/AA gaps from \(0.03\) to \(0.17\) percentage points across top RobustBench models [PAGE 8], and BETA is \(5.11\times\) faster on average [PAGE 19]. A long-horizon evaluation program that ignores compute asymmetry can misattribute robustness to restarts rather than to the attacker objective. The source’s counter-lineage is “heuristic-free” evaluation with the negative-margin objective [PAGE 8].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. The standard defender models the attacker as a surrogate maximizer, and the attacker models the defender through the same cross-entropy surface. This is a shared-intention fiction: both players act as if maximizing \(\ell\) is the same as maximizing the 0-1 event. The paper shows it is not: “maximizing a surrogate like the cross-entropy provides no guarantee that the the classification error will increase” [PAGE 2]. The habit regression appears as weak attackers and ineffective defenders [PAGE 4], then as robust overfitting after the first learning-rate decay [PAGE 7]. In recursive terms, the defender’s model of the attacker is systematically optimistic; the attacker’s model of the defender is misaligned with the true loss; the resulting training loop reinforces the wrong equilibrium.

Counter-trap. BETA changes the attacker’s mental model from “maximize one shared surrogate” to “for each wrong class \(j\ne y\), solve a targeted margin problem \(M_\theta(x+\eta,y)_j\), then select the class with the largest positive margin” [PAGE 5, PAGE 6]. This is a counter-trap because it does not try to beat the surrogate at its own game; it forces the attacker to represent each alternative class separately. The defender still uses a surrogate upper bound, but now the attacker’s lower-level objective is the exact misclassification event under the differentiability assumption. The source states: “the attacker can maximize the classification error directly using first-order optimization methods without resorting to a relaxation” [PAGE 5]. The recursive asymmetry is the point: defender minimizes an upper bound; attacker maximizes the exact event. The paper summarizes this as “the attacker ought to maximize a lower bound on the classification error … whereas the defender ought to minimize an upper bound” [PAGE 4].

Clock. The training clock exposes the habit. PGD robust test accuracy peaks after the first learning-rate decay and then drops, while BETA-AT does not suffer robust overfitting [PAGE 7]. Thus the clock-aware policy is not “train longer and restart more”; it is “align the attacker objective, then use last-iterate checkpoints because overfitting is reduced.” The recursive trap is to mistake extra clock time for better theory of the opponent.
