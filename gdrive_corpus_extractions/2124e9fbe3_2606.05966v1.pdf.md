<THINK_TRACE>
The benchmark should be read as a causal measurement apparatus rather than a conventional question-answer collection. The paper states that “CausalPhys, a benchmark of over 3,000 carefully curated video- and image-based questions spanning four domains: Perception, Anticipation, Intervention, and Goal Orientation” [PAGE 1], and that “Each question is paired with an expert-annotated causal graph capturing object–attribute–event dependencies” [PAGE 1]. The analytic chain therefore begins with a visual input \(X\), a query \(Q\), a ground-truth answer \(Y^*\), and a typed causal graph \(G\). The evaluated model returns a rationale and answer pair \((R,Y)\), as specified by “(𝑅,𝑌) = VLM(𝑋,𝑄)” [PAGE 5]. The central derivation is that answer correctness alone is insufficient: the benchmark projects the generated rationale onto the graph’s nodes and edges, producing four metrics. Accuracy measures exact outcome matching, Entity Faithfulness measures whether graph entities are mentioned, Relation Awareness measures whether directed causal dependencies are expressed, and Description Correctness measures whether attributes or events are described consistently with annotation.

The gray-zone audit begins with the causal hierarchy. The paper maps categories to Pearl-style causal operators: “Each of the four major categories corresponds to a causal range [43] (𝑃{𝑌| 𝑋}, 𝑃{𝑌| do(𝑋)}, 𝑃{Goal | do(𝑋)}, 𝑃{𝑌| do(𝑋′)})” [PAGE 2]. Perception is observational, Anticipation and Intervention are interventional, and Goal Orientation is counterfactual or planning-oriented. This matters because a model can obtain high Accuracy by exploiting observational regularities while still failing the interventional or counterfactual structure. The paper detects exactly this failure mode: “models reliably identify constituent objects and attributes (high EF, ≈0.7) but fail to synthesize them into coherent causal structures (low RA, ≈0.2-0.3)” [PAGE 6]. The benchmark also reports that “Relation-intensive subsets, specifically viewpoint transformation and optical inference, pose the greatest challenge; most models perform near-chance (below 40% or ≈0.3)” [PAGE 5]. Thus, the central diagnostic is not merely low accuracy but a structural decoupling between recognition and relational synthesis.

A second gray zone is the LLM-as-judge. The metrics depend on binary decisions \(M(z,R)\in\{0,1\}\) where the judge verifies whether an entity, relation, or description is present and correct [PAGE 5]. This introduces measurement assumptions: the rationale must be explicit enough for the judge to detect the target element. The paper validates this with reliability analysis, reporting “Both agreement settings exceed 0.80 (almost-perfect)” [PAGE 13]. Nevertheless, the metrics remain sensitive to paraphrase, coreference, and implicit causal language. The judging prompt states that for causal relation questions one must “require a clear causal/influence expression” and that “Mere co-occurrence is insufficient” [PAGE 17]. This is a strict criterion: a rationale that mentions two objects but does not state influence between them will receive low Relation Awareness even if the final answer is correct.

A third gray zone is training signal design. The paper’s Causal Rationale Fine-Tuning objective combines rationale likelihood and answer likelihood: “LCRFT(𝜃) =−E [ 𝜆𝑟 ∑ log 𝜋𝜃(𝑠𝑡|𝑋,𝑄,𝑠<𝑡) +𝜆𝑦 ∑ log 𝜋𝜃(𝑠𝑡|𝑋,𝑄,𝑠<𝑡) ]” [PAGE 7]. The important inference is that CRFT is not merely longer chain-of-thought supervision. The paper shows that answer-only fine-tuning can inflate accuracy while collapsing causal metrics, and that “SFT fine-tuning tends to make the model behave like a ‘guesser,’ prioritizing concluding final answers based on shallow experience rather than over structured, interpretable reasoning chains” [PAGE 8]. The comparison against CoT-SFT is crucial: “CoT-SFT trails CRFT on every causal metric (EF 0.4280 vs. 0.5969, RA 0.2080 vs. 0.2554)” [PAGE 8]. Therefore, the causal graph acts as a structural regularizer, not just a textual style prompt.

The final multi-hop inference is that CausalPhys separates three layers: perceptual extraction, causal composition, and answer selection. High Entity Faithfulness shows perceptual extraction can succeed. Low Relation Awareness shows causal composition fails. Answer-only accuracy can remain deceptively high because multiple-choice shortcuts or object co-occurrence statistics can predict the label. The benchmark’s deeper claim is that trustworthy physical reasoning requires the rationale to instantiate the graph’s directed dependencies, especially under intervention and viewpoint transformation.

The paper states: “Formally, each graph is a directed acyclic graph G = (V, E), where nodes V represent causal variables and edges E denote directed dependencies” [PAGE 4]. Each node is typed: “every node 𝑣∈V is defined as a typed tuple (𝑣𝜏, 𝑣𝑛, 𝑣𝑑): 𝑣𝜏specifies the semantic type, 𝑣𝑛gives the name of entity, and the optional 𝑣𝑑stores descriptive details” [PAGE 4]. The semantic classes are Objects \(O\), Attributes \(A\), and Events \(E\) [PAGE 4]. The edge semantics are given by “(𝑢, 𝑣) ∈E ⇐⇒ 𝑢is a direct cause of 𝑣” [PAGE 4]. Units: \(G\) is dimensionless as a combinatorial object; \(|V|\) is a count of nodes; \(|E|\) is a count of directed edges; node names are symbolic strings; descriptions are natural-language predicates.

\[
(R,Y) = \mathrm{VLM}(X,Q)
\]
where \(X\) is the visual input, \(Q\) is the query, \(R\) is the rationale, and \(Y\) is the predicted answer [PAGE 5]. Units: \(X\) may be an image, image sequence, or video; the source states that image sequences are constructed by “sampling up to 8 evenly spaced frames from a video” [PAGE 11] and that “Video instances are curated as short clips capped at 5 seconds” [PAGE 11]. Time is measured in seconds, frame index is dimensionless, and spatial image coordinates are measured in pixels after normalization to \(442 \times 442\) [PAGE 11].

The judge operator is binary:
\[
M(z,R) \in \{0,1\}
\]
where \(M(z,R)=1\) if the judge confirms that target element \(z\) is correctly reflected in \(R\) [PAGE 5]. Units: \(M\) is dimensionless and binary. Assumptions: \(z\) may be an entity, relation, or description; the judge is fact-based and constrained by the ground-truth causal graph [PAGE 5].

The four metrics are defined as follows.

Accuracy:
\[
\mathrm{ACC} = \mathbf{1}\{Y = Y^*\}
\]
[PAGE 5]. Units: dimensionless, range \([0,1]\). Breakdown regime: if the answer format is malformed or if the judge or parser cannot extract \(Y\), the indicator becomes ill-defined or false.

Entity Faithfulness:
\[
\mathrm{EF} = \frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{v \in V} M(v_n,R)
\]
[PAGE 5]. Units: dimensionless average over node-name mentions. Breakdown regime: if \(|V|=0\), the denominator is undefined; in practice the graph is nonempty. If rationales are extremely terse, EF collapses because entities are absent.

Relation Awareness:
\[
\mathrm{RA} = \frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{(u,v)\in E} M((u_n,v_n),R)
\]
[PAGE 5]. Units: dimensionless average over directed edge expressions. Breakdown regime: if \(|E|=0\), the metric is undefined; in practice causal graphs contain edges. RA is brittle under implicit causality because the judging prompt says “Mere co-occurrence is insufficient” [PAGE 17].

Description Correctness:
\[
\mathrm{DC} = \frac{1}{|A \cup E|}\sum_{v \in (A \cup E)} M(v_d,R)
\]
[PAGE 5]. Units: dimensionless average over attribute/event description checks. Breakdown regime: if no attribute or event nodes have descriptions, the denominator may be empty; the formulation assumes at least one describable node.

The causal ladder appears in the benchmark taxonomy: association, intervention, and counterfactuals [PAGE 4]. In probabilistic notation, the source references \(P(Y|X)\), \(P(Y|do(X))\), \(P(\mathrm{Goal}|do(X))\), and \(P(Y|do(X'))\) [PAGE 2]. Probability measures are dimensionless, with values in \([0,1]\). If one converts probabilities to information units, self-information is \(-\log p\), measured in bits for base-2 logarithms or nats for natural logarithms.

The CRFT training objective is:
\[
L_{\mathrm{CRFT}}(\theta)
=
-\mathbb{E}
\left[
\lambda_r
\sum_{t \in \mathrm{idx}(R_{\mathrm{gold}})}
\log \pi_\theta(s_t|X,Q,s_{<t})
+
\lambda_y
\sum_{t \in \mathrm{idx}(Y^*)}
\log \pi_\theta(s_t|X,Q,s_{<t})
\right]
\]
[PAGE 7]. Units: \(\log \pi_\theta\) is a log probability; if natural logarithm is used, the loss is in nats per token; if base-2 logarithm is used, it is in bits per token. \(\lambda_r\) and \(\lambda_y\) are dimensionless weights. The source notes a sweep over \(\lambda_{rat}\in\{0.1,0.2,0.5\}\) and \(\lambda_{ans}\in\{0.5,1,2\}\), selecting \(\lambda_{rat}=0.2\) and \(\lambda_{ans}=1\) [PAGE 17]. Breakdown regimes: if \(\lambda_r\) is too small, the model ignores causal rationale; if \(\lambda_y\) dominates, the model may converge toward answer-only guessing. The paper observes that “MCQ answers are often only one token and answer-only optimization can be unstable” [PAGE 17].

The judge reliability operators are Cohen’s kappa values, reported as dimensionless agreement statistics: “LLM–Human Agreement (𝜅) … 0.82” and “Cross-LLM Consistency (𝜅) … 0.84” [PAGE 13]. Units: dimensionless. Breakdown regime: kappa can be unstable if class prevalence is skewed; the source mitigates this by evaluating 320 samples, “20 per subcategory” [PAGE 13].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage of CausalPhys is retro-causal in the sense that it looks backward from current VLM failure modes to older theories of physical intuition, causal hierarchy, and diagnostic benchmarking. The paper begins with the claim that “Understanding and reasoning about the physical world lies at the heart of intelligence” and that humans are “guided by an intuitive grasp of physical causality that emerges early in cognitive development” [PAGE 1]. This places the work in a lineage that treats physical reasoning not as pattern recognition alone but as structured inference over objects, attributes, and events.

The benchmark explicitly connects to Pearl’s causal ladder. The source states that “We organize CausalPhys around the levels of causal understanding defined by Pearl’s causal ladder [43]: association, intervention, and counterfactuals” [PAGE 4]. The four categories are then assigned to these levels: Perception corresponds to association, Anticipation and Intervention probe intervention, and Goal Orientation aligns with counterfactual reasoning [PAGE 4]. This genealogy matters because earlier visual benchmarks often evaluated prediction accuracy without separating observational correlation from manipulation-based inference. The paper contrasts CausalPhys with prior work: “Classical benchmarks such as CLEVRER and CoPhy [10, 57] target elementary visual primitives of spheres, cubes, and rigid-body collisions” [PAGE 3]. Those benchmarks established the importance of physical prediction, but CausalPhys extends the scope toward real-world scenes, typed causal annotations, and mechanism-level evaluation.

A second lineage is the evolution from answer-based physical benchmarks to rationale-based diagnostics. The paper says of PhysBench and MVPBench: “Both focus on physics-centric understanding through answer-based evaluation, emphasizing prediction accuracy rather than the underlying reasoning process” [PAGE 3]. CausalPhys responds by adding “expert-annotated causal graphs that capture object–attribute–event dependencies” [PAGE 3]. This is a methodological inversion: instead of asking only whether the final label is correct, the benchmark asks whether the intermediate explanation is structurally faithful.

A third lineage is causal reasoning in language models. The paper notes that “While causal reasoning has been extensively studied for LLMs [29, 30, 37, 46], equivalent efforts in the VLMs remain comparatively nascent” [PAGE 3]. This explains why CRFT borrows from rational-based supervision but adapts it to multimodal physical scenes. The source states that CRFT “enforces alignment between a model’s latent reasoning and causal graphs” [PAGE 2]. The historical trajectory is therefore: symbolic causal graphs, causal question answering in language, visual physical benchmarks, and finally causal-graph-grounded VLM evaluation plus fine-tuning.

The multi-week traps in this lineage are numerous. The first trap is observational confounding: a model may learn that certain objects co-occur with certain answers without learning the physical mechanism. The paper identifies this when it says “Apparent successes in tasks like trajectory prediction are often artifacts of spurious pattern recognition (e.g., object presence) rather than genuine causal synthesis” [PAGE 5]. The second trap is annotation drift: expert annotators must encode physical dependencies into typed graphs over many instances. The source describes a calibration phase and continuous monitoring: “We continuously monitored inter-annotator agreement across graph annotations, with all conflicts adjudicated by senior annotators” [PAGE 4]. The third trap is judge drift: if the LLM judge changes its interpretation of causal language, metric values may shift. The paper counters this with cross-model and paraphrase checks: “Cross-LLM Consistency (𝜅) … 0.84” and “Paraphrase Stability (𝜅) … 0.90” [PAGE 13]. The fourth trap is training contamination: if the model memorizes benchmark-specific scenes rather than causal structure, transfer fails. The paper tests this with PhysBench transfer, reporting “CRFT improves Qwen2-VL-7B by +4.0% overall (58.3→62.3)” [PAGE 8]. The fifth trap is teacher imitation: if CRFT merely imitates a teacher LLM’s style, it may fail when the teacher changes. The paper’s teacher-swap analysis reports “comparable CRFT performance” with Claude Sonnet 4, arguing that CRFT learns “teacher-invariant causal structure” [PAGE 13].

Thus, the retro-causal genealogy is not merely historical. It identifies the exact mechanisms by which benchmark designers, annotators, judges, and models can accidentally reproduce shallow correlations while believing they are measuring causal understanding.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory of mind in CausalPhys can be modeled as a loop among scene interpretation, answer commitment, external verification, and policy correction. The VLM first observes a visual state \(X\) and query \(Q\). It then generates a rationale \(R\) and answer \(Y\). The benchmark’s judge then evaluates whether \(R\) contains the entities, relations, and descriptions required by the causal graph \(G\). The training procedure then uses graph-anchored rationales to adjust the model’s future reasoning. This loop is recursive because the output of evaluation becomes the supervision signal for the next model version.

The crisis habit regression is the tendency of a model to abandon explicit causal reasoning when uncertainty rises. The paper names this behavior directly: answer-only SFT encourages the model to behave like a “guesser,” “prioritizing concluding final answers based on shallow experience rather than over structured, interpretable reasoning chains” [PAGE 8]. In crisis conditions, such as ambiguous collision frames, occlusion, mirror reflections, or rapid temporal change, the model may default to a perceptual shortcut. For example, in collision prediction, the model may see two vehicles and infer “Yes” from visual proximity without tracking relative velocity, braking capacity, or evasive trajectory. The case study shows that one model states “a collision appears highly likely” [PAGE 18], while another model states “no imminent collision appears likely at this moment” due to following distance and wet-road caution [PAGE 18]. The ground truth in that case requires a specific causal interpretation of the frames. The habit regression is to select the most visually salient answer, not the most causally supported one.

The counter-trap is the graph-anchored rationale. CRFT forces the model to generate a causal chain before the answer. The training data are generated from a teacher LLM that must “explicitly reference nodes and edges in G,” “trace intermediate causal implications,” and “conclude with \(Y^*\)” [PAGE 6]. The rationale generation prompt requires that the model “Follow the correct logical order: causes must appear before their effects” [PAGE 16]. This is a counter-trap because it blocks premature answer commitment. The model cannot simply output a label; it must instantiate a causal narrative. The evaluation metrics then punish rationales that omit nodes or edges. Entity Faithfulness checks whether the relevant objects and attributes are present, while Relation Awareness checks whether the directed dependencies are present. Description Correctness checks whether the physical details are consistent with the annotation. Together, these metrics create a recursive incentive: to improve measured reasoning, the model must expose its causal structure to verification.

The clock layer is temporal. Physical reasoning is not static; it depends on ordering, delay, and horizon. The benchmark uses short temporal windows: “Video instances are curated as short clips capped at 5 seconds” [PAGE 11], and image sequences may contain “up to 8 evenly spaced frames” [PAGE 11]. These constraints define the observation clock. The model must infer state transitions within that clock. In Anticipation, the model must predict a future state after the observed interval. In Intervention, the model must evaluate a hypothetical modification at a future or counterfactual time. In Goal Orientation, the model must select an action that will achieve a future goal. The Temporal Shifting examples require reasoning about scene state after a delay, such as “which of the following scenes is not possible after 5 hours?” [PAGE 23]. Here the clock is environmental: daylight, dusk, and artificial illumination have different temporal constraints. The recursive loop becomes: observe frames within the clock, generate a causal rationale under temporal constraints, answer, verify against graph, and update.

The final recursive interpretation is that CausalPhys treats the model as an agent whose hidden reasoning must be made legible. The benchmark does not merely ask what the model knows; it asks whether the model can externalize the causal path from observation to answer. The training objective then closes the loop by rewarding rationales that instantiate the graph. The result is a shift from answer-only behavior to explanation-grounded behavior.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto proof can be formulated by defining an answer \(a\) as admissible only if it survives physical, biomechanical, and environmental constraints. Let \(X\) be the visual scene, \(G\) the causal graph, and \(a\) a candidate answer. Define three constraint predicates: \(C_{\mathrm{phys}}(a,X)\), \(C_{\mathrm{bio}}(a,X)\), and \(C_{\mathrm{env}}(a,X)\). The admissible set is:
\[
F = \{a : C_{\mathrm{phys}}(a,X)=1 \land C_{\mathrm{bio}}(a,X)=1 \land C_{\mathrm{env}}(a,X)=1\}.
\]
If \(a \notin F\), then \(a\) is vetoed. The veto is layer-specific but cumulative: a physically impossible answer is rejected even if it is linguistically plausible, and a biomechanically impossible action is rejected even if it is visually salient.

The physical layer uses conservation, contact, gravity, geometry, and kinematics. Units are SI: distance in meters, time in seconds, velocity in meters per second, angle in radians, force in newtons, and mass in kilograms. In the Tool Assignment case, the question is “Which tool should be use to disassemble the toy?” [PAGE 20]. The relevant physical constraint is fastener geometry. If the screw head is cross-shaped, a Phillips screwdriver is the tool that can engage the recess and transmit torque. Wrenches apply torque to external flats or hexagonal profiles, not cross-shaped recesses. Therefore, a wrench answer is vetoed by the physical constraint of tool-head engagement. The paper’s case study shows multiple models selecting a Phillips screwdriver, but one ground truth in the figure is listed as “B” [PAGE 20], illustrating that even near-correct physical reasoning can fail if the causal graph or visual evidence is not correctly aligned.

The biomechanical layer concerns reach, grasp, posture, and body motion. Units include joint angle in radians, reach distance in meters, and time to contact in seconds. In Object Relocation, the question is “To take out the pot, how many levels should the hand move?” [PAGE 20]. The admissible answer depends on the hand’s current shelf level and the pot’s shelf level. If the hand is below the pot, the hand must move upward; if above, downward. A candidate answer that requires moving through a solid shelf or exceeding arm reach is vetoed. The graph must encode the relative position attribute and the motion event. Without those relations, the model may choose a visually plausible but biomechanically invalid path.

The environmental layer includes weather, illumination, friction, visibility, and temporal feasibility. In the collision case, one response states that “The vehicle is traveling in rainy conditions with reduced visibility” and that “wet pavement reduces traction and increases stopping distances” [PAGE 18]. These are environmental constraints. They affect braking distance, tire-road friction, and driver reaction time. A collision prediction that ignores reduced friction may be physically overconfident; a no-collision prediction that ignores closing distance may be physically overpermissive. The correct veto depends on the measured or inferred trajectories in the frames.

The viewpoint and optical layers impose coordinate transformations. In Viewpoint Transformation, the question is “Where is the TV located respective to the camera in the second image?” [PAGE 22]. The correct answer requires tracking the camera’s orientation change. If the camera rotates, front, back, left, and right are not invariant labels; they must be transformed into the new camera frame. A model that says “Back” because the TV was behind the original camera may be vetoed if the new camera orientation makes the TV left or right. In Optical Inference, the question is “Where are the computer monitors located related to the camera?” [PAGE 24]. The mirror introduces a reflection transformation. The model must distinguish apparent mirror position from true room position. If the mirror reverses left and right, an answer based only on the reflected image is vetoed.

Temporal feasibility is another environmental veto. In Temporal Shifting, the question is “Assuming Photo N was taken right now, which of the following scenes is not possible after 5 hours?” [PAGE 23]. If Photo N is dusk and the candidate photo is full daylight, the transition may require more than 5 hours because a full night and sunrise cycle is longer than the stated interval. The veto is based on time \(t\) measured in seconds or hours and the astronomical or illumination state. A model that ignores the clock may select a scene that is visually similar but temporally impossible.

The proof of adjudication is therefore hierarchical. First, verify whether the answer satisfies the physical constraints encoded by the graph. Second, verify whether the agent or object motion satisfies biomechanical constraints. Third, verify whether the scene environment permits the predicted transition. If any layer returns false, the answer is vetoed. This explains why high Entity Faithfulness is insufficient: the model may mention the correct objects but fail the constraint proof. It also explains why Relation Awareness is central: the graph supplies the dependencies needed to evaluate the veto.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable reinforcement-learning-with-verifier counterfactual veto scenarios can be constructed from the benchmark. In each scenario, the verifier checks the rationale against the causal graph and physical constraints, then vetoes outputs that are causally unsupported. The predictions are expressed in terms of Accuracy, Entity Faithfulness, Relation Awareness, and Description Correctness.

Scenario 1: Collision forecasting under closing trajectory. Input: sequential dashcam frames showing two vehicles whose gap decreases. Candidate model output: “No collision.” Verifier check: the graph should contain a directed relation from decreasing inter-vehicle distance to collision risk, and possibly from limited evasive space to collision imminence. If the rationale says only that both cars are visible, it satisfies Entity Faithfulness but fails Relation Awareness. The judging prompt requires that for causal relation questions one must “require a clear causal/influence expression” and that “Mere co-occurrence is insufficient” [PAGE 17]. Verifier veto: reject “No collision” if the rationale lacks an edge from closing distance to collision outcome and if the visual evidence shows a shrinking gap. Prediction: the uncorrected model may have ACC near 0 on that item, EF moderate because cars are mentioned, RA low because the causal edge is absent, and DC low if relative speed or gap is not described. After CRFT, the rationale should mention the closing gap, limited space, and likely contact, causing RA and ACC to rise. This matches the paper’s observation that collision forecasting is an anticipation task requiring explicit evidence cues [PAGE 11].

Scenario 2: Fluid flow under misalignment. Input: a tilted container above a cup or table. Candidate model output: “Into the other container.” Verifier check: the graph should encode the container spout position, the receiving container position, gravity, and the path of the liquid. If the spout is not over the cup, the physical constraint vetoes “into the other container.” The case study shows models selecting “into the other container” while the listed ground truth in one example is “C” [PAGE 18], demonstrating that visually plausible pouring motion is not sufficient. Verifier veto: reject the answer if the rationale does not state that the spout is aligned with the receiving opening or if the graph shows the liquid path intersecting the table rather than the cup. Prediction: low RA and low DC for the uncorrected model because the direction of flow and the support boundary are not causally described. After CRFT, the rationale should state that the container is not sufficiently above the cup, that gravity pulls the liquid downward, and that the table is the impact surface. ACC should increase when the graph’s edge from spout position to liquid destination is explicitly used. This aligns with the paper’s fluid-flow example: “Where will the liquid from the container expected to be poured?” [PAGE 18].

Scenario 3: Mirror-based optical inference. Input: a mirror reflecting a room, with monitors visible in reflection. Candidate model output: “Front right.” Verifier check: the graph must include the mirror surface, the camera location, the reflected object location, and the transformation from apparent reflected coordinates to true room coordinates. The judging prompt requires explicit causal or influence language; a mere mention of the mirror is insufficient if the rationale does not state that reflection reverses or transforms the apparent position. Verifier veto: reject “Front right” if the rationale fails to apply the mirror transformation or if the graph indicates that the true location is behind the camera and to one side. The paper’s Optical Inference case shows models choosing “Back right” while the ground truth is listed as “B” [PAGE 24], illustrating that reflection reasoning is a common failure point. Prediction: uncorrected model may have high EF because monitors, mirror, and camera are mentioned, but low RA because the transformation edge is absent. DC may also be low if the rationale does not describe the reversal. After CRFT, the rationale should state that the mirror reflects objects behind the camera and that the apparent side must be transformed into the camera’s true frame. RA and ACC should improve when the transformation edge is expressed.

These scenarios are verifiable because each depends on observable frame evidence and explicit graph elements. They are counterfactual because the verifier asks what would happen if the missing edge were present: if the rationale included the closing-distance-to-collision edge, the collision answer would become supported; if the rationale included the spout-misalignment-to-table edge, the fluid answer would change; if the rationale included the mirror-transformation edge, the optical answer would change. The verifier therefore does not merely reward final labels. It rewards the presence of the causal operators required to make the labels physically valid.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
