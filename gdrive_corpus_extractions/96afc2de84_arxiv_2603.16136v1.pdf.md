Let \(u\) index a player, \(g\in\{L,T\}\) index League and TFT, and \(C_g\) be the set of functional categories in game \(g\). The paper’s core flexibility operator is defined from end-game category counts. For each category \(i\in C_g\),

\]
where \(N_{u,g,i}\) is the number of games in category \(i\), \(N_{u,g}\) is total games, and \(p_{u,g,i}\) is dimensionless. The flexibility score is
\[
F_{u,g}=1-\frac{1}{|C_g|}\sum_{i\in C_g}\left|p_{u,g,\max}-p_{u,g,i}\right|.
\]
For League, \(|C_L|=21\) order-agnostic role combinations; for TFT, \(|C_T|=N_{\mathrm{comps}}\), with
\[
N_{\mathrm{comps}}=N_{\mathrm{origins}}+N_{\mathrm{classes}}+\left(N_{\mathrm{origins}}\times N_{\mathrm{classes}}\right)+1.
\]
The paper states: “We define a champion’s functional play style as this order-agnostic combination of play style roles” [PAGE 10], and for TFT it calculates “the total number of possible pairs of origins and classes for each set” [PAGE 11]. Units: \(N\) is a count; \(p\) and \(F\) are dimensionless proportions. The score is bounded as \(F_{u,g}\in[1/|C_g|,1]\) under nondegenerate counts. If \(N_{u,g}=0\), \(p\) and \(F\) are undefined; the paper’s filter of at least 50 Solo Queue games in each title avoids this singularity for included users [PAGE 10]. The paper’s lineage is a chain of retrocausal interpretation: present cross-game consistency is read backward as evidence of prior stable dispositions. The authors move from Bartle’s early player typology [PAGE 4] to modern role and metagame research, then to Gibson’s affordance theory [PAGE 2], Self-Determination Theory’s competence motive [PAGE 6], personality stability research [PAGE 6], and Giddens’ Structuration Theory [PAGE 2]. The central retrocausal move is that a player’s current League or TFT choice is treated as the visible trace of a prior behavioral signature. The paper says Mischel and Shoda’s CAPS theory “formalizes this through ‘behavioral signatures,’ where if-then patterns reflect underlying dispositional tendencies that remain stable” [PAGE 7]. It also says “Giddens also argues that individuals are knowledgeable agents who reflexively monitor their actions and develop routines through repetition [21]” [PAGE 7]. That is the historical warrant for reading cross-game correlation as agency and within-game sign patterns as structure.

The multi-week traps are where this genealogy becomes empirically fragile. First, the patch-lag trap: League balance patches occur every two weeks [PAGE 5], and TFT also receives balance patches every two weeks while sets refresh every few months [PAGE 5]. A player’s average flexibility over many weeks mixes pre-patch and post-patch adaptation. If elite players adapt within one patch cycle but the model averages over a year, the estimated adaptation coefficient is attenuated. Second, the set-reset trap: TFT composition categories are set-specific, so \(F_{u,T}\) is not a fixed category-space measure across sets; the paper averages per-set scores but does not prove measurement invariance. Third, the rank-decay trap: elite players faced “rank decay penalties for inactivity and stricter matchmaking” during the dataset’s timeframe [PAGE 9], so elite behavior is partly a response to rank maintenance, not only to strategic incentive. Fourth, the ecosystem trap: both games are Riot titles, so cross-game consistency may reflect shared assets, shared metagame knowledge, and shared account habit rather than a general personality trait. The paper acknowledges the shared-ecosystem limit and says “this does limit external validity” [PAGE 23]. Fifth, the meta-learning trap: the paper cites evidence that “there is a predominant meta strategy in League of Legends that is correlated with higher competitive success” but also “select off-meta strategies that yield higher win rates” [PAGE 4]. A player who deliberately counter-picks the meta can look “flexible” for reasons that are strategic and short-lived, not dispositional. The retrocausal reading therefore needs a clock: per-game adaptation, per-session adaptation, per-patch adaptation, per-set adaptation, and per-season disposition are different objects. The paper’s strongest historical contribution is not proving agency over structure; it is showing that a large individual-level cross-game panel can generate stable descriptive correlations even when the structural contrast is confounded by genre, patch cycle, and selection.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive model is a player who models self, opponent, team, and meta at multiple clocks. Let \(D_u\) be baseline disposition, \(M_t\) the player’s model of the current meta, \(C_t\) the competitive crisis level, and \(\tau\) the evaluation clock. A compact policy representation is
\[
\pi_u(a_t\mid s_t)=\sigma\left(\alpha D_u+\beta M_t+\gamma C_t+\delta \frac{\partial \hat{W}_{t+1}}{\partial a_t}+\eta(\tau)\right),
\]
where \(a_t\) is champion or composition choice, \(s_t\) is state, \(\hat{W}_{t+1}\) is expected win probability, and \(\sigma\) is a softmax or logistic map. This is not the paper’s estimated model; it is an audit device for the three mechanisms the paper implies. “Crisis habit regression” means that as \(C_t\) rises, the weight on \(D_u\) rises and the player falls back to routinized picks. The paper’s Structuration language supports this: routines operate through “practical consciousness” and “persist across contexts because they are deeply ingrained through prior experience” [PAGE 7], while reflexive monitoring is “ongoing self-awareness of one’s own actions and the ability to adjust one’s behavior as needed” [PAGE 8]. Under crisis, reflexive monitoring is costly, so habit regresses. Prediction: after losses, rank decay threats, or tournament pressure, repeat-category probability should increase, and flexibility should fall even when the structural incentive favors breadth.

“Counter-trap” means that a player over-models the opponent or meta and deliberately deviates to counter it. In League, off-meta picks can exploit draft expectations; in TFT, forcing a contested composition or over-pivoting can counter expected lobby behavior. The paper’s own cite says off-meta strategies can yield higher win rates [PAGE 4], but the same behavior can be a trap if it sacrifices mastery. Prediction: high flexibility generated by counter-meta deviation should interact negatively with win rate in League when mechanical mastery is low, but may be positive in TFT when pivot speed is high. The paper’s own-game coefficients are consistent with this asymmetry: League flexibility is negative for League win rate, TFT flexibility is positive for TFT win rate [PAGE 15]. But the cross-game TFT-flexibility coefficient in the League model is also negative, which suggests that counter-trap-like breadth may not transfer cleanly.

“Clock” means the timescale at which the player evaluates the tradeoff. At the per-game clock, the player may pivot; at the session clock, fatigue and streak effects enter; at the patch clock, the meta moves; at the set clock, the category space changes; at the season clock, rank and identity dominate. The paper’s SHAP ambiguity is exactly a clock problem: “players who play a higher percentage of competitive games tend to exhibit greater flexibility” while “competitive success corresponds with decreased flexibility (specialization)” [PAGE 17], and in TFT “increased engagement leads to greater TFT flexibility” but “increased temporal engagement in TFT decreases flexibility” [PAGE 20]. The recursive theory-of-mind reading is that players are not one policy; they have a fast counter-trap policy, a slow habit policy, and a meta-reflexive policy. Elite players are predicted to shift weight toward the meta-reflexive policy more than non-elite players, but the paper shows retention of baseline disposition: “structure exerts pressure on agency, and agency adapts somewhat, but competitive incentives (structure) are not enough” [PAGE 21]. The counter-trap audit should therefore distinguish adaptive flexibility from compulsive deviation. A player who switches because the board demands it is flexible; a player who switches because they believe the opponent expects the default is counter-trap; a player who repeats under stress is habit regression. The paper’s end-game snapshots cannot fully separate these, which is why the theory-of-mind interpretation must remain a hypothesis, not a proven mechanism.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical, biomechanical, and weather veto is straightforward: the source provides no physical, biomechanical, or weather variables with which to adjudicate those layers. The observed data are Riot API end states, ranks, win rates, queue choices, and engagement aggregates. The paper states that “Riot’s API only returned the end state of each League or TFT game” [PAGE 10]. It also states that the authors were “unable to survey users for their personalities or motivations” [PAGE 23]. There are no mouse/keyboard telemetry, reaction-time measures, heart-rate measures, sleep measures, device latency, local weather, or geolocation data. The dataset is also North American only: “Our dataset only contains players from the North American servers due to API rate limits” [PAGE 24]. That does not provide weather variation, and even if it did, no weather variable enters any model. Therefore any claim that flexibility is caused by biomechanical fatigue, physical load, device ergonomics, or weather is vetoed as unsupported by the source. Session length and daily streak are behavioral engagement proxies, not biomechanical measures. The paper notes that “performance may decline after a certain threshold of consecutive games in a session” [PAGE 12], but that is a cited hypothesis, not a measured biomechanical mechanism. The correct cross-layer verdict is: the study is a telemetric behavioral-layer study; physical and weather explanations are outside its evidence set. A formal veto predicate is \(\mathcal{V}_{\text{phys}}=\mathbf{1}\{X_{\text{phys}}\subseteq X_{\text{obs}}\ \land\ \exists\text{ identification strategy}\}\). Here \(X_{\text{phys}}\nsubseteq X_{\text{obs}}\), so \(\mathcal{V}_{\text{phys}}=0\). This is a veto on inference, not proof that physical or weather effects are zero.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
In RLVR terms, use verifiable rewards: League win \(R_L=1\{\text{win}\}\), TFT top-four \(R_T=1\{\text{top four}\}\), plus rank change and flexibility score as measured outcomes. The three counterfactual veto scenarios below are designed to falsify H1, H2, or H3 with observable predictions. They do not require betting lines; they require patch/set interventions, held-out prediction, and within-player session analysis.

Scenario 1: Incentive-flip patch/set. Intervention: introduce a patch or TFT set that reverses the measured optimal flexibility in one title—e.g., a League patch that rewards role breadth, or a TFT set that rewards forcing a narrow composition. Prediction under H1 and H3: elite players’ within-player \(\Delta F_g\) should move toward the new optimum more than non-elite players’ \(\Delta F_g\), and the sign of the own-game flexibility coefficient should flip. Prediction under H2: the cross-game rank-order correlation \(\rho(F_L,F_T\mid W,E,S)\) should remain positive after the patch. Veto: if elite \(\Delta F_g\) is indistinguishable from zero, or if the own-game coefficient does not flip, H1/H3 lose their causal reading. If \(\rho\) collapses to zero after controlling for win rate, H2 is vetoed. This is verifiable with pre/post match histories and rank-stratified differences.

Scenario 2: Cross-game holdout transfer. Intervention: train a model on early-window flexibility in game A and predict late-window flexibility in game B, controlling for win rate, engagement, and seed skill. Prediction under H2: \(F_{u,A}\) should add out-of-sample predictive power beyond \(W_{u,A},W_{u,B},E_u,S_u\); the incremental \(R^2\) should be positive, and the cross-game feature should remain in the top SHAP tier. Veto: if incremental \(R^2\approx 0\) and the cross-game feature falls out of the top tier, the dispositional-persistence claim is weakened to a within-game engagement artifact. The paper already reports model-relative evidence in this direction: TFT flexibility is the second most important predictor of League flexibility [PAGE 18], and League flexibility is the fourth most important predictor of TFT flexibility [PAGE 20]. A held-out test would make that verifiable rather than SHAP-relative.

Scenario 3: Session-clock and habit-regression disambiguation. Intervention: use within-player consecutive-game index, forced breaks, and daily session length as clock variables. Prediction under “crisis habit regression”: as consecutive games in a session rise, repeat-category probability should rise and \(F_g\) should fall, especially after losses. Prediction under “counter-trap”: high flexibility driven by opponent modeling should increase variance and should interact negatively with win rate in League but possibly positively in TFT. Prediction under “engagement breadth”: total daily games should increase flexibility, while temporal engagement should decrease it, matching the paper’s ambiguous TFT statement that “increased engagement leads to greater TFT flexibility” but “increased temporal engagement in TFT decreases flexibility” [PAGE 20]. Veto: if neither consecutive-game index nor total daily games predicts within-player flexibility after player fixed effects, then the SHAP engagement signal is a proxy for unobserved skill or meta knowledge, not a clock mechanism. A second veto: if forced breaks do not change flexibility but do change win rate, then the mechanism is performance-only, not flexibility-only. These three scenarios give clear falsification conditions: sign flips, incremental predictive power, and within-player temporal slopes.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
