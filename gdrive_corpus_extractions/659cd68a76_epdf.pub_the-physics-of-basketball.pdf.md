<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
1. Newton’s second law operator:
\[
\sum_i \mathbf F_i=m\mathbf a.
\]
The source’s verbal form is that “the total (net) force on an object equals the product of the mass times the acceleration of the object” [PAGE 21]. Units: \(\mathbf F\) in N, \(m\) in kg, \(\mathbf a\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). Breakdown: the one-body constant-mass form fails for variable-mass systems such as rockets, as the source notes [PAGE 22].

2. Gravitational force operator:
\[
F_{\mathrm{gravity}}=G\frac{m_{\mathrm{basketball}}m_{\mathrm{Earth}}}{R_{\mathrm{Earth}}^2}
=m_{\mathrm{basketball}}g,
\]
with \(g\approx 9.8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) near Earth’s surface [PAGE 24–25]. Units: N. With buoyancy included, the effective downward acceleration becomes about \(9.66\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) for a basketball [PAGE 27]. Breakdown: \(g\) is local; the source’s Moon example changes weight, not mass [PAGE 25].

3. Buoyant force operator:
\[
F_{\mathrm{buoy}}=\rho_{\mathrm{air}}V_{\mathrm{basketball}}g.
\]
The source states that “the buoyant force is about 1.5% of the weight” [PAGE 27]. Units: N. Breakdown: buoyancy is small but non-negligible for a large, light basketball; if density is not modeled, the effective acceleration is slightly wrong.

4. Drag force operator:
\[
F_{\mathrm{drag}}=C_{\mathrm{drag}}\rho_{\mathrm{air}}Av^2.
\]
The text says that at game speeds “the air drag force on a basketball varies as the square of the speed, υ” [PAGE 28]. Working forms are \(F_{\mathrm{drag}}=0.0136v^2\) for a men’s ball and \(F_{\mathrm{drag}}=0.0127v^2\) for a women’s ball, with \(v\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and \(F\) in N [PAGE 150]. Terminal speed is about \(21\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) or \(47\ \mathrm{mph}\) [PAGE 30]. Breakdown: the working coefficients assume \(\rho_{\mathrm{air}}=1.22\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\) and a constant drag coefficient; the source explicitly justifies the equation for speeds less than about 10 m/s and treats the ball as a sphere [PAGE 150, PAGE 28].

5. Magnus force operator:
\[
F_{\mathrm{Magnus}}=C_{\mathrm{Magnus}}\rho_{\mathrm{air}}D^3\omega v.
\]
The source says the force is greater for faster linear speed and faster spin [PAGE 32]. Working equations are \(F_{\mathrm{Magnus}}=0.0180\omega v\) for men and \(F_{\mathrm{Magnus}}=0.0162\omega v\) for women, where \(\omega\) is in revolutions per second, \(v\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and \(F\) in N [PAGE 150–151]. Breakdown: if \(v=0\) or \(\omega=0\), the Magnus force is zero; topspin reverses the sign relative to backspin [PAGE 151]. The source describes the law as approximate for basketball and soccer under normal conditions [PAGE 32].

6. Coupled trajectory ODEs:
\[
m\frac{dv_x}{dt}
=-C_{\mathrm{drag}}\rho Avv_x-C_{\mathrm{Magnus}}\rho D^3\omega v_y,
\]
\[
m\frac{dv_y}{dt}
=F_{\mathrm{gravity}}+F_{\mathrm{buoyant}}
-C_{\mathrm{drag}}\rho Avv_y
+C_{\mathrm{Magnus}}\rho D^3\omega v_x.
\]
These follow the source’s Appendix III form [PAGE 151]. Units: every term is N, with \(v=\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}\). Breakdown: sign conventions must be consistent; “all equations are written with ω positive for a basketball with backspin” and “for a basketball with topspin ω is negative” [PAGE 151].

7. Nothing-but-net window condition:
\[
W(\theta_{\mathrm{approach}})\ge D_b
\]
is necessary for a straight-through shot under the stated release geometry. If \(W&lt;D_b\), the ball is too large for the window. At equality \(W=D_b\), the trajectory is tangent to the rim and the feasibility set has a boundary singularity. For a 4.2 m shot and 2.4 m release height, gravity-only gives cutoffs of \(29.0^\circ\) for men and \(27.9^\circ\) for women [PAGE 44]; the four-force model gives \(28.8^\circ\) and \(27.8^\circ\) [PAGE 54]. The source also gives an example at \(40^\circ\): men’s window about 0.340 m and women’s about 1% larger, with only about 0.10–0.11 m of vertical clearance over the ball [PAGE 54].

8. Hoopothesis operator:
\[
\theta^\star_{\mathrm{approach\ speed}}\approx 50.8^\circ,\qquad
\theta^\star_{\mathrm{launch\ speed}}\approx 48.2^\circ
\]
for the author’s foul-shot geometry. The source reports measured launch angles near \(51^\circ\) and proposes that a good shooter “minimize the approach speed” [PAGE 56–57]. For a taller shooter the minimum-approach-speed angle is about \(48.7^\circ\), with angle of approach about \(42.8^\circ\) and window about 0.365 m [PAGE 58–59]. Breakdown: these are geometry-specific optima; changing release height, distance, ball size, air density, or spin moves the optimum.

9. Rim-bounce operator:
Let \(\theta_{\mathrm{PC}}\) be the angle to the point of contact on the rim. The source restricts the first analysis to the top half of the rim, \(0^\circ\) to \(180^\circ\), and initially assumes a perfect, frictionless bounce [PAGE 70–71]. The key qualitative rule is: “if a basketball hits the front of the rim at the back of the hoop, it always goes in” [PAGE 89]. Allowing a back-rim bounce lowers the minimum approach angle to \(19.0^\circ\) for men and \(18.0^\circ\) for women in the stated foul-shot geometry, much lower than the \(28.8^\circ\) and \(27.8^\circ\) nothing-but-net limits [PAGE 89]. Breakdown: adding friction and spin changes the bounce categories; the source treats static and kinetic friction separately and notes that subsequent bounces are complicated [PAGE 79–84].

10. Layup/backboard operator:
For a layup rebounding off the backboard, the source reports horizontal rebound speeds “between 0.8 m/s and 1.3 m/s with an average of about 1.0 m/s” [PAGE 105]. If the ball leaves the backboard horizontally at 1.0 m/s, the allowed contact-height window is “between 0.14 m (5.5 in) and 0.59 m (23 in) above the rim” [PAGE 107]. The allowed angle range is very wide: from about \(57.3^\circ\) below the horizontal to about \(33.9^\circ\) above the horizontal in the source’s sign convention [PAGE 106]. Breakdown: these windows assume the stated horizontal rebound speed and a standard backboard geometry; changing speed or contact point moves the window.

11. Impulse operator:
\[
\int_{t_i}^{t_f}\mathbf F\,dt=m\mathbf v_f-m\mathbf v_i.
\]
The source defines impulse as the area under the force-time curve and checks it against momentum change [PAGE 101]. Units: N s. Example: about 5 N s upward impulse and about 4.9 N s from \(m\Delta v\) for a dropped basketball [PAGE 100–102]. Breakdown: the check requires correct contact time and velocity measurement; gravity impulse during contact is small but subtracted in the source’s example [PAGE 101].

12. Pressure force operator:
\[
F_{\mathrm{pressure}}=PA.
\]
The source uses about 160 kPa absolute pressure and an imprint area of \(9.2\times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^2}\) to calculate about 1,470 N on the flat bottom, larger than the 650 N maximum normal force [PAGE 105]. Breakdown: \(A\) must be small enough that pressure is uniform over the area; deformation and pressure distribution complicate the estimate [PAGE 104–105].

13. Bounce spring model:
\[
F_{\mathrm{spring}}=-kx,\qquad
T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}.
\]
The equivalent spring constant for a properly inflated basketball is about \(50\,000\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\), predicting contact time about 0.011 s versus the experimental 0.016 s [PAGE 107–108]. Breakdown: the model omits internal friction and damping; that is why it underestimates contact time and cannot explain the loss of bounce height.

14. Static and kinetic friction operators:
\[
F_{\mathrm{static}}\le \mu_{\mathrm{static}}N,\qquad
F_{\mathrm{kinetic}}=\mu_{\mathrm{kinetic}}N,
\]
with usually \(\mu_{\mathrm{kinetic}}&lt;\mu_{\mathrm{static}}\) [PAGE 114–115]. Breakdown: the static inequality has a hard slipping threshold; exceeding it produces kinetic friction and a smaller acceleration. The source’s shoe-design example shows that maximizing \(\mu_{\mathrm{static}}\) is not optimal because a foot can stick too strongly and injure the knee [PAGE 115–116].

15. Hang-time kinematics:
\[
v_y^2-v_{0y}^2=2a_yy,\qquad
v_y-v_{0y}=a_yt,\qquad
t_{\mathrm{top}}=\sqrt{\frac{2b}{g}},
\]
and the source’s result is that a player spends only about \(29\%\) of jump time in the bottom half and \(71\%\) in the upper half [PAGE 122–123]. Breakdown: the derivation assumes only gravity acts and ignores air forces and configuration changes; body/ball center-of-mass shifts can change the body trajectory during a dunk [PAGE 123].

16. Coefficient of restitution:
\[
e=-\frac{v_{\mathrm{floor,after}}-v_{\mathrm{ball,after}}}
{v_{\mathrm{floor,before}}-v_{\mathrm{ball,before}}}
\approx-\frac{v_{\mathrm{ball,after}}}{v_{\mathrm{ball,before}}}.
\]
The height-based form \(e=\sqrt{h_{\mathrm{after}}/h_{\mathrm{before}}}\) is invalid for precise work because of air resistance; the source says a perfect basketball dropped from 1.8 m “behaves as though it has a coefficient of restitution of about 0.96 rather than the value of 1” [PAGE 139]. Breakdown: the height formula conflates drag loss with collision loss.

17. Tempered-glass fracture operator:
The source states that cracks “can accelerate to speeds up to about one third the speed of sound in the material” and that for typical glass this is about \(1,500\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), so a crack can cross a backboard in about 0.001 s [PAGE 149]. Breakdown: the triggering condition is edge or interior tensile stress perturbation, not simply a large applied force; improper installation can leave an edge vulnerable [PAGE 129–131].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The source’s scientific lineage is explicit in its notes. Newton’s 1687 Principia is cited for the drag-force form [PAGE 141], Robins demonstrated a transverse aerodynamic force on a rotating sphere in 1742, and Magnus published on rotating cylinders in 1853; the modern name “Magnus effect” is retroactively applied to any moving spinning object [PAGE 141]. Adair’s The Physics of Baseball is used for the drag asymmetry on opposite sides of a spinning ball [PAGE 13, PAGE 142]. Brancazio’s 1981 “Physics of Basketball” supplies the minimum-launch-speed and angle-of-approach discussion [PAGE 142]. Hay’s biomechanics text supplies straight-line target geometry [PAGE 142]. Ireson’s “Beckham as Physicist?” supplies the basketball/soccer Magnus approximation [PAGE 142]. Cross’s grip-slip paper complicates the simple friction bounce model [PAGE 144]. Linthorne and Cross supply force-plate jump analysis [PAGE 144]. This genealogy matters because later corrections are not arbitrary: they are layered onto earlier approximations.

The first multi-week trap is the gravity-only parabola. It is visually plausible and often “close enough” to a made shot, but the source’s digitized foul shot shows that gravity-only theory predicts an overshoot and, at best, a hit on the back of the hoop [PAGE 30]. A learner can spend weeks tuning launch speed to fit a gravity-only model and thereby absorb a systematic error.

The second trap is treating drag as the complete correction. When drag is added, the theoretical path becomes lower than the actual path and “air resistance overcorrects” [PAGE 31]. The correct inference is not “drag is wrong” but “the residual has another vertical-lift term,” namely Magnus.

The third trap is dismissing Magnus for basketball because basketballs are not famous for curving. The source estimates that at the same linear and rotational speeds the Magnus force on a basketball is about 38 times stronger than on a baseball, but baseballs usually have much higher linear and rotational speeds [PAGE 34]. The long sidearm pass is the source’s counterexample: the ball curves because both linear and rotational speeds are large [PAGE 34–35]. The multi-week trap is overgeneralizing from ordinary jump-shot curvature to all basketball trajectories.

The fourth trap is using bounce height to calculate coefficient of restitution. The source states that a perfect basketball dropped from 1.8 m behaves as if \(e\approx0.96\) because air resistance removes height [PAGE 139]. A student can spend weeks “correcting” a ball’s material elasticity when the actual loss is aerodynamic.

The fifth trap is concluding that tall players are physically barred from good foul shooting. The source does find a slightly smaller window for a 2.75 m release than for a 2.46 m release, with a 5% smaller vertical margin at 40° angle of approach [PAGE 58–59]. But the tall shooter’s optimum shifts to a lower launch angle and larger angle of approach, and Dirk Nowitzki is cited at 85.6% career foul shooting [PAGE 42]. The physical veto is conditional, not absolute.

The sixth trap is maximizing static friction in shoe design. The source’s varnished-floor injury example shows that too much static friction can prevent the foot from rotating while the upper body turns, tearing cartilage [PAGE 115]. The correct design target is an optimal range of static and kinetic friction for pivoting and translation, not a maximum [PAGE 115–116]. These traps are “multi-week” because each survives short qualitative tests and only fails under controlled residual analysis or a counterfactual intervention.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In a crisis, the shooter’s explicit deliberative system loses time and the trained motor program dominates. The source gives a concrete timing basis. For a jump shot, the ball should be released at the top of the jump: “when the ball is released, the upward speed of the head is about zero” [PAGE 51]. For a foul shot or set shot, the head is still moving upward at release, and the total time is about 0.45 s versus about 0.62 s for a jump shot [PAGE 55]. The wrist snap itself lasts about 0.1 s and imparts about two revolutions per second [PAGE 60–61]. Under clock pressure, a player cannot consciously rebuild these timings; the habit that regresses is the practiced release, wrist snap, and follow-through.

Crisis habit regression is therefore not automatically bad. If practice encoded the correct invariants, regression preserves them. If practice encoded a lower-ball habit, regression amplifies it. The source admits that the author lowers the ball excessively before some shots, which “takes time” and starts the shot from a lower point, helping the defender [PAGE 53–54]. The counter-trap is to delay the upward ball motion so that the ball remains with the body during the initial knee bend, preserving options to fake, dribble, or pass [PAGE 54]. That is a theory-of-mind move: the shooter models the defender’s expectation that the ball will rise immediately, then withholds the cue.

The defender’s recursive model is also explicit. After observing a few shots, “the person on defense identifies the standard release point” and occasionally goes for the ball [PAGE 46]. The shooter can counter by keeping a little height in reserve and raising the ball at the last moment, or by varying the jump with a fadeaway or lateral drift. The source notes that Michael Jordan’s jumps were seldom the same, which added difficulty for defenders [PAGE 66–67]. But the source also warns that most practice should be standard shooting, with adjusted shots proportional to their frequency in games; a game is “mostly played via reflex,” while practice is where thinking develops skill [PAGE 67]. The equilibrium is therefore a mixed strategy: mostly standard release, occasional counter-trap variation, and enough repetition that the standard shot survives crisis regression.

The clock changes the equilibrium. A jump shot buys release height but costs time and depends on apex timing. A set shot or foul shot is quicker and has a wider allowed angle range when release height is not essential [PAGE 55]. Under a short clock, the rational counter-trap may be to use the quicker set-shot pattern if the defender is not close enough to block it. The recursive belief problem is: the shooter believes the defender expects a jump-shot apex release; the defender believes the shooter will use the practiced standard; the shooter can exploit that belief once, but repeated exploitation destroys the deception. The source’s practical rule is therefore not “always improvise” but “practice the standard until it is reflexive, then think during practice about when to vary it” [PAGE 67].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The global feasibility set is the intersection
\[
\mathcal S=\mathcal S_{\mathrm{phys}}\cap\mathcal S_{\mathrm{biomech}}\cap\mathcal S_{\mathrm{env}}.
\]
A proposed action is vetoed if any layer has an empty intersection. Physical veto proof: the trajectory must satisfy the four-force ODE and pass through the hoop window. If \(\theta_{\mathrm{approach}}\) is below the model’s cutoff, \(W(\theta_{\mathrm{approach}})&lt;D_b\), so no nothing-but-net trajectory exists under the stated geometry. The source gives \(28.8^\circ\) for men and \(27.8^\circ\) for women under the full model [PAGE 54]. This veto cannot be overridden by “focus” or “will”; it is a geometric and dynamical impossibility within the model’s assumptions. A back-rim bounce can relax the threshold to \(19.0^\circ\) and \(18.0^\circ\) in the stated foul-shot geometry, but that is a different physical channel, not a violation of the nothing-but-net veto [PAGE 89].

Biomechanical veto proof: the body must deliver the required impulse, and the foot-floor interface must supply the required static friction. The source’s force-plate data show that a jump shot involves a knee bend, a rapid force rise, and a peak force; the author’s jump produced about 1,800 N, while Calvin White produced about 2,800 N, about 2.8 times body weight [PAGE 142–145]. If the required impulse exceeds the athlete’s force-time capacity, the jump or release cannot be executed. The static-friction condition \(F_{\mathrm{static}}\le\mu_{\mathrm{static}}N\) is another hard veto: if the cut or stop requires more friction than the shoe-floor pair can supply, the foot slips and kinetic friction takes over [PAGE 114–115]. The source’s injury example proves that increasing \(\mu_{\mathrm{static}}\) without bound is not a fix; a pivot can be vetoed by excessive friction as well as by insufficient friction [PAGE 115].

Environmental veto proof: the source’s working drag and Magnus equations assume a specific air density, \(\rho_{\mathrm{air}}=1.22\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), and the drag working equation is explicitly calibrated for a men’s or women’s basketball [PAGE 150]. If air density changes, the coefficients must be recalculated; the pages do not provide a wind, rain, temperature, or humidity model. Therefore any claim that outdoor weather changes a shot in a specific way is outside the source’s verifiable domain. The only environmental variables actually documented are air density, inflation pressure, court surface friction, and backboard installation. Inflation pressure is a hard environmental veto for bounce height: FIBA specifies that a ball dropped from about 1.8 m must rebound to between 1.2 m and 1.4 m, and the source shows bounce height increasing with inflation pressure [PAGE 97–98, PAGE 111]. Court surface and varnish affect the static-friction veto [PAGE 115]. Tempered-glass backboard failure is edge-sensitive and installation-sensitive, so a backboard can shatter from a small edge perturbation rather than a large force [PAGE 129–131]. The cross-layer adjudication is thus: a shot can be physically valid but biomechanically unavailable, or biomechanically available but environmentally vetoed by low pressure, wrong surface friction, or improper equipment.

Scenario 1: Ablate the four-force model on a digitized foul shot. Intervention: take the source’s Mike Heary foul-shot trajectory data and fit four models: gravity-only, gravity plus drag, gravity plus drag plus Magnus, and the complete four-force model. Verifiable prediction: gravity-only will overshoot the basket; adding drag will overcorrect downward; adding Magnus will restore lift; adding buoyancy will improve the fit, as the source reports [PAGE 30–33]. Veto condition: if the complete four-force model does not reduce position RMSE relative to gravity-only by a pre-registered margin on the digitized 1/60 s path, reject the claim that the four-force model is predictive for this shot. This is verifiable from video coordinates and model residuals.

Scenario 2: Fixed-speed launch-angle sweep. Intervention: use a mechanical launcher at the source’s foul-shot geometry, set launch speed to \(6.75\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and sweep launch angle below and above \(50.8^\circ\). Verifiable prediction: decreasing angle from \(50.8^\circ\) first moves the trajectory toward the back rim, then toward the front rim; the source states that the ball strikes the front of the hoop for angles smaller than \(45.0^\circ\) for men and \(44.8^\circ\) for women, and very small angles produce an air ball [PAGE 61]. Veto condition: if the launcher repeatedly produces nothing-but-net at \(44^\circ\) or below under the stated geometry and air conditions, the source’s fixed-speed angle map is falsified. A second check is that launch angle near \(50.8^\circ\) should minimize approach speed, not necessarily launch speed [PAGE 57].

Scenario 3: Backboard layup contact-height window. Intervention: project a basketball horizontally off a standard backboard at \(1.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) from contact heights \(0.10\), \(0.14\), \(0.35\), \(0.59\), and \(0.65\ \mathrm{m}\) above the rim. Verifiable prediction: the \(0.14\)–\(0.59\ \mathrm{m}\) contacts should enter at high rate, with the \(0.35\ \mathrm{m}\) contact near the design target; the \(0.10\ \mathrm{m}\) and \(0.65\ \mathrm{m}\) contacts should miss more often by hitting the back or front rim [PAGE 106–107].
