<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The only faithful mathematical referent for "modeling what an opponent will do" is minimax/saddle-point theory, which the book develops explicitly. Two players, payoff matrix $P$, mixed strategies $u,v$ on the simplices, expected payoff $u^TPv$ [PAGE 244]. Player 1, moving first and revealing $u$, solves $\min_u\max_i (P^Tu)_i$ [PAGE 244]; player 2 moving first solves the transposed problem [PAGE 244]. The weak max-min inequality $\sup_z\inf_w f\le\inf_w\sup_z f$ holds with no assumptions (exercise 5.24) [PAGE 295]; equality (the saddle-point property) requires convex-concave structure and closedness [PAGE 281]. This is precisely the "second-mover advantage" reasoning: the payoff to player 2 is larger if player 1 must commit first, and saddle-point equality means "there is no advantage to playing second" [PAGE 253]. The deep link back to duality: primal-dual optimal $(x^\star,\lambda^\star)$ form a saddle point of $L$ iff strong duality holds [PAGE 253]. There is no "crisis habit regression" or "clock" in a static optimality proof; the honest analogue of an adversary's evolving belief is the *perturbation dual*: an adversary choosing $(u,v)$ is bounded by shadow prices $\lambda^\star,\nu^\star$ [PAGE 265].
