- μ* is an outer measure on H(R):

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The construction of outer measures predates Halmos's 1950 text and originates with Constantin Carathéodory in 1914, who introduced the method to extend measures from algebras to σ-algebras in his work on integration theory. Before Carathéodory, Lebesgue (1902) defined measure for specific classes of sets (intervals) and used inner and outer approximations, but lacked a general framework. Borel (1898) worked with countable unions of intervals. The key insight was to define an outer measure for *all* subsets of the space, then restrict to a σ-algebra of "measurable" sets where the outer measure becomes countably additive. This allowed the treatment of more complex sets (Borel, Lebesgue) uniformly.

Historically, this development was driven by the need to integrate functions beyond step functions and to handle sets arising in analysis (e.g., projections, sections). The gray zone in the early 20th century was whether such an extension would be unique and whether the measurable sets would include all sets of interest. Carathéodory's criterion—A set E is measurable if for every test set T, μ*(T) = μ*(T ∩ E) + μ*(T ∩ E^c)—resolved this by characterizing measurability intrinsically.

The multi-week trap for students is to confuse the ring R with the σ-ring generated by R. The outer measure μ* is defined on H(R), which may be much larger than the σ-ring. The extension theorem shows that μ* agrees with μ on R, but on H(R) \ R, μ* provides a canonical assignment based on covering costs. This is often misinterpreted as defining a measure on all subsets, which fails for non-measurable sets (Vitali sets). Another trap is assuming that any outer measure arises from a measure on a ring; this is true only if the outer measure satisfies certain conditions (e.g., regularity). The genealogy thus reveals a progression from concrete geometric measures (Lebesgue) to abstract set-function axioms (Carathéodory), enabling modern probability theory (Kolmogorov, 1933) and functional analysis (Banach spaces of integrable functions).
