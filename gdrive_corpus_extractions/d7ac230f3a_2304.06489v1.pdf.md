Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Recursive theory of mind here refers to the model’s nested inference about the user’s intent while the user’s own distribution is shifting. Three dynamical regimes compete.

Crisis habit regression: when the target distribution shifts abruptly—new user, new sensor position, or new device—the feature extractor regresses to source-domain priors. Because earlier layers are assumed to capture generic embeddings ([PAGE 3]), the model emits source-like activations for target samples that lie outside the training manifold. On [PAGE 10]–[PAGE 11], cross-person heterogeneity is described as variability in body size, shape, movement patterns, and behavior traits; under crisis, the model defaults to the “average” person in the source set, misclassifying idiosyncratic target motions. This is habit regression: the predictor P(y|x) collapses to Pₛ(y|x) rather than adapting to Pₜ(y|x).

Counter-trap: pseudo-labeling creates a self-referential trap. Co-training and self-training generate target labels from the current model ([PAGE 9], [PAGE 12]); if early pseudo-labels are wrong, they are fed back as ground truth, reinforcing error. [PAGE 12] speculates that incorrect pseudo-labels cause “negative learning”; [PAGE 20] names this “conﬁrmation bias.” The counter-trap is recursive because the model’s belief about the target labels becomes both the input and the supervision signal, with no external oracle to break the loop unless active learning intervenes ([PAGE 9], [51]).

Clock: real-time domain adaptation ([PAGE 18], [50]) operates under a latency constraint. Target samples arrive sequentially; the adaptation clock is the inter-arrival interval versus the convergence time of batch-normalization statistics or adversarial alignment. [PAGE 18] states that [50] performs adaptation “on-the-fly” because unlabeled data cannot be stored. If the adaptation clock is slower than the drift clock (concept drift over days/weeks, [PAGE 15]–[PAGE 16]), the model is permanently chasing a moving target. The recursive theory of mind must therefore estimate not only the user’s current activity label but also the rate at which the user’s own sensor distribution is aging out of the source domain.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and environmental veto proof.

Domain adaptation should not be attempted unconditionally. A veto adjudication layer must evaluate three cross-layer confounders before alignment is permitted.

Physical layer (sensor hardware): Cross-device heterogeneity arises from measurement ranges, manufacturer, model, and calibration ([PAGE 15]). Two smartphones with nominally identical accelerometers produce different readings for the same activity. Veto condition: if sensor noise floors or dynamic ranges differ beyond the calibration envelope of the normalization layer ([PAGE 8]), distribution alignment aligns noise rather than signal, and adaptation must be vetoed.

Biomechanical layer (body placement): Cross-position heterogeneity ([PAGE 13]–[PAGE 14]) is not a mere statistical shift. Wrist and ankle accelerometer signals differ because of distinct limb movement patterns, amplitudes, frequencies, and orientations. If the source domain is ankle-mounted and the target is wrist-mounted, the mapping between feature spaces may be non-diffeomorphic; aligning P(Xₛ) and P(Xₜ) cannot recover a conditional P(y|x) that is biomechanically distinct. Veto condition: when source and target body positions engage different kinematic chains, marginal alignment is physically invalid.

Environmental layer (context and weather): [PAGE 15]–[PAGE 16] ties concept drift to environmental change; [PAGE 19]–[PAGE 20] note the lack of multi-modal datasets pairing IMU with environmental sensors. Temperature, humidity, and surface type alter IMU noise and activity execution. Veto condition: if environmental covariates are unobserved and correlated with label shifts, the ignorability assumption P(Yₛ|Xₛ) = P(Yₜ|Xₜ) is violated.

Adjudication rule: proceed with domain adaptation only if (i) hardware is calibrated or harmonized, (ii) source and target placements lie on the same kinematic chain or a learned spatial transform exists ([36], [PAGE 7]), and (iii) environmental stationarity holds over the adaptation window. Otherwise, veto and trigger full recalibration or source-free adaptation ([PAGE 5], Table 2).

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR veto scenarios and counterfactual predictions.

RLVR (reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards) veto scenarios are defined as binary gates: if the verifiable condition holds, domain adaptation is vetoed and a fallback (recalibration or source-only inference) is executed. Each scenario is testable on the datasets and metrics reported in the source.

Veto Scenario 1 — Cross-position extremity veto.
Condition: source = ankle, target = wrist (or vice versa) on PAMAP2 or DSADS, where [PAGE 13]–[PAGE 14] documents biomechanical divergence.
Counterfactual prediction: adversarial marginal alignment will reduce domain discrepancy but F1 will collapse toward the 40–60% semi-supervised baseline reported on [PAGE 14] for DSADS/PAMAP, because P(y|x) shifts with limb dynamics. Vetoing adaptation and using a position-specific model or source-free DA should outperform forced alignment. Verifiable via a held-out wrist↔ankle split measuring F1 with and without gradient-reversal alignment ([33], [PAGE 14]).

Veto Scenario 2 — Pseudo-label confirmation trap.
Condition: semi-supervised DA with ≤10% labeled target data on DSADS/PAMAP, where [PAGE 14] reports 40–60% accuracy and [PAGE 20] attributes underperformance to confirmation bias.
Counterfactual prediction: gating pseudo-labels by entropy threshold (accept only high-confidence predictions) will prevent negative transfer. If vetoed when mean pseudo-label entropy exceeds a threshold, the model should remain at or above the unsupervised adversarial baseline ([45], [PAGE 12]) rather than degrading. Verifiable by entropy-ablation on AugToAct [32] or STL [30] using the 20% labeled-target setting from [PAGE 12].

Veto Scenario 3 — Concept-drift temporal veto.
Condition: target IMU stream exceeds the adaptation window without recalibration, invoking concept drift from device wear, environment, or behavior ([PAGE 15]–[PAGE 16], [100]).
Counterfactual prediction: after a drift interval (e.g., multi-week simulation on WISDM/PAMAP2 by injecting sensor-noise or distribution shifts), a static adapted model’s F1 will decay below 78.5% (the [PAGE 16] cross-sensor benchmark from [65]); a veto that triggers full recalibration will restore performance. Verifiable by synthetically aging PAMAP2 or WISDM streams and measuring F1 decay curves against an online AdaBN baseline ([50], [PAGE 8], [PAGE 18]).
