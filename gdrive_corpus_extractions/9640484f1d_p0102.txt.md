1.  **Polygenic Trait Model (Infinitesimal Model):**

    \[
    P = \mu + G + E
    \]
    where:
    *   \( \mu \) = population mean (constant).
    *   \( G = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i x_i \) = additive genetic value, sum over \( n \) loci, with \( a_i \) being the effect size of allele substitution at locus \( i \), and \( x_i \) the number of "increasing" alleles (0,1,2) at that locus. This assumes additivity (no dominance/epistasis for simplicity).
    *   \( E \) = environmental deviation, assumed uncorrelated with \( G \) within the population.
    *   **Units:** \( P, \mu, G, E \) in meters (or cm). \( a_i \) in meters per allele.
    *   **Breakdown Regime:** This model breaks down if major effect loci exist (violating infinitesimal assumption) or if gene-environment correlation/covariance is strong.

2.  **Parent-Offspring Regression & Heritability:**
    From the regression of offspring (\( O \)) on mid-parent (\( M \)):
    \[
    O = \alpha + \beta M + \varepsilon
    \]
    where \( \beta \) is the regression coefficient. Galton found \( \beta \approx 2/3 \).
    Under the additive model, the narrow-sense heritability (\( h^2 \)) is equal to this regression coefficient:
    \[
    h^2 = \frac{Cov(O, M)}{Var(M)} = \beta
    \]
    More precisely, if \( M = (P_1 + P_2)/2 \), then \( Cov(O, M) = \frac{1}{2}V_A \) and \( Var(M) = \frac{1}{2}V_P + \frac{1}{2}V_A \) under random mating. In large samples with no assortative mating, \( \beta \to V_A/V_P = h^2 \).
    *   **Units:** Dimensionless (ratio of variances).
    *   **Singularity:** If \( Var(M) \to 0 \) (no variation among parents), \( \beta \) is undefined. If \( h^2 = 0 \), offspring are unrelated to parents.

3.  **Fraternal Correlation (for siblings raised together):**
    The expected correlation between full siblings (\( r_{FS} \)) is:
    \[
    r_{FS} = \frac{1}{2}h^2 + c^2
    \]
    where \( c^2 \) is the proportion of variance due to shared environment. Galton's "Variability in Fraternities" section would aim to estimate this.
    *   **Assumption:** No assortative mating, no common environmental effects beyond family.
    *   **Breakdown:** With assortative mating, the sibling correlation increases.

4.  **Reformulation of Galton's "Law of Filial Regression":**
    Galton's empirical finding can be stated as:
    \[
    E[O | M] = \mu + \frac{2}{3}(M - \mu)
    \]
    This shows the deviation of offspring from the mean is 2/3 the deviation of the mid-parent from the mean.

**Historical Lineage:**
*   **Pre-Galton (Blending Inheritance):** Darwin and others posited that parental traits blend like fluids in offspring. This explained regression to the mean but predicted variance would halve each generation, making natural selection impossible.
*   **Galton's Breakthrough (1886-1889):** Through meticulous measurement of peas and humans (stature), Galton discovered that while offspring resemble parents, they also "revert" toward ancestral mediocrity. He invented correlation and regression to quantify this. The 1889 book is the magnum opus synthesizing these findings.
*   **Mendel (1865, rediscovered 1900):** Provided the mechanism of particulate inheritance (genes). Initially seen as contradictory to Galton's continuous variation, R.A. Fisher (1918) reconciled them: many Mendelian factors (polygenes) produce a continuous distribution, and the regression formula emerges naturally from Mendelian segregation.
*   **Biometrician vs. Mendelian Debate:** Galton's student Karl Pearson became a leader of the Biometric school, emphasizing continuous variation and statistics. Wilhelm Johannsen led the Mendelian school, emphasizing discrete factors. Fisher's synthesis united them, proving Galton's statistical laws were consequences of Mendelian genetics.
*   **Quantitative Genetics (1930s-present):** The field built directly on Galton's methods. Sewall Wright's path analysis, Lush's animal breeding, Falconer's textbooks—all use the variance decomposition initiated here. Modern GWAS and genomic prediction are high-dimensional extensions.

**Multi-Week/Month Traps for Analysts:**
*   **Trap 1: Confounding Regression with Causation.** An analyst might see Galton's "regression" and immediately think of causal models. However, Galton's regression was purely statistical description, not causal inference. Mistaking it for a causal law leads to flawed policy (e.g., assuming tallness causes intelligence).
*   **Trap 2: Ignoring Environmental Change.** Applying historical heritability estimates (like Galton's) to modern populations ignores massive environmental improvements (nutrition, healthcare) that increase mean height and alter heritability estimates. A static view is a trap.
*   **Trap 3: Equating Statistical Association with Biological Determinism.** Galton's work was used by eugenicists to argue for racial superiority based on height. The scientific method described here is neutral; the misuse comes from conflating population statistics with individual destiny or moral worth.
*   **Trap 4: Overlooking Gene-Environment Correlation.** The modern understanding that individuals select environments correlated with their genetics (active rGE) means that even Galton's careful controls for "bringing up" were incomplete. Passive rGE (parents provide both genes and environment) inflates heritability estimates if not modeled.
