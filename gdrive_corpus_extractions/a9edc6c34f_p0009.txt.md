Once `O` crosses the calibrated `P_{thresh}` (e.g., 19% in a 7-game set), the algorithm triggers the `$2` penalty, increasing the salary of those very players. This forces managers into a second-order dilemma: Do I pay the premium for my preferred player because their underlying production (`F1/F3`) justifies it despite the higher cost? Or do I chase "value" by selecting a lower-owned alternative, knowing that if everyone else makes the same contrarian leap, that alternative's ownership might then spike, triggering its own penalty and neutralizing the value? 

This creates a recursive loop of anticipation. A sophisticated manager (Player A) predicts that the crowd will avoid a superstar due to the impending `OP` trigger, thus lowering the superstar's *actual* ownership below the threshold, allowing Player A to draft them at the base price—a classic "contrarian arbitrage." However, if enough sophisticated agents (Players B-Z) arrive at the same conclusion, ownership rises back above the threshold, re-triggering the penalty. The system thereby enforces a Nash equilibrium where the true market-clearing price is constantly oscillating around the penalty boundary. The "crisis habit" regression manifests when managers, faced with complex series-length calculations, revert to simple heuristics like "always start the highest F3 player," which guarantees they hit the `OP` trap repeatedly, slowly bleeding salary cap efficiency against opponents who correctly model the population's fear of the penalty.
