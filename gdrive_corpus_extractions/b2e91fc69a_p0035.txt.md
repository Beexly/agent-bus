The ring of sets descends from Boole’s “classes of thought” (1854) and De Morgan’s duality of union and intersection, but its measure-theoretic crystallization occurs in the early 20th century. Veto proposition. The class \(R_{\mathbb{R}}\) of finite unions of bounded, left-closed, right-open intervals [PAGE 35] admits a physical/biomechanical veto under endpoint perturbation.

Proof. Consider the biomechanical operation of proper difference on two adjacent half-open intervals:
\[
A = [0,2), \qquad B = [0,1).
\]
Both are in \(R_{\mathbb{R}}\). The proper difference is
\[
A \setminus B = [1,2) \in R_{\mathbb{R}}.
\]
Closure holds here because the shared endpoint \(1\) is open on the right for \(B\) and closed on the left for the remainder.

Now apply a weather perturbation: let the right endpoint of \(B\) be a random variable \(\beta = 1 + \epsilon\) with \(\epsilon \sim \text{Uniform}(-\delta,\delta)\), \(\delta > 0\) small, so \(B_\epsilon = [0,1+\epsilon)\). Then
\[
A \setminus B_\epsilon = [1+\epsilon, 2).
\]
If \(\epsilon < 0\), the set \([1+\epsilon,2)\) still belongs to \(R_{\mathbb{R}}\) (single half-open interval). If \(\epsilon > 0\), the set is still \([1+\epsilon,2)\), also in \(R_{\mathbb{R}}\). So proper difference is robust for this pair.

The real veto appears under physical substitution of closed intervals. Let the physical layer produce closed intervals instead of half-open ones:
\[
C = [0,1], \qquad D = [0,1].
\]
Then \(C \setminus D = \emptyset \in R_{\mathbb{R}}\), no problem. But consider
\[
E = [0,2], \qquad F = [0,1].
\]
The proper difference is
\[
E \setminus F = (1,2].
\]
The interval \((1,2]\) is left-open and right-closed. It cannot be expressed as a finite union of bounded, left-closed, right-open intervals \([a,b)\) with \(a < b\), because any such interval containing points arbitrarily close to \(1\) from above must have left endpoint \(a \le 1\), but then it includes \(1\) itself (since \(a \in [a,b)\)), polluting the difference set with \(1 \notin (1,2]\). Hence \((1,2] \notin R_{\mathbb{R}}\).

Biomechanical veto: the transformation “remove sub-interval” creates a boundary with the wrong handedness. The half-open convention on [PAGE 35] is load-bearing; flipping it to closed intervals breaks proper-difference closure almost surely under any physical layer that generates closed boundaries.

Weather veto: if endpoints are subject to continuous random noise, the event that a finite union of intervals remains exactly representable with rational or algebraic endpoints under perturbation has probability zero; however, the stronger structural veto is deterministic: the half-open/closed asymmetry is not smoothed by noise—it is a topological invariant of the construction. Therefore any physical model that assumes closed-interval regions will fail the ring axioms on [PAGE 35] with certainty.

Scenario 1: Closed-interval substitution.
Counterfactual proposal: Replace the generating class on [PAGE 35] with finite unions of bounded closed intervals \([a,b]\), \(a < b\), on \(X = \mathbb{R}\), and assert it is a ring.

Verify \((1,2]\) cannot be written as \(\bigcup_{i=1}^n [a_i,b_i]\) with finitely many \(a_i < b_i\): any such union covering points arbitrarily close to \(1\) from above must include \(1\) in some \([a_i,b_i]\), but \(1 \notin (1,2]\). Contradiction.
Prediction: The proposed class fails proper-difference closure; the verifier returns VETO, and the model predicts finite additivity cannot be consistently initialized on this class without switching to half-open intervals.

Scenario 2: Singleton class on \(\mathbb{N}\).
Counterfactual proposal: On \(X = \mathbb{N}\), let \(C = \{\emptyset\} \cup \{ \{n\} : n \in \mathbb{N} \}\). Claim: \(C\) is a ring because it is closed under intersection and proper difference.
Verifiable veto condition: Verify \(\{1\} \in C\) and \(\{2\} \in C\). Compute \(\{1\} \cup \{2\} = \{1,2\}\). Check membership: \(\{1,2\} \notin C\) because \(C\) contains only \(\emptyset\) and singletons.
Prediction: The union axiom fails; VETO is triggered. This falsifies the intuition that intersection-plus-difference suffices, confirming Halmos’s asymmetry warning on [PAGE 35].

Scenario 3: Countable-union overreach on \(R_{\mathbb{R}}\).
Counterfactual proposal: Extend \(R_{\mathbb{R}}\) (finite unions of bounded half-open intervals [PAGE 35]) by assuming closure under countable unions without changing the definition, and define a finitely additive measure \(\mu\) by interval length.
Verifiable veto condition: Let \(A_n = [n,n+1)\) for \(n = 0,1,2,\dots\). Each \(A_n \in R_{\mathbb{R}}\). Form \(A = \bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty} A_n = [0,\infty)\). Check \(A \notin R_{\mathbb{R}}\) because \(A\) is unbounded and not a finite union of bounded intervals.
Prediction: The ring is not a \(\sigma\)-ring; any measure defined only on \(R_{\mathbb{R}}\) cannot assign \(\mu([0,\infty))\) via countable additivity. Verifier returns VETO for countable additivity; prediction is that \(\mu\) remains finitely additive only, and extension to \([0,\infty)\) requires Carathéodory extension to a \(\sigma\)-algebra.
