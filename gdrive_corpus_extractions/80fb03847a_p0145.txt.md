<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The concept of the "gradient of reinforcement" emerges directly from Edward L. Thorndike's Law of Effect (1898), which posited that behaviors followed by satisfying states are stamped in. However, Thorndike's original formulation was vaguely temporal. The genealogy of this problem involves a shift from teleological explanations ("purposeful striving") to mechanistic, contiguous associations. 

In 1931, G.W. Sprague conducted pivotal work on the "delay of reinforcement," demonstrating that even massive delays (up to 60 seconds in some setups) could still support maze learning in rats, though the rate diminished. This historical lineage establishes that the gradient is not a binary switch but a continuous function. The retrocausal aspect lies in the fact that a future event (eating) is perceived to exert causal force backward in time to strengthen a past event (digging/turning). 

The trap laid by this history is the assumption of a fixed "backward shadow." Early behaviorists like Watson (cited in the text) treated behavior as a simple S-R chain, struggling to account for how an R could connect to an E separated by seconds. The multi-week trap in modern cognitive science is the over-reliance on "reward prediction error" (Dopamine RPE) models, which often assume a discrete, punctate timing signal. The Thorndikian gradient suggests a much broader, perhaps diffuse, field of temporal credit assignment that persists long after the dopamine transient has returned to baseline. The transition from Watson's sawdust box to Hamilton's retention chamber highlights an evolutionary refinement in experimental design aimed at isolating the temporal variable from spatial complexity—a lineage that continues in modern interval-scheduling paradigms.
