**Physical/biomechanical veto – Recombination and segregation:** During meiosis, homologous chromosomes exchange segments via crossing‑over, producing gametes that are mosaics of the two parental genomes. Consequently, the proportion of an ancestor’s DNA inherited by a descendant is a binomial random variable with mean \(2^{-g}\) but variance proportional to \(2^{-g}(1-2^{-g})\). **Mitochondrial and Y‑chromosome veto – Uniparental inheritance:** The binary tree treats all ancestors symmetrically, but certain genetic elements follow strictly uniparental routes. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is transmitted exclusively through mothers; therefore only ancestors with odd indices (female line) can contribute mtDNA. Any model that assumes equal autosomal contribution from all labelled nodes will overestimate the diversity of these uniparental markers.

**Environmental veto – Gene‑environment interaction (G×E):** Phenotypic expression depends not only on genotype but also on environmental exposures, which vary across individuals and locations. Identical twins (who share the same pedigree index set) can exhibit markedly different traits due to differential nutrition, stress, or microbial exposure. The pedigree notation, being purely genealogical, lacks any mechanism to incorporate such factors, rendering its predictive power limited to narrow‑sense heritability estimates that ignore G×E.

**Epigenetic veto – Non‑DNA‑sequence inheritance:** Chemical modifications to DNA and histones (e.g., methylation) can be transmitted across generations and influence gene expression without altering the underlying sequence. These marks are often reset during gametogenesis, but some escape erasure, leading to transgenerational epigenetic inheritance. Such phenomena create inheritance pathways that bypass the Mendelian segregation rules assumed by the binary tree, introducing additional layers of complexity that the integer labels cannot capture.

Collectively, these vetoes demonstrate that the pedigree index is a necessary but insufficient descriptor of biological inheritance. Robust models must integrate probabilistic transmission, uniparental markers, environmental covariates, and epigenetic dynamics to avoid over‑reliance on a historically convenient but biologically incomplete formalism.
