(ii) Workload-ratio dilution trap: the acute:chronic workload ratio (ACWR) contributed little globally, plausibly because its signal was diluted by the 21-day input window [PAGE 10]; shortening or lengthening that window changes the very risk constructs being measured. (iii) Horizon compression trap: baseline models showed significant performance reduction when the forecasting window exceeded one day [PAGE 6], so the 7-day DeepHit horizon is a hard boundary; extending it for planning convenience degrades calibration without retraining. (iv) RNN infeasibility trap: missingness and irregular temporal spacing precluded Dynamic-DeepHit with recurrent backbones [PAGE 11], meaning the model cannot assimilate unevenly spaced training/match events natively. (v) Missingness-as-outcome trap: subjective missingness 7d was a key predictor, potentially because players withhold responses when anticipating injury [PAGE 10]; this creates a retrocausal loop in which the input partially encodes the outcome the model is trying to forecast.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Crisis habit regression. Under staffing pressure or short turnaround between matches, practitioners tend to regress to static pre-season binary classifiers (Random Forest, XGBoost, Logistic Regression) despite their documented shortcomings: these cannot capture temporal dependencies or cumulative workload effects central to injury development (Delecroix et al., 2018 [PAGE 2]), and they cannot adjust to mid-season environmental changes (Leckey et al., 2024 [PAGE 2]). On this dataset the best baseline, Random Forest, achieved F1 = 0.533 with precision = 1.000 and recall = 0.364 [PAGE 6], while DeepHit reached C-index = 0.762 [PAGE 6]. Regressing to the baseline sacrifices roughly 0.23 of concordance-equivalent discrimination and abandons individualized, time-varying risk estimates [PAGE 1]. It also reintroduces the season-wide caution problem the paper explicitly criticizes [PAGE 2].

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is iatrogenic: labeling a player “high risk” without explainability can heighten anxiety or hesitant play, which itself increases injury likelihood (Slimani et al., 2018 [PAGE 2]), and algorithmic bias could unjustifiably exclude players from training or competition (Musat et al., 2024 [PAGE 2]). Without interpretable output, coaches cannot identify causes of increased risk or adjust training accordingly (Rossi et al., 2021 [PAGE 2]). The study mitigates this via SHAP: on the highest-LOPO player, global season-long drivers were high stress, high high-intensity running volume, low mood, and low sleep quality, whereas the specific high-risk day preceding an acute thigh injury added high fatigue as a primary contributor [PAGE 8, 10]. However, SHAP is a local linear approximation; because speed metrics and load features are correlated, local attributions can shift between global and day-specific views [PAGE 10], so explanation stability is itself a recursion point.

Clock. The model clock consists of the 21-day look-back input window and the 7-day prediction horizon [PAGE 5], producing daily time-varying risk estimates [PAGE 1]. If coaching decisions operate on a slower clock (e.g., only weekly load planning), the predicted risk window may already have shifted by the time an intervention lands. The recursive loop is prediction → training-load adjustment → altered future load trajectory → changed injury risk → new prediction. Without per-player risk thresholds and rolling-origin re-evaluation [PAGE 10, 11], the model clock desynchronizes from the player’s physiological clock, particularly after acute injury or return-to-play transitions where time-since-last-injury is not yet modeled [PAGE 11].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and environmental veto proof.

Physical layer (sensor and plausibility vetoes). Raw GPS/heart-rate inputs were cleaned before modeling: maximum speeds above 32 km h⁻¹ were excluded as physiologically implausible (Vescovi, 2018 [PAGE 3]); sessions longer than 200 minutes were excluded as signaling errors [PAGE 3]; running distances above 16 km were excluded, while retaining values up to that bound because histogram inspection showed valid observations (typical match distance 9–11 km per Datson et al., 2017 [PAGE 3]). Any incoming observation exceeding these physical bounds triggers an automatic veto: speed > 32 km h⁻¹, duration > 200 min, or distance > 16 km are outside the training distribution and cannot produce valid DeepHit risk estimates.

Biomechanical layer (tissue and injury-history vetoes). Thighs and knees were the most frequently injured body parts [PAGE 6], with 24 acute and 19 overuse injuries recorded [PAGE 6]. “Past injury count” is a cumulative derived feature encoding reinjury propensity [PAGE 4]. High-intensity running volume, soreness, monotony, and fatigue were established SHAP drivers [PAGE 8, 10]. A biomechanical veto fires when a player presents with acute thigh/knee soreness concurrent with elevated recent high-intensity running distance (sp_hir_d) or high monotony/strain, regardless of the model’s raw C-index ranking, because the MLP does not explicitly represent tissue strain, joint kinematics, or collision mechanics.

Weather/environmental layer (absent-covariate veto). Table 1 contains no meteorological variables (temperature, precipitation, wind, pitch hardness, humidity) [PAGE 4]. Because cold/wet conditions alter muscle-tendon compliance and traction, model risk estimates are unadjusted for environmental modifiers. A weather veto applies whenever conditions fall outside the historical training envelope of the 2020–2021 Norwegian season; under such conditions, DeepHit outputs must be down-weighted and coach judgment substituted.

Adjudication rule. A DeepHit prediction is actionable for an individual player-day only if all four conditions hold: (a) player-stratum C-index ≥ 0.70 [PAGE 8]; (b) every raw input lies within the physical cleaning bounds of [PAGE 3]; (c) SHAP attribution aligns with established mechanisms (load, stress, recovery, prior injury) rather than spurious time-of-day proxies [PAGE 10]; and (d) no weather/pitch extremity exists outside the dataset envelope [PAGE 4]. Failure of any condition defaults the decision to coach-led load management without algorithmic override.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with falsifiable predictions.

Scenario 1 — High subjective missingness veto.
Condition: a held-out player exhibits subjective missingness 7d > 0.5 (more than half of daily questionnaires incomplete over the preceding week).
Verifiable basis: 15 of 37 players had approximately half their data missing on training days [PAGE 6], and LOPO performance was poor for players with irregular, sparse, or outlier data relative to teammates [PAGE 7, 9].
Veto: suppress individualized risk prescription; revert to team-level monitoring until adherence improves.
Prediction: the held-out player’s LOPO C-index falls below 0.70 (the acceptable threshold [PAGE 8]) and likely occupies the lower half of the LOPO distribution whose interquartile range is 0.192 [PAGE 7]. If deployed anyway, the bespoke imputation will substitute teammate-relative values [PAGE 3] and encode disengagement rather than physiological risk [PAGE 10], inflating false-risk assignments.

Scenario 2 — Temporal horizon/architecture violation veto.
Condition: the forecasting horizon is extended beyond 7 days or the input window reduced below 21 days without complete retraining, chronological re-split, and LOPO re-validation.
Verifiable basis: DeepHit was implemented with a fixed 21-day input window and 7-day prediction horizon [PAGE 5]; baseline models with forecasting windows greater than one day showed significant performance reductions and were not pursued [PAGE 6]; linear-interpolation DeepHit achieved only C-index = 0.660 versus 0.762 for bespoke imputation [PAGE 6].
Veto: reject the horizon change and require full re-validation before any operational use.
Prediction: C-index declines by more than 0.10 absolute (from 0.762 to below 0.662), approaching or underperforming the linear-interpolation baseline [PAGE 6] and breaching the C > 0.7 acceptability boundary [PAGE 8].

Scenario 3 — Physiological outlier / low-adherence player veto.
Condition: a new or held-out player is a clear outlier relative to teammates or has fewer than roughly 50% of expected sessions tracked.
Verifiable basis: C-index correlated positively with sessions tracked (r = 0.44) but not with injury count (r = –0.08); outliers relative to teammates and players with higher within-player variance reduced predictive performance [PAGE 7]; LOPO C-indices ranged widely, peaking at 0.974 but performing poorly for others [PAGE 7].
Veto: withhold individual prediction until fine-tuning with individual player data or per-player thresholds are established [PAGE 9].
Prediction: that player’s C-index is below 0.70 and likely in the bottom quartile of LOPO performance; the global C-index of 0.762 [PAGE 6] does not generalize to this stratum without personalization [PAGE 9].
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
