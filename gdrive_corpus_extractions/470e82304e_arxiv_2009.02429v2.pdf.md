<THINK_TRACE>
The source is a computer-vision paper, not a match-prediction paper: Chan, Levine, and Javan build an offline ResNet+LSTM tracklet classifier for NHL broadcast player identification by jersey number [PAGE 1]. The multi-hop derivation is: broadcast frames are low-resolution and players occlude/blur each other [PAGE 2]; a single frame therefore gives a noisy posterior over 81 classes; a tracklet supplies repeated observations of the same latent identity; a recurrent layer can accumulate evidence within a 16-frame window [PAGE 10]; and a second 1D-CNN can learn systematic digit-confusion patterns in the averaged confidence vector [PAGE 13]. Formally, the pipeline is a composition \(f_{\text{ResNet}}\rightarrow \mathrm{LSTM}\rightarrow \mathrm{softmax}\rightarrow \text{mean fusion}\rightarrow g_{\text{1D-CNN}}\). The paper reports ResNet-10 at 59.08%, LRCN at 80.83%, ResNet+LSTM at 82.79%, and ResNet+LSTM with final 1D-CNN at 87.01% [PAGE 23]. The claimed causal gain is temporal evidence: “Our ResNet+LSTM network delivered the highest performance in player tracklet identification with an 82.79% accuracy rate, followed by the LRCN with an accuracy of 80.83% and the ResNet-10 with an accuracy of 59.08%” [PAGE 16]. The late-fusion gain is smaller but material: “Taking advantage of these patterns in the confidence scores has proved to increase the accuracy results by 4.22%” [PAGE 13].

The gray-zone audit must separate what the paper proves from what it only reports. It proves, on its NHL-HPT test split, that a sequence model beats a single-frame ResNet-10 baseline and that a secondary 1D-CNN beats arithmetic mean fusion under the authors’ protocol. It does not prove player-disjoint or game-disjoint generalization: the paper says “We randomly divide the dataset into two sets: a training set with 5,267 tracklet examples, and a test set with 1,278 tracklet examples” [PAGE 8]. If multiple tracklets from the same player or game cross the split, the 87.01% figure may be optimistic for unseen players. A second gray zone is class semantics: the 81 classes include “79 diﬀerent jersey numbers, referees, and unidentiﬁable tracklets” [PAGE 8], so accuracy mixes jersey-number identification, referee rejection, and unknown rejection. A third gray zone is visibility: labels are set by inspection of the back jersey number, but “a player's jersey number may not be visible in every image in a tracklet” [PAGE 8]; the model is therefore not always doing OCR, but sometimes temporal inference over partially visible digits. A fourth gray zone is the 16-frame LSTM window: the paper states that “the confidence probability increases within intervals of 16 frames” and that “At the beginning of each section of 16 frames, the confidence probability is low since the LSTM has not yet observed any previous frames” [PAGE 17]. This is a clock boundary, not a continuous long-memory claim. A fifth gray zone is the offline design: “the full system is designed as an oﬄine solution since the ﬁnal player number of the player tracklet can only be predicted by the secondary CNN classiﬁer after the fusion of the partial bounding box predictions” [PAGE 9]. Any real-time interpretation would be an extrapolation beyond the source. Finally, the model deliberately excludes priors: “we identify players solely based on the visual features of the jersey number without taking into consideration any priors, such as game or player context” [PAGE 5]. That is a clean visual-only assumption, but it also means the system cannot resolve a fully hidden back number except by abstention or learned visual artifacts. The correct audit stance is therefore: accept the reported within-dataset ranking, but treat the absolute 87.01% as conditional on random tracklet splitting, class imbalance, visible-number prevalence, and the offline fusion protocol.

The LSTM maps the sequence to hidden states: \((h_t,c_t)=\mathrm{LSTM}_{\psi}(v_t,h_{t-1},c_{t-1})\), with \(h_t,c_t\in\mathbb{R}^{256}\) [PAGE 10]. The paper states: “The LSTM layer has 256 hidden units and receives a series of 16 512-dimensional vectors as an input sequence from the CNN layers” [PAGE 10]. Dropout uses rate \(p=0.5\), then a fully connected layer and softmax produce an 81-dimensional probability vector \(p_t=\mathrm{softmax}(W_o\tilde h_t+b_o)\), where \(p_{t,m}\in[0,1]\) and \(\sum_{m=1}^{81}p_{t,m}=1\). The source says the “softmax classiﬁer outputs an 81-dimensional vector (for 81 classes in our dataset) as a conﬁdence score” [PAGE 11].

The residual operator is explicitly given. For a residual stack, \(y=F(x,W_i)+x\), and for the 2-layer stacks \(F(x,W_i)=W_2\sigma(W_1x)\); when dimensions differ, the projection form is \(y=W_2\sigma(W_1x)+W_sx\) [PAGE 10]. Here \(x,y\) are dimensionless feature-map tensors, \(W_1,W_2,W_s\) are dimensionless weight tensors, and \(\sigma\) denotes batch normalization plus ReLU. The exact breakdown regime is shape mismatch: \(F(x,W_i)+x\) is undefined unless \(x\) and \(F\) have equal shape; the source states “y = F(x, Wi) + x is only computable if the dimensions of x and F are equal” and that a projection shortcut is required in the second, third, and fourth stacks [PAGE 10]. The architecture table gives input \(240\times320\times3\rightarrow227\times227\times3\), Conv1 \(7\times7,64,\text{stride }2,\text{pad }3\), four residual stacks, global average pooling, LSTM 256 hidden units with a 16-frame sequence, dropout 0.5, and an 81-class FC [PAGE 12].

Late fusion is arithmetic mean followed by a secondary classifier. For \(F_n=[p_{1n},\ldots,p_{Mn}]\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\) with \(M=81\), the paper computes \(F_{\text{avg}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}F_n\in\mathbb{R}^{81}\) [PAGE 12]. It then predicts \(\hat y=\arg\max_{m}\,[g_\phi(F_{\text{avg}})]_m\), where \(g_\phi\) is the shallow 1D-CNN. The paper says “we first calculate the average conﬁdence score for each tracklet by averaging the probabilities for each label across all the frames” [PAGE 12]. The 1D-CNN has 6 convolutional layers and 3 fully connected layers, with input \(1\times81\), \(1\times3\) filters, stride 1, padding 1, residual additions, FC sizes 256, 256, 81 [PAGE 14]. Exact breakdown regimes: if \(N=0\), the average \(F_{\text{avg}}\) is undefined because the denominator is zero; the source assumes nonzero tracklets because its tracklets are at least 16 frames [PAGE 17]. If \(M=0\), softmax is undefined; the source fixes \(M=81\) [PAGE 12]. If the 1D-CNN’s padding is removed, the spatial length shrinks and residual additions can become shape-invalid; the paper avoids this by using “ﬁlter sizes of 1x3 with a stride of 1 and padding of 1 on each side” [PAGE 13]. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The nested observers are: the player, who knows his identity but may face away; the camera, which sees only visible surfaces; the annotator, who inspects the back number across images; the ResNet+LSTM, which maintains a per-frame belief over 81 classes; the 1D-CNN, which sees only the averaged confidence vector; and the evaluator, who compares the final label to ground truth. The player has no obligation to display the number; the camera’s belief is limited by “occlusion, motion blur, and the angle in which the players are facing the camera” [PAGE 2]; the annotator can label unknown when the number is not visible [PAGE 8]; the model is trained visual-only, without “game or player context” [PAGE 5]; and the fusion classifier sees the model’s confidence pattern, not raw pixels [PAGE 13]. This recursion matters because a high-confidence model belief is not the same as an annotator’s visibility belief.

Crisis habit regression appears when the model’s belief collapses to a habitual digit neighborhood under blur. The paper reports that “Clusters can be seen in the matrix, which suggests that these numbers were predicted as another number that is chronologically adjacent or close” [PAGE 22], and that “Visually, it is easier to mislabel a single digit number with another single digit number when players are blurry” [PAGE 22]. The counter-trap is to inject a prior such as time-on-ice to narrow candidates; that would violate the source’s visual-only premise, and the paper explicitly says such context “were not available” [PAGE 5]. The clock is the 16-frame LSTM window: within each window, confidence rises as hidden state accumulates, then resets at the next window boundary [PAGE 17]. Thus the model’s theory of the player is periodically reborn every 16 frames. The correct recursive audit is: do not interpret a confident frame as proof of identity; interpret it as evidence conditional on visibility, angle, and window position. If the back number is invisible across the whole tracklet, the annotator’s correct belief is “unknown,” and a confident jersey-number belief is a false belief, not a successful inference.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical/optical veto is primary. The source says the jersey number is “not always visible due to occlusion, motion blur, and the angle in which the players are facing the camera” [PAGE 2]. If the back number is not visible anywhere in the tracklet, the dataset reserves “unidentiﬁable tracklets” and the label “unknown” [PAGE 8]. Therefore a high-confidence non-unknown prediction on a fully invisible tracklet is vetoed by the physical visibility layer: there is no visual evidence to support it. The resolution layer also vetoes fine facial/body identification: teammates are “almost indistinguishable from each other” because of “limited frame resolution” [PAGE 2]. The biomechanical/orientation layer vetoes back-number reading when the player faces the camera or when the torso is occluded; arm-sleeve numbers do not rescue the case because they are “usually too small to be recognizable” [PAGE 8]. The weather layer is not operative in this indoor NHL broadcast corpus: the source describes main-camera rink video [PAGE 2] and contains no weather variable. A weather veto can only be admitted if an external variable is shown to change optical transmission, lighting, or visibility; otherwise it is non-identifiable and must be rejected. The adjudication order is therefore: physical visibility veto \(\succ\) biomechanical orientation veto \(\succ\) model confidence. The model’s 87.01% [PAGE 23] is an average over admissible tracklets; it cannot override the layer that says the evidence is absent.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
