- **S4 — Protocol-shortcut bias.** [PAGE 5]: "we elected not to deﬂate the air bladders in our protocol"; "The style of helmets that we used does not allow for cheek-pad removal when worn by an athlete." Removing these steps biases time downward relative to full NATA-recommended sequences. The reported times are therefore a fast bound, not a typical case.
- **S5 — Ventilation-validity void.** [PAGE 1]: "The actual ability to effectively ventilate a patient with a helmet on and face mask removed was not studied and has not been established in the literature." No tidal volume, \(\mathrm{SpO_2}\), or \(\mathrm{ETCO_2}\) datum exists; the \(w_H\) term of \(\mathcal{J}\) is unparameterized by this source.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The causal arrow in this literature runs backward. The 2009 NATA position statement (Swartz et al., ref. 7) is cited for the recommendation that "the helmet and shoulder pads remain in place and airway access be achieved via face-mask removal" [PAGE 1]. But [PAGE 2] states the evidentiary status bluntly: "However, no researchers have published reports comparing helmet removal and face-mask removal to support this recommendation." The guideline preceded the comparative trial by six years. This 2015 paper is therefore retrospective ratification, not prospective justification — a textbook retrocausal genealogy in which the recommendation generated the experiment that was later said to have generated the recommendation.

The pre-2015 lineage is cadaveric and radiographic, not behavioral. Donaldson et al. 1998 (ref. 4), Palumbo et al. 1996 (ref. 5), and Swenson et al. 1997 (ref. 6) established that independent helmet removal displaces the cervical spine from neutral [PAGE 1: "isolated independent removal of football helmets has been shown to move the cervical spine out of neutral alignment"]. Ray et al. 2002 (ref. 15) quantified motion across three airway-exposure techniques. Swartz et al. 2005 (ref. 11) and 2010 (ref. 8), plus Toler et al. 2010 (ref. 9) and Copeland et al. 2007 (ref. 14), built the face-mask-removal time literature on partial quick-release and cordless-screwdriver systems. Dahl et al. 2009 (ref. 17) and Horodyski et al. 2009 (ref. 18) carried the question into unstable-cervical-spine cadaveric models and compared flat-torso versus elevated-torso pad removal. Burkey et al. 2011 (ref. 16) moved to manikin intubation. Poonnoose et al. 2002 (ref. 3) and Banerjee et al. 2004 (ref. 12) supply the clinical-mismanagement rationale.

Three multi-week traps are latent in this genealogy.

**Trap 1 — The 50-second anchor.** [PAGE 6] recalls that "Toler et al found that removal of a partial quick-release–style face mask took approximately 50 seconds, whereas Swartz et al noted that removal times were around 33 seconds with the same style." Clinicians trained on that anchor will treat a 31-second Riddell 360 face-mask removal as an improvement, when in this study it is the *slowest* condition and slower than helmet removal. Anchors from prior generations of equipment do not update automatically.

**Trap 2 — The device-name conflation.** "Quick-release" denotes at least three mechanically distinct things in this lineage: two-of-four attachment points [PAGE 2: "Previous systems incorporated quick-release face-mask attachments at 2 of 4 positions"]; a full push-button system (Riddell 360); and a quarter-turn screw system (ION 4D). The 10-second performance gap between the two full systems — 20.45 s versus 31.22 s [PAGE 4] — shows the label carries no predictive content. Any protocol keyed to "quick-release present" rather than to the specific mechanism is unsupported.

**Trap 3 — The flat-torso monoculture.** [PAGE 6]: "We used the ﬂat-torso technique for traditional shoulder-pad removal. Other methods for shoulder-pad removal exist and have been investigated, but we are unable to speculate on how the RipKord design might compare with those alternate techniques." The 29.22 s traditional-pad figure is therefore a single-technique estimate, and the 7.26 s RipKord advantage is a contrast against one comparator, not against the space of techniques.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three clocks run concurrently in this scenario, and the responder's behavior is governed by whichever clock is most salient.

**Clock A — the protocol clock, \(T_{\text{fail}} = 180\ \mathrm{s}\).** [PAGE 3]: "If the face mask was not placed on the X within 3 minutes of the trial start, the trial was stopped … and repeated." This is a hard goal-gradient boundary. Human performance under a visible countdown degrades in accuracy as the boundary approaches; the study's repeat-on-failure rule sterilizes exactly that degradation out of the dataset.

**Clock B — the acceptability clock, \(\tau^\ast \approx 30\ \mathrm{s}\).** [PAGE 5]: "access to the airway in a clinically acceptable time of around 30 seconds or less." This clock is not enforced, only cited. It is the clock that separates the four conditions into pass/fail under \(\mathcal{A}_{ij}\).

**Clock C — the desaturation clock, \(t_{1/2}\).** Unmeasured in this source. This is the only clock with physiologic consequences, and it is the only one the paper cannot parameterize.

**Crisis habit regression.** The deepest counterfactual in the paper is habit transfer from non-shouldered sports. [PAGE 2]: these recommendations "differ from protocols used by many providers for suspected spine injuries in patients wearing helmets without shoulder pads (eg, cycling, motorsports), when removal of the helmet is necessary to secure airway access and establish neutral cervical alignment." A responder whose dominant training set is cycling or motorsports has a well-rehearsed helmet-removal engram. Under time pressure — Clock A salient — that engram fires. In football equipment, that same engram is the one the cadaveric literature (refs. 4–6) identifies as the neutral-alignment violator. The regression is not a knowledge deficit; it is correct behavior applied to the wrong equipment class, and it is invisible to the responder because the motor program feels fluent.

**The counter-trap.** The obvious counter-trap is "always remove the face mask." The data partially contradict it. For Riddell 360, face-mask removal is the slowest condition and exceeds \(\tau^\ast\) on the upper bound. A responder who has internalized "face mask, always" will spend 31.22 s on a Riddell 360 when 28.82 s of helmet removal was available — and will do so while violating the motion objective. The correct policy is conditional on equipment, not on rule. A second counter-trap: the shoulder-pad result is a *time* win with a *difficulty* null, and RipKord's perceived-exertion mean is numerically higher (2.28 vs 2.13, t19 = 0.80, P = .44) [PAGE 6]. A responder expecting "the quick-release pads are easier" will experience mild disconfirmation and may attribute it to personal error rather than to the design.

**Clock arbitration.** When Clock A and Clock B conflict with Clock C, the correct arbitration is to default to the motion-minimizing action rather than the time-minimizing action, because the motion term in \(\mathcal{J}\) is bounded by an unmeasured \(E_{\text{crit}}\) (S1) while the time term is bounded by a measurable \(\tau^\ast\). Under S1 the neural weight \(w_N\) is unidentified, so an agent that optimizes time alone is making an implicit and unsupported assertion that \(w_N \approx 0\). The paper states this directly [PAGE 6]: "Our research methods limited our ability to establish a relationship between the magnitudes of the differences between conditions with an increased risk of iatrogenic injury."

**Perspective-taking layer.** The rescuer-2 role is defined by a handoff: rescuer 2 "assumed control of cervical stabilization of the head from the front with forearms resting on the model's chest between the open shoulder pads" [PAGE 3]. Every second rescuer 2 spends is a second the head is not stabilized by rescuer 1's hands. The shoulder-pad time saving (7.26 s) is therefore not free time; it is time during which the stabilization topology is degraded. The paper does not measure motion during this specific handoff interval, which is a genuine gap: the motion outcomes were windowed to the removal transition [PAGE 4], and the handoff interval may fall partly outside it.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
**Physical/mechanical veto — bladder valves.** [PAGE 5]: "not all valves for the helmet air bladders are accessible in a supine athlete." This is a hard veto on the deflate-first step: the maneuver is not a matter of skill but of geometry. The authors act on the veto: "we elected not to deﬂate the air bladders in our protocol." Consequence — the reported times are a lower bound relative to any protocol that insists on deflation.

**Physical/mechanical veto — cheek pads.** [PAGE 5]: "The style of helmets that we used does not allow for cheek-pad removal when worn by an athlete." Again a structural veto, not a training gap. The paper's explicit acknowledgment: "Eliminating these steps allowed helmet removal to be performed faster than it would have been if those steps had been performed."

**Biomechanical veto — equipment configuration determines the algorithm.** The NATA rule is conditional on shoulder pads being present. [PAGE 2] notes that for helmets worn without shoulder pads — cycling, motorsports — "removal of the helmet is necessary to secure airway access and establish neutral cervical alignment." So the biomechanical veto is not "helmet removal is dangerous" but "helmet removal *while pads remain* is dangerous." Any protocol that drops the pads-present condition commits a category error.

**Radiographic/biomechanical veto — helmet type is not neutral.** Table 3 [PAGE 5] shows a significant helmet-type main effect in the sagittal plane (F1,19 = 4.93, P = .04, ES 0.51) and a stronger one in the frontal plane (F1,19 = 8.45, P = .009, ES 0.66). Riddell 360 helmet removal produced 14.37° sagittal and 7.46° frontal versus ION 4D's 15.40° and 6.54°. The two helmet platforms load different planes. A single "football helmet" abstraction is vetoed by the data.

**Spatial/environmental veto — ambulance and ED clearance.** [PAGE 6]: "The ability to do this would become more important during transport in an ambulance or in a small emergency department space where clearance over the head may not be available." The traditional flat-torso removal requires pulling pads up and over the head; RipKord separates and slides them off the upper extremities [PAGE 3: "the shoulder pads were removed by pulling the left and right sides of the pads apart and sliding them down and off the upper extremities"]. In a low-clearance enclosure the traditional maneuver is physically unavailable regardless of the 29.22 vs 21.96 s contrast. Environmental constraint dominates the timing result.

**Accessory veto.** [PAGE 6]: "many football players wear other shoulder-pad accessories, such as back plates and rib pads. Researchers should study the effect that these might have on removing shoulder pads with a RipKord design." Back plates sit precisely over the cable path described in [PAGE 3] — "The RipKord system weaves a cable into the back of the shoulder pads" — so the accessory veto is plausibly a mechanism-level interference, not merely an unstudied correlate.

**Weather / field-condition veto.** Unmeasured in this source. Ambient temperature alters polymer compliance of quick-release buttons and cable grooves, and precipitation alters grip on a cordless screwdriver; neither was varied. Every trial occurred in "a controlled laboratory setting" [PAGE 1]. Under the audit rules, weather is an unadjudicated layer, and its omission means the reported times cannot be asserted to hold on a cold-weather sideline.

**Morphology veto.** Two models, both "healthy college-aged male volunteers … of similar stature" [PAGE 2]. No height or mass gradient. [PAGE 6] concedes the extrapolation is unknown. A 140 kg lineman with a short thick neck is outside the sampled manifold.

**Temporal veto — the truncation.** As derived in S3 above, the reported means are conditional on success within 180 s with unlimited retries. In an actual emergency there is exactly one attempt. The censoring is a layer that vetoes direct transfer of the point estimates to single-attempt field performance.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
**Veto Scenario 1 — Threshold-boundary falsification.**
*Claim under test:* face-mask removal is the universally preferred airway-access action for both helmet platforms.
*Source basis:* [PAGE 5]: "allows for access to the airway in a clinically acceptable time of around 30 seconds or less"; [PAGE 4]: Riddell 360 face-mask removal 31.22 s (27.52, 34.91) versus Riddell 360 helmet removal 28.82 s (25.64, 31.99).
*Prediction:* If the acceptability criterion \(\tau^\ast = 30\ \mathrm{s}\) is applied as a hard constraint to the upper 95% confidence limit via \(\mathcal{A}_{ij}\), the Riddell 360 face-mask condition is the *only* condition that fails on its point estimate, and it also fails on its lower limit (27.52 s is below 30 s, but the interval brackets 30 s while the point estimate exceeds it). Replicating with \(n = 20\) pairs and reporting the full empirical distribution of individual trial times, a material fraction of Riddell 360 face-mask trials will exceed 30 s, whereas ION 4D face-mask trials (UCL 22.12 s) will rarely do so. Veto fires if the Riddell 360 face-mask distribution has no mass above 30 s.

**Veto Scenario 2 — Ventilation decoupling.**
*Claim under test:* a faster removal time implies better airway control.
*Source basis:* [PAGE 1], Key Points: "The actual ability to effectively ventilate a patient with a helmet on and face mask removed was not studied and has not been established in the literature." [PAGE 6]: "researchers should continue to examine the effects of helmet designs on viable emergency airway support, as some investigators have explored inserting a pocket mask beneath the face mask and using airway adjuncts in a simulation-manikin research model."
*Prediction:* In a bag-valve-mask bench study on the same two helmet platforms with the face mask removed, delivered tidal volume will be governed by residual retention geometry (chin strap cut, side channels, jaw access) and will show no rank correlation with the 20.45 s versus 31.22 s removal-time ordering. Specifically, ION 4D should not outperform Riddell 360 on tidal volume merely because it is 10.77 s faster. Veto fires if tidal volume ranks identically to removal time across all four conditions.

**Veto Scenario 3 — Motion null under cadaveric instability.**
*Claim under test:* RipKord's 7.26 s time advantage justifies changing shoulder-pad practice.
*Source basis:* [PAGE 5]: RipKord 21.96 s versus traditional 29.22 s, t19 = 9.80, P < .001 — but sagittal t19 = 1.63, P = .12; frontal t19 = 0.80, P = .44; transverse t19 = 1.10, P = .29. And [PAGE 6]: "We used the ﬂat-torso technique for traditional shoulder-pad removal … we are unable to speculate on how the RipKord design might compare with those alternate techniques." Also [PAGE 6]: back plates and rib pads unstudied.
*Prediction:* In a cadaveric unstable-cervical-spine model following the Dahl (ref. 17) / Horodyski (ref. 18) protocol, intervertebral angular displacement will not differ between RipKord and traditional flat-torso removal beyond the model's resolution, while on-scene time will differ by approximately the observed 7.26 s. However, once a back plate is added over the RipKord cable path [PAGE 3: "weaves a cable into the back of the shoulder pads"], the time advantage will attenuate substantially. Veto fires if the cadaveric angular displacement differs significantly in the sagittal plane — which would mean the healthy-model null was a resolution artifact rather than a true null.

**Auxiliary falsifier (single-attempt censoring).** Not scored as one of the three, but recorded: because [PAGE 3] discards and repeats failed trials, the paper implies 100% eventual success. A first-attempt-only replication without retries should show a nonzero failure rate, and under the ordering of \(\mathcal{A}_{ij}\) it should be higher for Riddell 360 face-mask removal than for ION 4D face-mask removal. If first-attempt success is uniformly 100%, the censoring concern is retired.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
