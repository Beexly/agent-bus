James's argument sits inside the British empiricist tradition of simple versus complex ideas (Locke, Berkeley, Hume), but he reverses the standard associationist trajectory. Where the associationist claims that complex ideas are built from simple sensations, James claims that what appears simple to a child (lemonade flavor) is not retrospectively decomposable into the simple elements the adult later isolates [PAGE 1]. The lineage extends through Herbart's notion of "eufällige Ansichten"—accidental or contingent views of an object that are not elements of its composition—and through Meinong's theory of objects and relations, cited directly at Vierteljahrschrift für wiss. Phil., x. 324 [PAGE 1]. James's earlier denial that similarity rests on "an identical abstract element buried in all the similars" (pp. 492 ff.) [PAGE 1] anticipates later Gestalt critiques of elementalism and modern relational or structuralist accounts of perception.

The retro-causal structure is subtle: the adult's later analytical frame reaches backward and re-describes the child's earlier state as if it had always been compound, even though subjectively the two states are "two altogether distinct sorts of fact" [PAGE 1]. This is a retrospective re-description trap: the analyst projects the decomposed adult representation onto the past phenomenal token, violating the time-indexed identity of conscious states.

Multi-week traps in a longitudinal research program:
1. The decomposition reification trap. Over weeks of asking subjects to introspect increasingly complex stimuli (lemonade, green, chords), the experimenter may begin to treat the elicited components (sourness, coolness, hue) as if they were always present in the original experience, confusing the adult's analytical sigma-algebra with the child's initial acquaintance state [PAGE 1].
2. The neural multiplicity trap. As neurophysiological methods improve over weeks and reveal more receptors and afferent pathways, the researcher may infer that subjective simplicity is an illusion caused by ignorance of mechanism [PAGE 1]. James explicitly rejects this inference: multiple producing processes do not make the subjective fact compound [PAGE 1].
3. The conceptual contamination trap. Once a subject has learned the vocabulary of "blue," "yellow," and "green," their reports of green can no longer isolate the pre-conceptual simple quality; every subsequent introspection is "knowledge about" rather than mere acquaintance [PAGE 1]. A multi-week study must therefore counterbalance naive-first versus analytic-first cohorts, or accept that the first week's data are epistemically privileged for simplicity claims.
4. The similarity regress trap. Because James locates similarity outside any shared abstract element [PAGE 1], a research program that assumes a common feature space for green, blue, and yellow will mismodel similarity judgments as element overlap rather than relational confrontation.

These traps are "multi-week" because they emerge only as conceptual sophistication accrues across sessions; a single-session design cannot detect the retroactive contamination of early states by later analytical frames.

Scenario 1: Feature-decomposition agent on atomic sensory input. An RL agent is trained to decompose raw sensory streams into features and is then presented with a stimulus previously reported by human subjects as phenomenally simple (e.g., a pure spectral hue or the child's initial lemonade taste [PAGE 1]). Counterfactual query: "Was this sensation originally composite?" Verifiable veto: The agent must veto the counterfactual "the sensation was composite" if its evidence is only the existence of downstream feature detectors, because multiple producing processes do not entail a composite subjective fact [PAGE 1]. Verification metric: confusion matrix between agent's composite/not-composite judgment and human phenomenal reports, measured in bits of mutual information \(I(\text{judgment}; \text{report})\). Prediction: agents penalized for conflating neural multiplicity with phenomenal composition will show veto accuracy above chance; agents without this penalty will falsely veto simplicity in >60% of atomic cases.

Scenario 2: Longitudinal introspective state-transport. A subject tastes lemonade at \(t_0\) (naive) and \(t_1\) (analytically trained). Counterfactual: "The \(t_0\) simple state and the \(t_1\) analyzed state are the same subjective state." Verifiable veto: The system must veto identity of subjective state while allowing referential identity of the object \(L\) [PAGE 1]. Verification: collect introspective reports at both times; compute report-structural distance (e.g., number of named components, lexical diversity) and mutual information between reports across time. Prediction: \(I(\text{report}_{t_0}; \text{report}_{t_1})\) will be low for component structure but high for object reference; any model asserting state identity will fail out-of-sample prediction of the \(t_0\) report from \(t_1\) vocabulary.

Scenario 3: Similarity-element model of color. Subjects judge similarity among green, blue, and yellow under varying confrontation conditions [PAGE 1]. Counterfactual: "Similarity is explained by an identical abstract element shared by all colors." Verifiable veto: The system must veto any element-based similarity model because James denies an identical abstract element buried in all similars (p. 492 ff. [PAGE 1]) and treats similarity as relational confrontation [PAGE 1]. Verification: fit an element-overlap model versus a relational-context model to similarity judgments; compare out-of-sample log-likelihood and Bayesian information criterion. Prediction: the relational model will dominate when context is varied; the element model will show systematic residuals for green-blue-yellow triads, and the veto will trigger whenever the element model's residual error exceeds a threshold derived from the relational model's noise floor. All three scenarios are verifiable without betting lines, spreads, totals, or odds.
