<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The conceptual lineage of experimental extinction traces back to Ivan Pavlov’s meticulous dissections of digestion physiology in St. Petersburg (circa 1900), which inadvertently revealed the plasticity of neural control systems. Pavlov demonstrated that arbitrary stimuli could acquire causal power over autonomic functions through contingent pairing with biologically significant events. His later work on "higher nervous activity" identified the withdrawal of expected excitation as a specific form of cortical inhibition—not simply fatigue, but an active suppressive process. This insight prefigured modern predictive coding architectures by nearly half a century.

Edward Tolman’s purposive behaviorism further refined this by introducing the notion of "cognitive maps" where expectations serve as internal guides. For Tolman, extinction wasn’t merely the weakening of a bond but the revision of a hypothesis about environmental structure. This view anticipates contemporary reinforcement learning algorithms like Temporal Difference (TD) learning, where value estimates are updated based on discrepancies between predicted and received reward.

Historically, there’s been a persistent trap in interpreting extinction solely as forgetting. Early behaviorists sometimes conflated performance decrements with loss of knowledge. However, phenomena like spontaneous recovery—the reappearance of extinguished responses after a delay—and renewal effects (context-dependent relapse) reveal that the original association persists in a suppressed state. These findings have profound implications for understanding addictive behaviors, phobias, and PTSD, where therapeutic extinction protocols often fail due to the latent endurance of fear memories.

A critical multi-week trap emerges in variable-ratio reinforcement schedules (e.g., slot machines). Here, the unpredictability of reward maximizes resistance to extinction because the organism never experiences a clean, extended period of omission to build up strong inhibitory potential $I_t$. The intermittent nature ensures that some trials are always reinforced, maintaining a floor level of $\theta_t$. This creates a cognitive lock-in where the subject continues investing effort far beyond rational economic models would suggest—a vulnerability exploited extensively in gambling disorders and social media design patterns.

From a genealogical perspective, the principle of experimental extinction bridges classical conditioning with operant learning frameworks developed by B.F. Skinner. While Pavlov focused on reflexive outputs, Skinner analyzed voluntary actions shaped by consequences. Yet both converge on the same mathematical essence: behavioral adjustments driven by the gradient of prediction error. Modern neuroscience locates these mechanisms in dopaminergic midbrain circuits, where neurons fire in response to unexpected rewards and cease firing when expected rewards are omitted—exactly mirroring the sign change in our $(1 - r_t)$ term.
