The first hop is the identification of the core operator: SMOT converts a single-shot detector, specifically an SSD-style face or person detector, into an online multi-object tracker by making detection and tracking simultaneous. The paper states this directly: “SMOT adopts the recently proposed scheme of tracking by re-detection” [PAGE 1]. The second hop is to recognize why this is not a trivial replacement of one detector with another. Traditional tracking-by-detection separates detection, tracklet generation, and tracklet linking [PAGE 1]. SMOT collapses detection and short-term tracking into one stage and leaves only online tracklet linking as a separate stage [PAGE 1, PAGE 2]. This structural change matters because the tracker inherits temporal context at the level of anchor propagation rather than merely post-processing independent detections.

The third hop is to formalize the tracklet-generation state. For frame \(F_t\), the tracker maintains a set of active tracklets \(\{L_j\}\), each associated with a bounding box \(B_j^t\) and a detection confidence \(c_j^t\). The source defines the bounding box representation as “top-left corner \((x_i^t, y_i^t)\), height \(h_i^t\), and width \(w_i^t\)” [PAGE 5]. The redetection operator predicts a next-frame box \(\hat{B}_j^{t+1}\), selects tracking anchors near that predicted box, and aggregates anchor outputs into a redetection box and score. The source gives the redetection aggregation as an explicit formula on [PAGE 6]. The important gray-zone audit is that the formula assumes the selected anchors are close enough to the true object location that the detector’s regression can correct them. The paper supports this with a shift-robustness experiment, stating that “the detector can correctly localize the object position even with a relative shift ratio of 0.33” [PAGE 7]. That empirical breakdown point becomes a formal constraint on how much inter-frame motion can be tolerated before redetection becomes unreliable.

The fourth hop is motion assistance. The paper introduces a motion estimator \(H_{\text{motion}}\) using dense optical flow, defined as an area-normalized displacement over the pixels of the current bounding box: “\(\hat{B}_j^{t+1} = H_{\text{motion}}(B_j^t) = B_j^t \oplus \frac{\sum_{(h,w)\in B_j^t}\vec{d}(h,w)}{\text{area}(B_j^t)}\)” [PAGE 8]. This equation converts a pixel-wise flow field into a single center displacement for the bounding box. The assumption is that the average flow inside the box approximates the object’s rigid translation between frames. The breakdown regime is non-rigid motion, occlusion, flow discontinuities, or large pose change, where the average flow may not represent the true object displacement. The paper compares FarneBack flow and PWCNet flow, finding that more accurate flow improves tracking [PAGE 8, PAGE 12].

The fifth hop is online tracklet linking. Tracklets often terminate because of occlusion or temporary disappearance, so SMOT links short tracklets into long-term tracks using appearance embeddings. The source describes the linker as maintaining “a bank of the identity embeddings where each track has one feature” and matching a new tracklet to existing tracks or adding a new identity [PAGE 9]. This introduces a second gray-zone audit: appearance similarity can be insufficient under extreme pose, motion blur, compression artifacts, or shot changes. However, constant-time tracklet generation does not eliminate all computation: the linker extracts embeddings for newly created tracklets, and motion-assisted redetection adds optical-flow cost. These are amortized or conditional costs, not per-object detection costs, but they remain relevant in a complete latency budget.

The formal state variables of SMOT are frame-indexed and object-indexed. Let \(F_t\) denote frame \(t\). Let \(B_j^t\) denote the bounding box of tracked object \(j\) at time \(t\). In source notation, a bounding box contains top-left coordinates, height, and width: “top-left corner \((x_i^t, y_i^t)\), height \(h_i^t\), and width \(w_i^t\)” [PAGE 5]. Units are pixels for \(x\), \(y\), \(h\), and \(w\). The confidence score \(c_j^t\) is a detector output probability, so it is dimensionless and lies in \([0,1]\). The active-track threshold is denoted \(\sigma_{\text{active}}\) and is also dimensionless. The implementation sets detection threshold \(\sigma_{\text{det}} = 0.9\) and tracking threshold \(\sigma_{\text{tk}} = 0.4\) [PAGE 10]. The paper also uses a non-maximal suppression threshold of \(0.6\) and an IoU threshold between tracks and new detections of \(0.3\) [PAGE 10].

The redetection equation from the source is the core tracklet-generation operator. In the text, the detector produces anchor-level boxes \(\{B_{jk}^{t+1}\}\) and confidences \(\{c_{jk}^{t+1}\}\) for the tracking anchors associated with object \(j\) [PAGE 6]. The aggregated redetection box and score are written as weighted combinations over \(K\) anchors. A formal version, consistent with the paper’s intent, is:

\[
B_j^{t+1} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_{jk}^{t+1} B_{jk}^{t+1}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_{jk}^{t+1}},
\qquad
c_j^{t+1} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_{jk}^{t+1} c_{jk}^{t+1}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_{jk}^{t+1}}.
\]

Here \(B_j^{t+1}\) has pixel-valued coordinate, height, and width components. \(c_j^{t+1}\) is dimensionless. The weights \(w_{jk}^{t+1}\) are assigned by a tracking anchor assignment function \(A\), written in the source as \(w_{jk} = A(a_{jk}, \hat{B}_j)\) [PAGE 6]. If the assignment uses IoU, the weights are dimensionless intersection-over-union values in \([0,1]\). If learned weights are used, they must be normalized so the weighted average remains scale-consistent; the paper leaves learned weighting as future work [PAGE 7].

The acceptance rule is a hard probability threshold. The source says: “if \(c_j^{t+1} \geq \sigma_{\text{active}}\), this is a valid redetection” [PAGE 6]. If the score falls below the threshold, the tracklet is terminated and released [PAGE 6, PAGE 7]. This is the main termination operator:

\[
L_j \text{ continues at } t+1 \iff c_j^{t+1} \geq \sigma_{\text{active}}.
\]

The predicted next-frame box before redetection is generated by a motion operator \(H\). The identity version is \(\hat{B}_j^{t+1} = B_j^t\), which assumes negligible motion [PAGE 6]. The motion-assisted version is given on [PAGE 8]:

\[
\hat{B}_j^{t+1} = H_{\text{motion}}(B_j^t)
= B_j^t \oplus
\frac{\sum_{(h,w)\in B_j^t}\vec{d}(h,w)}
{\text{area}(B_j^t)}.
\]

Units: \(\vec{d}(h,w)\) is a two-element pixel displacement vector per frame, so its components are pixels per frame. \(\text{area}(B_j^t)\) is in square-pixels if the summation is over pixel coordinates, but in the equation it functions as a normalization over the box region; the resulting averaged displacement has units of pixels per frame. The operator \(\oplus\) shifts the center of the bounding box by that average displacement vector. The paper notes that dense optical flow provides “pixel-wise displacement prediction between two frames” [PAGE 8]. The two flow variants tested are FarneBack flow and PWCNet flow [PAGE 8]. The breakdown regime occurs when the average flow is not representative: non-rigid deformation, partial occlusion, background flow leakage inside the box, or flow-field singularities.

The anchor robustness condition is empirically formalized by the paper’s shift experiment. It states: “the detector can correctly localize the object position even with a relative shift ratio of 0.33” [PAGE 7]. A formal dimensionless normalized shift can be defined as

\[
\epsilon_j^t =
\frac{\|\bar{d}_j^t\|_2}
{\sqrt{\text{area}(B_j^t)}},
\]

where \(\bar{d}_j^t\) is the average inter-frame displacement of object \(j\) and \(\sqrt{\text{area}(B_j^t)}\) is a characteristic length scale of the bounding box in pixels. \(\epsilon_j^t\) is dimensionless. Under the paper’s empirical regime, redetection is expected to remain reliable when \(\epsilon_j^t\) is near or below the tested shift ratio \(0.33\) [PAGE 7]. If \(\epsilon_j^t\) greatly exceeds this regime, the tracking anchors may no longer lie close enough to the true object location for regression to recover it.

The linking stage uses appearance embeddings. Let \(\phi(S_i)\) be the identity embedding of track \(S_i\). The source says each track is represented by “an identity embedding \(\phi(S_i)\) produced by averaging the output of an embedding model \(\phi\) on objects in this track” [PAGE 9]. The embedding implementation is a “face embedding network with 128-dimensional feature vectors” [PAGE 10]. Thus \(\phi(\cdot) \in \mathbb{R}^{128}\). If embeddings are L2-normalized, cosine similarity or Euclidean distance is dimensionless. The face verification threshold is given as \(0.97\) [PAGE 10]. A linking decision can be written abstractly as:

\[
L_j \text{ links to } S_i
\iff
d(\phi(L_j), \phi(S_i)) \leq \tau_{\text{link}},
\]

where \(d(\cdot,\cdot)\) is the matching distance and \(\tau_{\text{link}}\) is the verification threshold, implemented as \(0.97\) for face verification in the paper [PAGE 10]. The linker solves a bipartite matching problem between new tracklets and existing inactive tracks [PAGE 9]. If no match is found, a new track identity is created [PAGE 9].

Breakdown regimes in source operators:
1. No tracking anchors near the predicted box: the weighted redetection sum becomes undefined or unstable.
2. \(c_j^{t+1} < \sigma_{\text{active}}\): the tracklet terminates [PAGE 6].
3. Large motion beyond anchor correction: the predicted box is too far from the object, violating the empirical \(0.33\) shift robustness regime [PAGE 7].
4. Optical flow failure: average displacement is wrong, especially under occlusion, shot changes, or non-rigid motion [PAGE 8, PAGE 11].
5. Appearance embedding mismatch: linking either fails to recover the true identity or creates a false link if embeddings are too similar [PAGE 9, PAGE 11].
6. Detection threshold too high: new objects are missed because \(\sigma_{\text{det}} = 0.9\) is strict [PAGE 10].
7. Tracking threshold too low: false redetections survive; too high: true tracklets terminate prematurely [PAGE 12].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of SMOT is a chain of detector and tracker design choices inherited from prior work. The paper situates itself against tracking-by-detection methods that use a “three-stage scheme (detection, tracklet generation and linking)” [PAGE 1]. It also identifies tracking by re-detection as the alternative lineage: “we propose the single-shot multi object tracker (SMOT), a new framework for multi object tracking … It is composed of two cascaded stages, the tracklet generation stage and the tracklet linking stage” [PAGE 2]. The direct ancestor of the re-detection stage is Tracktor++, referenced as [1]. The paper explains that in Tracktor++ “Faster R-CNN [28] is used and re-detection is implemented by passing the previous frame detection as additional tracking proposals for the next frame” [PAGE 2]. SMOT inherits the conceptual mechanism but changes the detector substrate: “we implement the idea of re-detection with single shot face (SSD) detectors” [PAGE 2].

A second lineage is the anchor-based detector tradition. SSD detectors do not have explicit proposal classification and regression stages like Faster R-CNN. The source states: “in SSD detectors, there is no proposal classification and regression stage as in two-stage detectors … where the proposals can be passed through” [PAGE 2, PAGE 3]. SMOT’s key contribution is to emulate proposal insertion by using anchors: “we can use a set of predefined anchors to approximate the rough locations of the tracked objects. We call this set of anchors the tracking anchors of an object” [PAGE 6]. This is a direct transfer of the SSD sliding-window proposal concept into a temporal tracking mechanism.

A third lineage is motion estimation. The paper says motion estimation between consecutive frames is “a widely investigated problem” and uses dense optical flow to build \(H\) [PAGE 8]. It evaluates both a traditional method, FarneBack flow, and a learned method, PWCNet flow [PAGE 8]. This lineage connects classical polynomial-expansion flow and modern pyramid-warping cost-volume networks. The retro-causal implication is that SMOT’s robustness depends not only on the tracker but also on the quality of the motion predictor. The paper confirms this: “the more accurate PWCNet leads to better tracking accuracy” [PAGE 12].

A fourth lineage is appearance-based re-identification. The linker uses an embedding model, specifically a face embedding network with 128-dimensional feature vectors [PAGE 10]. This connects SMOT to deep metric learning for face recognition and person re-identification. The paper also implements DeepSORT as a baseline and notes that DeepSORT performance is “highly dependent on the quality of its object detector and re-ID features” [PAGE 11]. This creates a multi-week trap: a tracker may appear robust because of strong detection, but fail when appearance embeddings are ambiguous or when detection quality degrades.

Historical traps are explicit in the paper. First, the detector trap: tracking-by-detection relies on the detector’s ability to handle “motion blurriness, large pose variations, and video compression artifacts” [PAGE 2]. Second, the missed-detection trap: “it is impossible to recover an object bounding box through temporal context once it is missed by the detector” in conventional tracking-by-detection [PAGE 2]. Third, the low-cost matching trap: “low-cost matching techniques (e.g. IoU matching) used in the tracklet generation process are prone to false matches, especially in occluded cases thus creating tracks with multi objects” [PAGE 2]. Fourth, the motion trap: the identity prediction assumption “has a strong assumption that the target does not have large motion” [PAGE 4]. Fifth, the shot-change trap: even with re-detection, “shot changes but new face appear at the same region, this leads to identity miss match in tracking” [PAGE 11].

The retro-causal genealogy therefore warns that SMOT should not be read as a universal replacement for all tracking pipelines. It is a targeted adaptation of re-detection to SSD-style detectors for low-latency face and person tracking. Its lineage explains both its strengths and its inherited failure modes: strong constant-time tracklet generation, reduced transfer count relative to some baselines, but continued sensitivity to motion, occlusion, and appearance ambiguity.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind analysis treats the tracker as an agent that must model three adversaries: detector uncertainty, motion uncertainty, and identity ambiguity. The first mental model is the crisis habit of detection tuning. The paper observes that “a higher detection threshold leads to fewer false positive detections and thus improves the precision rate, while the recall rates are less affected” [PAGE 12]. This encourages a habit of setting \(\sigma_{\text{det}} = 0.9\) to preserve precision. The counter-trap is that overly strict detection can miss heavily blurred or compressed faces, especially in low-resolution video. The clock constraint counters this habit: because SMOT is intended for online processing, the system cannot afford an unbounded cascade of delayed detectors. It must accept a thresholded detector and rely on temporal redetection to recover or reject candidates.

The second mental model is the habit of tracklet persistence. The tracking threshold \(\sigma_{\text{active}}\) determines whether a tracklet survives. The source says the confidence score is used “to kill potentially occluded tracks” and to update the anchor box [PAGE 4]. A tracker designer may habitually lower the threshold to avoid losing identity through occlusion. The counter-trap is that a low threshold admits false redetections and can create corrupted tracklets. The paper’s ablation supports tuning: “The number of transfers can be further reduced by tuning the tracking threshold \(\sigma_{\text{active}}\)” and recommends “the combination of 0.9 and 0.4 for the detection and tracking thresholds” [PAGE 12]. The clock says that each terminated tracklet becomes a fragmentation event that the linker must later repair, so premature termination has downstream latency and identity cost.

The third mental model is the habit of linking only newly formed tracklets. The source explains that the linker does not need to re-extract embeddings for already linked tracklets: “once a tracklet is linked we do not need to re-extract its embedding” [PAGE 9]. It also says appearance embedding extraction “do not need to be run on each frame, as in videos the appearance of objects usually change slowly” [PAGE 9]. This is an efficient counter-trap against per-frame embedding cost. However, the recursive failure mode is that if a tracklet is terminated and later reappears, the linker must match it to an older track whose stored embedding may be stale. The model of the adversary, namely pose change or motion blur, may not be captured by the stored average embedding. The clock forces a tradeoff: frequent embedding refresh improves identity fidelity but increases runtime; sparse embedding refresh preserves speed but increases identity risk.

The fourth mental model is the constant-runtime promise. The paper says SMOT is “able to generate tracklets with a constant per-frame runtime” [PAGE 1] and that the anchor-based regression is “agnostic to instance numbers” [PAGE 3]. This creates a habit of assuming that more objects do not increase cost. The counter-trap is that optical flow, NMS, and linking still have costs, and extreme crowding can increase anchor overlap, NMS ambiguity, and matching ambiguity. The clock verifies this by reporting FPS at multiple resolutions: “17, 22, 27, 40 frame per second” for \(1024, 768, 512, 256\) inputs on one NVIDIA V100 GPU [PAGE 13]. The runtime is fast but not free; it is resolution-dependent and module-dependent.

The final recursive object is identity itself. The linker assumes that appearance embedding similarity implies same identity. The paper’s shot-change example shows the adversarial case: a new face appears at the same region after a shot change, causing identity mismatch [PAGE 11]. The tracker must therefore model not only “what is visible now” but also “what was previously associated with this region.” The habit of using spatial continuity can be exploited by adversarial editing, such as cuts or crowd occlusions. The counter-trap is to require a high face-verification threshold, implemented as \(0.97\) [PAGE 10], and to use bipartite matching only when appearance evidence is strong. The clock demands that this matching occur online and cheaply, so the system cannot perform exhaustive long-term reasoning over all possible track histories.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto proof must adjudicate physical, biomechanical, and environmental-style nuisance layers that can invalidate SMOT’s operators. In this paper, the physical layer is the video formation process: frame exposure, motion blur, compression artifacts, pose variation, and shot changes. The paper explicitly lists these as video-specific nuances that challenge detectors: “motion blurriness, large pose variations, and video compression artifacts” [PAGE 2]. The biomechanical layer is the human face or person: facial pose, expression, singing, dancing, occlusion by other people, and body deformation. The Music Videos dataset is described as containing “edited scenes with singing and dancing movement” and “fast motion, shot changing and crowd occlusions” [PAGE 10]. The weather-like layer, although no meteorological variable is present, is analogous to environmental nuisance: illumination changes, outdoor/indoor shifts, and sensor noise. MOT17 is described as covering “indoor and outdoor person tracking cases” with “lots of person occlusion, fast motion” [PAGE 10].

Veto proof for motion layer:
Define the normalized inter-frame displacement

\[
\epsilon_j^t =
\frac{\|\bar{d}_j^t\|_2}
{\sqrt{\text{area}(B_j^t)}}.
\]

The source empirically establishes that the SSD regressor can correct anchor shifts up to a “relative shift ratio of 0.33” [PAGE 7]. If the true normalized displacement satisfies \(\epsilon_j^t \gg 0.33\) and the motion estimator fails to compensate, then the predicted box \(\hat{B}_j^{t+1}\) will be too far from the object. The selected tracking anchors will have low IoU with the true object. Their detector outputs will either be background-like or poorly regressed. Consequently, the aggregated confidence \(c_j^{t+1}\) will tend below \(\sigma_{\text{active}}\). Since the source says “if \(c_j^{t+1} < \sigma_{\text{active}}\), this is a failed redetection. The tracklet is considered terminated” [PAGE 6, PAGE 7], the motion layer vetoes continuation.

Veto proof for occlusion layer:
Occlusion reduces visible face evidence. The detector confidence is a probability-like score. If occlusion causes the anchor outputs to fall below the tracking threshold, then the redetection acceptance condition fails:

\[
c_j^{t+1} < \sigma_{\text{active}} = 0.4
\quad \Rightarrow \quad
\text{tracklet } L_j \text{ terminates}.
\]

This is directly aligned with the paper’s statement that the confidence score is used “to kill potentially occluded tracks” [PAGE 4]. The veto is not merely heuristic; it follows from the formal termination rule. The downstream consequence is fragmentation. If the object reappears later, identity recovery depends on the linker. If the appearance embedding is insufficient, the linker may either fail to reconnect the track or create a false identity link.

Veto proof for shot-change layer:
Shot changes violate the assumption of small continuous motion. The optical-flow operator assumes temporal correspondence between consecutive frames. If a cut occurs, the flow field may be meaningless or dominated by global editing motion. The source flags this as an identity risk: “shot changes but new face appear at the same region, this leads to identity miss match in tracking” [PAGE 11]. Formally, if \(F_{t+1}\) is not a temporal continuation of \(F_t\) but a new shot, then \(\vec{d}(h,w)\) does not represent object motion. The predicted box \(\hat{B}_j^{t+1}\) may be spatially plausible but semantically wrong. The redetection confidence may remain high if a different face occupies the same region. In that case, the physical layer does not veto redetection, but the identity layer must veto linking. The face verification threshold \(0.97\) is the formal veto gate [PAGE 10].

Veto proof for appearance-collision layer:
The linker uses appearance embedding distance. If two distinct identities have embedding distance below the verification threshold, the linker may merge them. The source’s transfer metric measures cases of “mis-matching multi objects into one track” [PAGE 3]. A formal veto condition for identity safety is:

\[
d(\phi(L_j), \phi(S_i)) > \tau_{\text{link}}
\quad \Rightarrow \quad
\text{do not link}.
\]

If this condition is violated because the embedding model maps different identities close together, the appearance layer vetoes correct identity preservation. This is a cross-layer failure: the visual layer may be physically valid, but the identity layer rejects it.

The adjudication conclusion is that SMOT is physically valid only inside a bounded regime: inter-frame motion must be small enough for anchor regression, flow estimation must approximate true object displacement, occlusion must not persist beyond the point where appearance re-identification can recover identity, and shot changes must not create embedding collisions. Outside those regimes, the formal operators either terminate tracklets or produce identity errors.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Fast-motion veto.
Counterfactual: If a tracked face moves between frames by more than the empirical anchor-correction regime, specifically if the normalized displacement \(\epsilon_j^t\) substantially exceeds \(0.33\), then the predicted box \(\hat{B}_j^{t+1}\) will not be close enough to the true face. The tracking anchors will have low IoU with the true object. The aggregated redetection confidence \(c_j^{t+1}\) will fall below \(\sigma_{\text{active}} = 0.4\). The tracklet will terminate.
Verifiable prediction: In an ablation where identity-only motion prediction is used, fast-motion sequences should show lower recall and more tracklet breaks than motion-assisted redetection. The paper’s results support this indirectly: SMOT with Farneback and PWCNet improves MOTA and reduces ID switches compared with basic SMOT on MOT17, Hannah, and MusicVideo [PAGE 12]. On MOT17, basic SMOT has MOTA \(0.529\), while SMOT with Farneback has \(0.554\) and SMOT with PWCNet has \(0.560\) [PAGE 12]. On Hannah, basic SMOT MOTA is \(0.609\), Farneback is \(0.726\), and PWCNet is \(0.716\) [PAGE 12].
Verification method: Inject controlled synthetic shifts into a benchmark, measure redetection confidence as a function of \(\epsilon_j^t\), and test the prediction that confidence collapses when \(\epsilon_j^t\) exceeds the source’s robust shift regime [PAGE 7].

Scenario 2: Occlusion-confidence veto.
Counterfactual: If a face is occluded such that the detector cannot produce anchor confidences above \(\sigma_{\text{active}}\), then the current tracklet must terminate. If the face reappears later, the linker may reconnect it only if the appearance embedding of the new tracklet is sufficiently close to an existing track embedding. If the embedding distance is greater than the verification threshold, the linker creates a new identity.
Verifiable prediction: Tracklets with long occlusion gaps should show two possible outcomes: successful identity recovery with low ID switch, or fragmentation with increased track count and possible identity transfer. The linker’s benefit should be visible when comparing tracklet generation alone with tracklet generation plus online linking. The paper reports that online linking changes Hannah performance from SMOT with Farneback MOTA \(0.726\), IDF1 \(0.580\), Transfer \(10\) to SMOT with online linking MOTA \(0.728\), IDF1 \(0.654\), Transfer \(165\) [PAGE 12]. This shows that linking can improve IDF1 and MOTA slightly but may also increase transfer counts under ambiguous appearance.
Verification method: Create occlusion-injected sequences, record gap length and embedding distance, then test whether reconnect probability decreases as gap length increases and as embedding distance increases. The formal veto is:

\[
d(\phi(L_j), \phi(S_i)) > 0.97
\quad \Rightarrow \quad
\text{new identity, not reconnected}.
\]

Scenario 3: Shot-change identity-collision veto.
Counterfactual: If a shot change places a new face in the same spatial region as a previously tracked face, the motion predictor may produce a spatially plausible but semantically false correspondence. If the new face’s embedding is similar to the old track embedding, the linker may incorrectly merge the new face into the old identity. If the embedding distance exceeds the threshold, the linker correctly creates a new track.
Verifiable prediction: Shot-change events should correlate with identity mismatch or transfer events unless the embedding threshold rejects the false match. The paper explicitly identifies this failure mode: “shot changes but new face appear at the same region, this leads to identity miss match in tracking” [PAGE 11]. On MusicVideo, where shot changing is a known challenge [PAGE 10], online linking improves IDF1 from \(0.120\) for SMOT with Farneback to \(0.244\) for SMOT with online linking, but Transfer increases from \(44\) to \(545\) [PAGE 12]. This suggests that linking can recover fragmented tracks but also may create identity transfers in edited video.
Verification method: Annotate shot boundaries, then measure whether track identity switches concentrate near shot boundaries. Test the counterfactual by increasing or decreasing the face verification threshold and observing whether Transfer and ID switch change in the predicted direction. The formal veto is:

\[
d(\phi(L_j), \phi(S_i)) > \tau_{\text{link}}
\quad \Rightarrow \quad
\text{reject false post-cut link}.
\]

These three RLVR-style counterfactual vetoes are verifiable because they rely on measurable variables present in the source: redetection confidence, tracking threshold, optical-flow displacement, appearance embedding distance, face verification threshold, and reported tracking metrics such as MOTA, IDF1, Transfer, and ID switch [PAGE 6, PAGE 8, PAGE 10, PAGE 12].
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
