<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The theoretical lineage runs from Hull's 1943 deductive behaviorism (*Principles of Behavior*) back through the delay-of-reinforcement literature associated with Wolfe and Perin, and forward to the Hull-Spence neobehaviorist tradition. Hull treats habit strength \(s_{HR}\) as an intervening variable inferred from performance, not observed directly ([PAGE 1]). The σ-index is a psychometric bridge between observable choice proportions and the latent \(s_{HR}\). Wolfe's empirical gradient and Perin's gradient are treated as empirical regularities that the formal equations must save.

A central multi-week trap is embedded in the pooling of days 7–10 ([PAGE 1]). By collapsing four days into one stable estimate, Hull assumes stationarity of habit strength across that window. Over a multi-week experimental trajectory, however, several retroactive processes contaminate this stationarity: spontaneous recovery, extinction during non-reinforced delay periods, and retroactive interference from later trials. Because the σ-values are drawn from late-session performance, they may already encode extinction effects that Hull acknowledges but does not fully model ([PAGE 1]: "appreciable amounts of extinction effects con-"). The delay gradient is therefore not a pure measure of reinforcement delay; it is a gradient confounded by session-order history.

A second retro-causal trap is the two-component decomposition ([PAGE 1]). By positing an unobserved "minor" slow learning process, Hull's system can fit almost any residual pattern. This is retro-causal in the sense that the minor process is postulated after the fact to explain deviations from the major negative growth curve. Without an independent variable that isolates the slow component (for instance, manipulating inter-trial interval over weeks while holding delay constant), the decomposition is immunized against falsification.

A third trap concerns Perin's sloping asymptote ([PAGE 1]). An asymptote that crosses the horizontal axis mathematically generates negative habit strength at sufficiently large N. If the experiment runs long enough, the model predicts values outside the meaningful range of the construct. This is a temporal boundary condition: the theory's validity window closes as N increases, yet the data table extends only to trials 7–10, offering no evidence about behavior near the crossover point N* = -b/m.

The multi-week vulnerability is therefore this: the experiment treats a short-window performance sample (days 7–10, N=60 reference point) as if it indexes a stable habit strength that can be projected across delays and across time. In reality, the organism's habit strength is a moving target shaped by extinction, satiation, and temporal expectation, none of which are fully separable from the delay variable in the reported design.

In Hullian terms, this is a crisis habit regression: the effective \(s_{HR}\) expressed in behavior drops not because the underlying habit is erased, but because the delay disrupts the response-reinforcement pairing. The observed decline in σ from 1.21 at 0" to .18 at 20' ([PAGE 1]) is consistent with such regression, but it is also consistent with pure decay; the data cannot distinguish regression of the response hierarchy from decay of the strength trace.

Layer 2 — Experimenter counter-trap. Fitting a two-component model to eight data points ([PAGE 1]) is a counter-trap: the experimenter invents a latent minor process to preserve the major negative growth function when the data resist a single-curve fit. The counter-trap is recursive because the experimenter's model of the subject's learning (two processes) is itself unobservable and is justified only by its fit to the very performance data it was designed to explain. The risk is circularity: the minor process is assumed because the fit is poor; the fit is deemed adequate because the minor process was added.

Layer 3 — Clock and temporal credit assignment. The delay intervals (0" to 20') are experimenter-clock durations ([PAGE 1]). The subject's internal clock need not align with seconds and minutes. If the subject encodes delay categorically (e.g., "short" vs "long") rather than on a continuous ratio scale, the σ-index will show step-like discontinuities rather than the smooth negative growth function fitted to the first four points. The apparent anomaly at 2.5' (.42 σ > .40 σ at 1') ([PAGE 1]) may reflect such categorical timing rather than measurement noise.

Recursive closure: The subject anticipates the delay schedule; the experimenter anticipates that anticipation by fitting correction curves; the tangent \(T_{60}\) at N=60 ([PAGE 1]) is a local slice that ignores this recursive anticipation. If the subject changes its timing strategy across days 7–10, the pooled σ-index is a mixture of regimes, violating the assumption that a single \(s_{HR}\) underlies all four days.

Physical layer. The dependent measure is "choice of the act which removes the manipulative bar" ([PAGE 1]). Bar removal requires force application, displacement, and motor execution. If the delay interval exceeds the animal's inter-response refractory period or induces muscular fatigue, the response topography changes across conditions. Veto condition: any delay condition in which the mean force or latency to bar removal differs by more than 15% from the 0" baseline invalidates the comparison, because the σ-index would then reflect motor capacity rather than habit strength.

Biomechanical layer. The reference point is N = 60 trials at 50% choice ([PAGE 1]). This presumes that the animal can emit 60 bar-press trials without satiation, dehydration, or locomotor degradation. Over days 7–10 ([PAGE 1]), cumulative feeding or water restriction alters motivation. Veto condition: if body weight deviates by more than 5% from free-feeding baseline, or if response rate declines within the 60-trial block, the 50% choice point is no longer a valid habit-strength anchor and all derived σ-values are vetoed.

Weather/environmental layer. The experiment is conducted in a controlled laboratory, but the source gives no environmental controls ([PAGE 1]). Temperature and humidity affect rodent motor performance and metabolic rate. Veto condition: if ambient temperature is not recorded and held constant within ±2°C across delay conditions, the thermal layer introduces an unmeasured confound that covaries with session order (and therefore with the pooled days 7–10). Absent environmental logs, the entire gradient must be treated as environmentally contaminated.

Cross-layer veto proof. A datum from [PAGE 1] survives only if all of the following hold simultaneously: (i) binomial independence and fixed n for the σ transformation; (ii) delay restricted to the fitted domain (0"–1' for the negative growth function) unless the two-component model is independently validated; (iii) N < N* = -b/m for Perin's gradient to avoid negative habit strength; (iv) T60 interpreted strictly as a local derivative, not as \(s_{HR}\); (v) bar-press biomechanics stable across delay; (vi) no satiation or fatigue across N=60 trials; (vii) environmental variables constant. Violation of any one clause voids the inference that delay per se causes the observed habit-strength gradient.
