<THINK_TRACE>
The source is a survey, not a single trained model. Its logical spine runs through a chain of operators: raw player and ball observations are encoded as trajectories and multi-agent relation sequences; those sequences are reduced or clustered with dynamical-systems operators such as dynamic mode decomposition (DMD) and Koopman spectral kernels; supervised heads classify or regress play outcomes; generative or planning-based simulators produce future and counterfactual behaviors; and practical applications inherit whichever assumptions survived the chain. The paper states the central difficulty directly: “The rules regarding the real-world biological multi-agent behaviors such as team sports are often largely unknown due to their inherently higher-order interactions, cognition, and body dynamics” [PAGE 1]. That sentence is not rhetorical. It defines the gray zone: any data-driven operator that suppresses higher-order interaction, cognition, or body dynamics may predict well while failing to explain or to simulate admissibly.

The first multi-hop derivation is the trajectory-to-relation hop. The paper defines a single-agent trajectory as a sequence of features and a multi-agent trajectory as a sequence over K agents [PAGE 2]. A relation sequence turns agent positions into pair relations: “Ri’s component Ri,k,l represents the relation between agents k and l at each i, as computed by Ri,k,l = h(pi,k, pi,l)” [PAGE 2]. The immediate assumption is that h is meaningful for the task. If h is a distance, the relation is in meters; if h is a Gaussian kernel, it is dimensionless and depends on a bandwidth in meters. That hop is not innocent: if agents are unlabeled, the relation tensor is not comparable across plays without a permutation rule. The paper later notes that “Since DMD is a dimensionality reduction method like PCA, the extracted dynamical property is permutation-invariant” [PAGE 4], which is a virtue for dynamics but not a solution for identity-dependent tactical roles.

The second hop is relation-to-spectrum. The paper says DMD “can decompose data into a small number of time dynamics (i.e., frequency and growth rate) and their coefficients” and that it is “based on the spectral theory of the Koopman operator” [PAGE 3]. The audit point is explicit: “Theoretically, to compute DMD, the data must be rich enough to approximate the eigenfunctions of the Koopman operator. However, in basic DMD algorithms that naively use the obtained data, the above assumption is not satisfied e.g., when the data dimension is too small to approximate the eigenfunctions” [PAGE 3]. Therefore, a DMD result is not automatically a discovered rule; it is an approximation whose validity depends on rank, sampling, and excitation. In a low-dimensional or repetitive dataset, the spectrum can be dominated by numerical artifacts or by the few modes the data can support.

The third hop is spectrum-to-decision. Clustering and classification can use DMD-derived similarities, but the paper repeatedly warns about interpretability and validation. Unsupervised methods “do not use objective variables (labeled data)” and are “sometimes difficult to validate them quantitatively” [PAGE 4]. Supervised methods can be evaluated, but they may be end-to-end black boxes. Counterfactual simulation is especially exposed: “the evaluation of counterfactual prediction (i.e., predicting ‘what if’ situations) can be used to validate the models [76, 19] based on the user’s knowledge, whereas there is no ground truth in a counterfactual situation” [PAGE 5]. That is the deepest gray zone: a simulator can be plausible, visually interpretable, and still unfalsifiable if the counterfactual has no measurable outcome.

A fourth hop is observation-to-decentralization. The paper states: “Most of these methods assume full observation to achieve long-term prediction in a centralized manner” [PAGE 5], and “Rigorously, decentralized modeling [19] is needed to enable computation of each agent observation (or contribution)” [PAGE 5]. Thus a model trained with global state is not automatically a model of an agent’s policy. If the intended application is a player-facing or coach-facing “what would this player see and do” question, full-observation leakage is a veto, not a minor implementation detail. The same applies to physical plausibility: the paper explicitly grounds skillful maneuvers in “cognition,” “force,” and “torque” measured by motion capture and force platforms [PAGE 2]. A generated trajectory that ignores those layers may be tactically interesting but biomechanically inadmissible. Weather is not modeled in the source; any weather-dependent claim is therefore an external layer that must be measured or explicitly declared out of scope.

The gray-zone audit therefore has four tests. First, is the relation operator identifiable under player permutation? Second, is the spectral operator supported by sufficiently rich data, or is it rank-starved? Third, is the evaluator using held-out outcomes, or only user intuition about a counterfactual? Fourth, does the generated behavior respect observation limits and body dynamics? If any answer is negative, the correct conclusion is not “the model is wrong everywhere,” but “the model’s claim is narrower than it appears.” That is the survey’s practical lesson, and it is why the paper balances interpretable feature extraction against black-box predictive performance rather than treating either as sufficient.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The paper’s formal data objects are stated in Section 2. For a single agent, a trajectory of length m is
\[
P=(p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_m),\qquad p_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
\]
where \(d\) is the feature dimension; if the feature is a spatial coordinate, \(p_i\) has unit meters, and if it is velocity, meters per second [PAGE 2]. For \(K\) agents,
\[
P_K=(p_{K,1},p_{K,2},\ldots,p_{K,m}),\qquad
p_{K,i}=[p_{i,1},p_{i,2},\ldots,p_{i,K}]\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times d}.
\]
The relation sequence is
\[
R_K=(R_1,R_2,\ldots,R_m),\qquad R_i\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K},
\]
with
\[
R_{i,k,l}=h(p_{i,k},p_{i,l}).
\]
The source specifies that \(h\) may be “a distance function [21, 9] or a Gaussian kernel [22, 23]” [PAGE 2]. A representative Gaussian form is
\[
k_{\sigma}(p,q)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\lVert p-q\rVert_2^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),
\]
which is dimensionless, with \(\sigma\) in meters. Assumptions: agents are identifiable or permutation-handled; sampling is regular enough for \(i\) to index time; and \(h\) is symmetric if the relation is treated as undirected. Breakdown regimes: if \(\sigma\to 0\), the kernel approaches a diagonal indicator and the Gram matrix becomes rank-collapsed; if \(\sigma\to\infty\), all agents become nearly equivalent and tactical contrast vanishes; if \(h\) is a raw distance, the relation scale is in meters and is not normalized across tracking systems.

For DMD, the paper does not print the algorithm’s matrix equations, but it states the operator’s role: DMD decomposes data into “frequency and growth rate” and coefficients, and is “based on the spectral theory of the Koopman operator” [PAGE 3]. A standard discrete-time formalization, used here only to make units and breakdowns explicit, is
\[
x_{t+1}=A x_t,\qquad A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},
\]
with eigenvalues
\[
\lambda_j=r_j e^{i\theta_j}.
\]
The discrete-time growth per step \(r_j\) is dimensionless; the continuous-time growth rate is
\[
g_j=\frac{\ln r_j}{\Delta t}\quad [\mathrm{s}^{-1}],
\]
and the frequency is
\[
\omega_j=\frac{\theta_j}{2\pi\Delta t}\quad [\mathrm{Hz}],
\]
where \(\Delta t\) is the sampling interval in seconds. The source’s own warning is the key assumption: “the data must be rich enough to approximate the eigenfunctions of the Koopman operator” [PAGE 3]. Breakdown regimes include rank deficiency, insufficient excitation, undersampling, and nonstationary dynamics that do not admit a fixed spectrum. The paper also states that the extracted dynamical property is “permutation-invariant” [PAGE 4], which is useful for dynamics but does not by itself solve role assignment.

For counterfactual simulation, the source treats a “what if” prediction as an operator over future trajectories, but it explicitly denies ground truth: “there is no ground truth in a counterfactual situation” [PAGE 5]. Formally, if \(s_t\) is the observed state and \(do(a_t=a')\) is an intervened action, the simulator produces
\[
\mathcal{S}:(s_t,do(a_t=a'))\mapsto
\hat{p}(s_{t+1:t+H}\mid s_t,do(a_t=a')),
\]
with probability values dimensionless in \([0,1]\). Assumptions: the intervention is well defined, the state is sufficient, and the training distribution covers analogous interventions. Breakdown: if the intervention is off-support, the simulator is extrapolating; if the evaluation is only visual, the output is not falsifiable. The paper’s practical application discussion reinforces this: short-term prediction can show “how will a certain move work for a player in the same situation as a good player” [PAGE 6], but that is a hypothesis-generation use, not proof of a causal rule.

Finally, biomechanical feasibility is not a free-floating metaphor in the source. The paper cites measurement systems that analyze maneuvers “in terms of their cognition [33, 34], force [35], and torque [36]” [PAGE 2]. A conservative admissibility set can be written as
\[
\mathcal{B}=\{x(\cdot):\ \tau(t)\in\mathcal{T}_{\max},\ 
F_g(t)\in\mathcal{F}_{\max},\ 
a(t)\in\mathcal{A}_{\max}\ \forall t\},
\]
where \(\tau\) is torque in \(\mathrm{N\,m}\), \(F_g\) is ground reaction force in \(\mathrm{N}\), and \(a\) is acceleration in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). The thresholds must come from measured or cited biomechanics; the source does not provide numeric thresholds, so inventing them would violate the source. The breakdown regime is any generated trajectory that requires torque or ground force outside the measured envelope, or that violates field and collision constraints. Weather is absent from the paper’s operators; a weather claim requires an external variable such as wind speed in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), precipitation in \(\mathrm{mm\,h^{-1}}\), and temperature in \(\mathrm{K}\), plus an explicit model. Without that layer, weather-dependent causal claims are unadjudicated.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage is visible in its references. The rule-based ancestor is the social-force model, cited as Helbing and Molnar’s “Social force model for pedestrian dynamics” [PAGE 6], and the paper applies social forces to a 3-vs-1 ball-possession task through Yokoyama et al. [PAGE 6]. The dynamical-systems ancestor is Koopman’s 1931 Hilbert-space transformation [PAGE 8], followed by DMD work by Rowley, Mezić, Bagheri, Schlatter, and Henningson and by Schmid [PAGE 8]. The kernel-learning branch is Kawahara’s “Dynamic mode decomposition with reproducing kernels for Koopman spectral analysis” [PAGE 8], and the sports-specific branch is Fujii, Inaba, and Kawahara’s “Koopman spectral kernels for comparing complex dynamics: Application to multiagent sport plays” [PAGE 7]. The survey then connects these to physically interpretable classification of network dynamics [PAGE 7], trajectory clustering with Fréchet distance and dynamic time warping [PAGE 8], Hungarian assignment for role permutation [PAGE 8], and graph neural networks that learn permutation-equivariant features [PAGE 5].

Retrocausally, the present survey is not simply “machine learning for sports.” It is a descendant of two older programs: physics-style interaction modeling and spectral decomposition of nonlinear dynamics. The social-force program supplies interpretable interaction terms but is too simple for general team behavior; the Koopman-DMD program supplies data-driven spectra but needs rich data and careful rank control. The paper’s synthesis is to use the spectral branch for interpretable dynamics and the supervised or generative branch for prediction and simulation. That genealogy explains why the paper keeps returning to “mathematical structure” and “visualization of learned representations” rather than prediction alone [PAGE 1].

The multi-week trap is longitudinal nonstationarity. A model trained on early-season plays and evaluated on later weeks can drift because tactics, lineups, roles, and tracking calibration change. The paper’s own practical discussion notes gaps “between the resolution of practical application and research on tactical planning in invasive sports” [PAGE 6], and it emphasizes that practical use may require “higher (almost perfect) performance” [PAGE 6]. A DMD spectrum estimated in week one is not guaranteed to be the same operator in week eight. A second trap is identity drift: the permutation problem described for trajectory comparison [PAGE 3-4] becomes a multi-week problem when players change roles or jersey assignments. A third trap is counterfactual overfitting: because there is no ground truth in a counterfactual situation [PAGE 5], a simulator can be tuned to a coach’s intuition and then fail on the next opponent. The safe genealogical inference is therefore narrow: these operators are best treated as hypothesis generators and representation extractors, not as eternal rules of play.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The source does not formalize recursive theory of mind, but it supplies three ingredients that make the distinction necessary: higher-order interaction and cognition [PAGE 1], emergency-dependent role switching, and counterfactual prediction. The paper reports that “the defender flexibly changes the frequency of the four roles (i.e., switching, overlapping, ignoring, global-help) according to the level of the emergency” [PAGE 2-3]. That is state-dependent policy, not proof of nested belief reasoning. A recursive model would need to distinguish at least three explanations for an apparent strategic shift.

First, crisis habit regression: under high emergency, an agent’s policy simplifies toward a lower-order habit, such as individual ball-chasing or immediate action, instead of maintaining team-coordinated role switching. The source’s emergency-dependent role frequency is consistent with this hypothesis, but it does not identify regression. A verifiable test is to label emergency states independently, then measure whether the entropy of the team-role distribution decreases and whether individual actions increase. If role entropy is defined as
\[
H(B_i)=-\sum_a B_i(a\mid s)\log_2 B_i(a\mid s)\quad [\mathrm{bits}],
\]
then crisis regression predicts a drop in \(H(B_i)\) under high emergency, while counter-trap reasoning predicts a more complex, opponent-conditioned change.

Second, counter-trap: the agent models the opponent’s model of the agent, and over-anticipates the opponent’s anticipation. The paper’s counterfactual warning is directly relevant: “there is no ground truth in a counterfactual situation” [PAGE 5]. A counter-trap explanation can always be told post hoc, so it must be tested with held-out strategic variation. A nested-belief notation would be \(B_i^{(0)}(a_{-i}\mid s)\) for an empirical opponent policy, \(B_i^{(1)}\) for the opponent’s policy, and \(B_i^{(2)}\) for the opponent’s belief about \(i\). Higher order is not automatically better; if the data contain no strategic variation, \(B_i^{(2)}\) is unidentifiable and will overfit.

Third, clock: the remaining time or possession clock is an observable state variable that changes the optimal policy. A policy can be written as \(\pi(a\mid s,\tau_t)\), where \(\tau_t\) is remaining time in seconds. If clock is omitted, a model may mislabel ordinary time-pressure adaptation as “crisis” or “counter-trap.” The paper’s emphasis on future prediction and long-term outcomes [PAGE 5-6] supports including clock and possession state explicitly. The adjudication rule is therefore: clock is exogenous and must be controlled; crisis regression is a pressure-induced simplification of policy; counter-trap is a recursive-belief hypothesis. Only the first two can be tested without a full opponent model, and the counter-trap claim requires out-of-sample strategic variation to avoid unfalsifiability.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical and biomechanical veto is the strongest layer because it is independent of tactical interpretation. The paper says general multi-agent modeling is difficult “due to their inherently higher-order social interactions, cognition, and body dynamics [8]” [PAGE 1], and it cites motion capture and force platforms for analyzing “cognition,” “force,” and “torque” [PAGE 2]. Therefore a generated or predicted trajectory is admissible only if it passes a biomechanical feasibility check. Symbolically, if \(x(t)\) is a proposed trajectory and \(\mathcal{B}\) is the measured feasible set of torque, ground reaction force, and acceleration, then
\[
x(\cdot)\notin\mathcal{B}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{veto the tactical claim}.
\]
This is not a claim that the source supplies numerical thresholds; it does not. The thresholds must be measured or cited from separate biomechanics. The veto proof is conditional: if a model requires a joint torque or ground reaction force outside the measured envelope, or an acceleration outside the tracking-supported envelope, then the model has generated an impossible body, and no amount of tactical reward can rescue it.

The observation-layer veto is similarly explicit. Most trajectory methods “assume full observation to achieve long-term prediction in a centralized manner” [PAGE 5], and the paper states that “decentralized modeling [19] is needed to enable computation of each agent observation (or contribution)” [PAGE 5]. If a claimed player-level policy uses future teammate positions or the global state, it is not a decentralized policy. The veto condition is: a model evaluated as an agent-local policy must be re-evaluated with all nonlocal information masked. If performance collapses, the original claim was a centralized simulator claim, not an agent-cognition claim.

The weather layer is not in the source. The paper’s data are player and ball trajectories and actions [PAGE 2]; it does not define wind, precipitation, temperature, or surface state. A weather-dependent claim therefore requires an external measured variable, for example wind velocity \(w(t)\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), precipitation rate in \(\mathrm{mm\,h^{-1}}\), and temperature in \(\mathrm{K}\). If those variables are absent, the correct adjudication is “unmodeled confound,” not “weather caused the behavior.” A conservative cross-layer rule is: physical and biomechanical feasibility vetoes override tactical reward; observation limits veto player-level cognitive claims; and absent weather data vetoes weather-causal claims. The source supports the first two layers directly and supports the third only by omission.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable reward checks can be constructed without any wagering or outcome-market claim.

Scenario 1: permutation-invariance veto for multi-agent dynamics. Take held-out relation sequences \(R_K\), apply a random permutation \(\pi\) to agent labels, and compute the DMD/Koopman spectral similarity between the original and permuted sequences. The source states that the DMD-extracted dynamical property is “permutation-invariant” [PAGE 4]. Prediction: a valid DMD-based similarity should remain invariant within numerical tolerance, and clustering assignments should not change. Veto condition: if the similarity changes materially or cluster membership flips, the pipeline is using identity labels rather than dynamics. Verifiable reward: 1 if the permutation test passes, 0 otherwise.

Scenario 2: decentralized-observation leakage veto. Train a trajectory predictor under the paper’s common full-observation regime [PAGE 5], then evaluate it with all agents except the target masked. The paper warns that “decentralized modeling [19] is needed to enable computation of each agent observation (or contribution)” [PAGE 5]. Prediction: a centralized model will show a large increase in average displacement error and final displacement error, measured in meters, when nonlocal information is masked; a properly decentralized or attention-based model will degrade less. Veto condition: if the model’s reported player-level performance depends on future or teammate-only state, the claim is vetoed as leakage.

Scenario 3: biomechanical-feasibility veto for counterfactual trajectories. Generate a counterfactual defensive or offensive trajectory from a sequence model, then run inverse dynamics or check against measured force-platform and torque envelopes. The source grounds skillful maneuvers in “force [35], and torque [36]” [PAGE 2], and it cites “Policy learning with partial observation and mechanical constraints for multi-person modeling” [PAGE 7]. Prediction: an unconstrained generative model will produce some trajectories that violate joint-torque or ground-reaction-force envelopes, while a mechanically constrained policy will reduce the violation rate. Veto condition: any generated trajectory requiring torque or ground force outside the measured feasible set is rejected before tactical evaluation. Verifiable reward: 1 if zero biomechanical violations occur on the held-out set, 0 if any violation occurs. These three checks are reproducible, source-grounded, and independent of subjective “looks plausible” judgments.
