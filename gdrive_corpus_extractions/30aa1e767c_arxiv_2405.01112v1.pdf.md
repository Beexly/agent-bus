The source describes a coupled perception-rendering pipeline rather than an isolated detector or pose estimator. The audit must therefore trace evidence across layers: multimodal sensing, bird’s-eye-view fusion, tracking association, trajectory recovery, voxelized pose estimation, unsupervised domain adaptation, avatar reconstruction, and virtual/augmented reality visualization. The paper states, “[PAGE 1] we present for the first time a comprehensive system for sports competition analysis and real-time visualization on VR/AR platforms,” and lists the operational chain as “data acquisition, multi-player tracking, pose estimation, player avator modeling, and actuation.” The strength of the system is that each layer supplies a missing constraint: cameras provide appearance, LiDAR provides metric 3D geometry, tracking provides identity and temporal continuity, pose estimation provides skeletal state, and avatar modeling provides a renderable body. The gray zone is that the same coupling creates failure cascades: a calibration error, an identity switch, a pose heatmap error, or a pseudo-label selection error can propagate into statistically plausible but physically false sports analytics.

At the sensing layer, the acquisition system is explicitly multimodal and multiview. The dataset uses “Livox Mid-100 LiDAR sensor and a FLIR camera,” with synchronization “∼5 ms accuracy” and recording at “a frame rate of 10 Hz” [PAGE 7]. The LiDAR density is “about 30,000 points per frame for each node” [PAGE 7]. These numbers matter because they define temporal and spatial resolution. At 10 Hz, the frame interval is Δt = 0.1 s. If a player reaches the reported top speed of 9.6 m/s [PAGE 8], the center-of-mass displacement between consecutive frames is 0.96 m. This is not a toy ratio; it is a direct kinematic consequence of frame rate and reported speed. It matters for tracking because the detector must re-localize a player almost one meter away between frames. It also matters for association because the geometry affinity must tolerate large inter-frame displacement while rejecting identity switches.

At the detection layer, the paper fuses point clouds and images in BEV. It says, “[PAGE 4] The multimodal feature map is obtained by combining the two modal-specific feature maps based on spatial position in bird’s eye view (BEV).” The geometric justification is stated as, “[PAGE 4] Assuming that the ground is horizontal, we can get the correspondence between the pixels of multiview images and the coordinates of ground point with the extrinsic and intrinsic parameters.” This assumption is powerful but fragile: if the court is not planar, if extrinsics drift, or if players are airborne, the projection mapping becomes biased. The detection head is described as “Single Shot Detector (SSD)” and the loss follows “SECOND” [PAGE 4]. The output is 3D bounding boxes with centers, dimensions, and rotation angle [PAGE 4]. Those boxes are the substrate for both tracking and later pose voxelization.

At the tracking layer, the paper’s central innovation is multimodal affinity. The final affinity matrix is “S = αSge + (1 −α)Sap” [PAGE 4], where geometry affinity uses 3D DIoU and appearance affinity uses cosine similarity of projected image features [PAGE 4]. The paper sets “α = 0.5” [PAGE 7]. This is a symmetric fusion choice, but it hides a deep assumption: geometry and appearance errors must be approximately balanced. If one modality is severely degraded, a fixed α may underweight the reliable modality. The paper partially addresses this by using 3D DIoU as the default when appearance is unreliable: “If the valid appearance features of the track is less than k or there is no valid feature for this detection, which means that the appearance information is not reliable, we use the 3D DIoU score as the default value” [PAGE 4]. That is a sensible fallback, but it is still a heuristic. It does not prove that the resulting identity continuity is globally optimal.

The regain module is the second tracking innovation. The paper says, “[PAGE 5] The regain score of inner tracks is defined by exploiting both geometry and appearance information: s = αG(d, g) + (1 −α)A(d, g).” The motivation is that players in basketball or football “won’t suddenly appear or disappear from inside the court” [PAGE 5]. The module keeps unmatched trajectories alive and uses geometric constraints such as entry/exit edges or expanding local neighborhoods [PAGE 5]. This is a strong inductive bias for sports: the court is bounded, and players move continuously. The bias can fail in dense scrums, referee occlusion, bench substitution, or broadcast cropping. The ablation supports the module: the paper reports that “our method with geometry constraints outperforms the baseline by 4.92% in HOTA” [PAGE 7], and the final multimodal method reaches “55.48” HOTA with “24” ID switches in the reported scenario [PAGE 7]. It also explicitly warns that “the MOTA metric is not suitable for evaluating long-term object tracking” [PAGE 7]. That warning is important because MOTA can remain high while identity continuity fails.

At the pose layer, the paper proposes “PointVoxel,” a top-down voxelized multimodal estimator. It creates a volumetric space “centered at the poincloud’s centroid whose size is consistent with detected bounding boxes’,” discretizes it into “X × Y × Z resolution,” fills occupied voxels with value 1, and extracts features with “V2V-Net” [PAGE 5]. RGB heatmaps are projected into the same volume, concatenated, and passed to a “Fusion-Net” [PAGE 5]. Joint coordinates are computed by “Soft-argmax” and trained with an L1 loss: “Lpose = ∑ ...” [PAGE 5]. The implementation uses “64×64×64 defined in a 2m×2m×2m space” [PAGE 7]. This yields a voxel edge of 2/64 = 0.03125 m, or 31.25 mm. That quantization scale is the spatial resolution floor for pose localization inside the volume. It is also the scale at which cross-sensor misalignment becomes dangerous. If the tracking box is off by one or two voxels, the pose network may estimate a skeleton shifted relative to the true player.

The unsupervised domain adaptation is the most delicate component. Because manual 3D annotation is expensive, the paper creates synthetic data with “SyncHuman” and then adapts to real data using pseudo labels. It uses off-the-shelf 2D pose as “pseudo 2D pose label” and defines “L2D” as the loss between projected 3D pose and pseudo 2D pose [PAGE 6]. It then uses entropy of the 3D heatmap as an uncertainty index: “we select the predicted pose ep whose entropy value is less than λ as the pseudo 3D pose label” [PAGE 6]. The gray-zone audit is severe: the entropy threshold can select poses that are locally confident but globally wrong. The paper itself admits that entropy-selected pseudo 3D pose loss “does not limit the rationality which means it may generate low-entropy invalid 3D poses” [PAGE 8]. Therefore, the human prior loss is not decorative; it is a constraint against anatomically implausible self-training.

At the avatar and visualization layer, the system reconstructs player meshes with “SelfRecon,” rigs them in “Unity,” and displays them through VR/AR devices such as “HoloLens” [PAGE 6][PAGE 9]. The tracking and pose outputs drive the avatars. The sports analytics outputs include “playing time, position distance, top speed, sprint distance, jogging time” [PAGE 8], and the paper defines sprint and jog thresholds: “[PAGE 9] a sprint is defined as a speed of more than 6 m/s and a jog as a speed of less than 1 m/s.” These thresholds turn geometric trajectories into athletic semantics. That translation is where physical plausibility becomes critical: if tracking jumps or pose errors create impossible speeds, the analytics become misleading even if the visualization looks smooth.

The overall audit conclusion is that the paper’s strongest evidence is at the system-integration level, not at universal metric optimality. Its multimodal tracking is clearly better than single-modality baselines in the reported HOTA ablation [PAGE 7]. Its pose method performs strongly in supervised and unsupervised settings on Panoptic and Player-Sync [PAGE 8]. But the evidence is bounded by dataset size, sensor configuration, synthetic-to-real transfer, and the absence of 3D pose ground truth on the BasketBall sequences [PAGE 8]. The most credible reading is that the method is promising for controlled basketball venues with calibrated LiDAR-camera rigs, but not yet a domain-independent sports analytics engine.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source provides several formal operators. I preserve their source notation, add units, assumptions, and breakdown regimes.

1. Geometry affinity: 3D DIoU. The paper defines
\[
G(d_t^i,g_t^i)=0.5\times\left(\mathrm{IoU}_{3D}-\frac{\rho^2(c_d,c_g)}{\rho^2(d,g)}+1\right)
\]
[PAGE 4]. Here \(d_t^i\) is a detection box and \(g_t^i\) is a track box; each box is represented by center, dimensions, and rotation angle [PAGE 4]. \(\rho(c_d,c_g)\) is the center-distance term, and \(\rho(d,g)\) is described as “the distance of their farthest corners” [PAGE 4]. Units are dimensionless. Assumption: boxes are valid 3D volumes with nonzero extent. Breakdown: if \(\rho(d,g)=0\), the penalty term is singular; if boxes are degenerate, IoU and DIoU become unstable. The score is bounded in the source description as ranging from zero to one after the penalty [PAGE 4].

2. Appearance affinity: cosine similarity. The paper defines
\[
A(d_{i,j}^t,g_{m,n}^t)=\frac{f_{i,j}^t\cdot f_{m,n}^t}{\|f_{i,j}^t\|\|f_{m,n}^t\|}
\]
[PAGE 4]. The features are appearance embeddings extracted from RoIAlign features, reduced to a 512-dimensional tensor [PAGE 4]. Units are dimensionless; range is \([-1,1]\) for cosine similarity. Assumption: feature norms are nonzero. Breakdown: if a patch is empty, fully occluded, or has zero norm, the similarity is undefined. The paper handles occlusion by checking 2D IoU and treating the farther object as occluded when IoU exceeds a threshold [PAGE 4].

3. Fused affinity. The final affinity is
\[
S=\alpha S_{ge}+(1-\alpha)S_{ap}
\]
[PAGE 4]. \(\alpha\) is dimensionless and weights geometry versus appearance. The paper uses \(\alpha=0.5\) as a general proportion [PAGE 7]. Breakdown: \(\alpha=1\) collapses to geometry only; \(\alpha=0\) collapses to appearance only. In dense crowds, geometry-only association can merge close players; in poor lighting or severe occlusion, appearance-only association can lose identity. The paper acknowledges that point-cloud-only systems are “prone to tracking errors due to poor detection performance for extremely close objects” [PAGE 2].

4. Trajectory regain score. For inner tracks,
\[
s=\alpha G(d,g)+(1-\alpha)A(d,g)
\]
[PAGE 5]. If constraints are not met, “The score is set to −1” [PAGE 5]. Units are dimensionless. Assumption: local object count is approximately constant, and players do not teleport inside the court [PAGE 5]. Breakdown: if a player is occluded for longer than the Kalman prediction horizon, or if a substitution occurs, the regain score may associate the wrong detection.

5. Pose heatmaps and joint coordinate extraction. The paper uses 3D heatmaps and “Soft-argmax” to calculate each joint’s 3D coordinate \(J_k\) [PAGE 5]. A standard soft-argmax operator over voxel centers \(x_i\) and normalized heatmap probabilities \(h_{k,i}\) is
\[
J_k=\frac{\sum_i h_{k,i}x_i}{\sum_i h_{k,i}}
\]
Assumption: \(h_{k,i}\ge 0\) and the heatmap represents a probability distribution. Units: meters if voxel centers are in meters. Breakdown: if the heatmap is multimodal, soft-argmax averages modes and places the joint between two plausible locations.

6. Supervised pose loss:
\[
L_{pose}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|J_k-J_k^*\|_1
\]
[PAGE 5]. Units: length units, typically meters or millimeters depending on label representation. \(K\) is the number of joints [PAGE 5]. Breakdown: L1 is robust but does not encode bone-length or joint-angle constraints directly.

7. Sensor scanning simulation. The synthetic LiDAR scan uses
\[
r=\alpha\times\cos\left(3.825\times(\theta_0+0.0017\times n)\right)
\]
with \(n\in[0,t\times10^5]\), \(t\) in seconds, \(\alpha\) the scanning radius in pixels, and \(\theta_0\) a random initial angle [PAGE 6]. Units: \(r\) is in pixel radius; \(t\) is seconds; \(n\) is a sample index. Assumption: the pattern approximates a Livox Mid-40 scan [PAGE 6]. Breakdown: if sampling is too sparse or aliasing occurs, the synthetic point cloud may miss thin limbs, causing domain mismatch.

8. Unsupervised 2D projection loss:
\[
L_{2D}=\sum_{v=1}^{V}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|P_v(J_{v,k})-\tilde{J}_{v,k}^{2D}\|_2^2
\]
[PAGE 6]. \(P_v\) is the projection function for view \(v\), \(V\) is the number of views, and \(\tilde{J}^{2D}\) is the pseudo 2D label [PAGE 6]. Units: squared pixel units if heatmaps are in pixel coordinates; normalized units if coordinates are normalized. Assumption: off-the-shelf 2D pose is sufficiently reliable. Breakdown: if the 2D estimator labels the wrong player or fails under occlusion, the projection loss supervises the wrong target.

9. Entropy uncertainty:
\[
H(h_k)=-\sum_i h_{k,i}\times\log h_{k,i}
\]
[PAGE 6]. \(h_k\) is described as “a spatial probability distribution” [PAGE 6]. Units: information units; bits if \(\log_2\), nats if natural log. The paper uses entropy as an “uncertainty index” [PAGE 6]. Breakdown: if \(h_{k,i}\) is not normalized, entropy is not comparable across joints or frames; if the network becomes overconfident, low entropy can be wrong.

10. Pseudo-3D loss and total unsupervised loss:
\[
L_{3D}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|J_k-\tilde{J}_k\|_1
\]
[PAGE 6],
\[
L_{unsup}=\omega_1L_{2D}+\omega_2\mathbf{1}(ep<\lambda)L_{3D}+\omega_3L_{prior}
\]
[PAGE 6]. The indicator means the pseudo-3D loss applies only when the predicted pose \(ep\) has entropy below threshold \(\lambda\) [PAGE 6]. The reported weights are \(\omega_1=0.02\), \(\omega_2=1\), \(\omega_3=10\), and \(\lambda=6\) [PAGE 7]. Units depend on the constituent losses; the weights are dimensionless. Breakdown: if \(\lambda\) is too permissive, invalid confident poses enter training; if \(\lambda\) is too strict, useful adaptation signal is lost.

11. Quantization and temporal resolution. The pose voxel grid is “64×64×64 defined in a 2m×2m×2m space” [PAGE 7]. Therefore voxel edge length is
\[
q=\frac{2\ \mathrm{m}}{64}=0.03125\ \mathrm{m}=31.25\ \mathrm{mm}.
\]
The frame rate is 10 Hz [PAGE 7], so \(\Delta t=0.1\ \mathrm{s}\). At the reported top speed of 9.6 m/s [PAGE 8], inter-frame displacement is
\[
d_{frame}=9.6\ \mathrm{m/s}\times0.1\ \mathrm{s}=0.96\ \mathrm{m},
\]
which equals approximately \(0.96/0.03125=30.72\) voxels. This is a derived breakdown metric: high-speed players move tens of voxels between frames, so temporal prediction and regain are not optional refinements.

12. Evaluation metrics. The paper reports HOTA, AssA, MOTA, and IDS [PAGE 7]. These are dimensionless percentages except IDS, which is a count. The paper states that “the MOTA metric is not suitable for evaluating long-term object tracking” [PAGE 7], making HOTA and AssA more relevant for identity continuity. Pose metrics are MPJPE and PA-MPJPE in millimeters [PAGE 8].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage of this system retro-causally explains why its design choices appear reasonable and why its traps recur. The paper is situated inside three converging histories: multiview sports tracking, multimodal 3D perception, and neural avatar rendering.

First, the tracking history moves from 2D image tracking to 3D geometry. The paper reviews “tracking-by-detection” methods such as DeepSort, which uses “Kalman filter” and “Hungarian algorithm,” and CenterTrack, which “directly regresses the offset” [PAGE 3]. It then contrasts camera-based surveillance with LiDAR-based autonomous-driving tracking, noting that LiDAR methods often “rely on 3D point clouds and lack sufficient appearance information” [PAGE 3]. This lineage creates the paper’s central counter-move: fuse LiDAR geometry with camera appearance. The claim “[PAGE 2] To our best knowledge, this is the first multi-player tracking research taking multiview RGB and point cloud as input” is a priority claim, but the more durable contribution is the engineering of a multimodal affinity matrix and a trajectory-regain module.

Second, the pose-estimation lineage moves from 2D lifting to multiview voxelization. The paper cites multiview datasets and voxel-based methods, noting that “voxel-based methods greatly improve the precision of 3D human pose estimation” [PAGE 3]. It also notes the limitation: voxel methods can be computationally expensive in large scenes [PAGE 3]. PointVoxel is a descendant of VoxelPose/PRN and V2V-Posenet. The source uses V2V-Net as the feature extractor and says the detailed design follows PRN [PAGE 7]. This genealogy explains the top-down design: first detect and localize each player, then estimate pose inside a local voxel volume. The trap is inherited: voxel methods depend on accurate person localization. If tracking boxes drift, the pose volume is centered on the wrong location.

Third, the domain-adaptation lineage reflects a broader shift from fully supervised 3D pose to weakly or unsupervised adaptation. The paper creates “SyncHuman” because “Manually annotating or capturing 3D poses for multiple individuals in large scenes is challenging, expensive, and hard to ensure generalizability” [PAGE 2]. It then uses pseudo 2D supervision, entropy-selected pseudo 3D supervision, and anatomical priors. The human prior loss is adapted from Bigalke et al., which the paper describes as penalizing “asymmetric limb lengths,” “implausible joint angles,” and “implausible bone lengths” [PAGE 6]. This is a retro-causal link to medical and anatomical domain adaptation: the constraints that help under-the-cover pose estimation are repurposed for sports. The multi-week trap is that anatomical priors may be too weak for extreme athletic poses, or too strong if they suppress valid but unusual basketball motions.

Fourth, the avatar-rendering lineage comes from film, gaming, and neural reconstruction. The paper uses SelfRecon for “high-precision reconstruction of players during competitions” and emphasizes “space-time coherent geometries of clothed human bodies from monocular videos” [PAGE 6]. The capture assumption is crucial: the method “starts with a monocular video that captures a self-rotating player” [PAGE 6]. This creates a production trap. If the athlete cannot rotate cleanly, if clothing deforms unpredictably, or if lighting is inconsistent, the avatar mesh may be incomplete. The avatar layer therefore depends on a controlled capture protocol that is separate from the live game perception pipeline.

Fifth, the VR/AR lineage explains the system’s public-facing motivation. The paper states that existing VR broadcasting “generally require a large array of cameras” and “high computational costs for rendering,” making real-time broadcast difficult [PAGE 2]. The proposed system tries to reduce cost by reconstructing players as rigged avatars rather than rendering dense camera arrays. The retro-causal design consequence is that the system prioritizes drivable low-cost avatars over photoreal volumetric capture. The trap is that the viewer experience can be immersive even when the underlying analytics contain identity or pose errors, because smooth avatars mask noisy labels.

The multi-week traps across this genealogy are: sensor calibration drift, synthetic-real domain shift, pseudo-label self-confirmation, occlusion-induced identity switch, avatar capture failure, and evaluation mismatch. The paper partially addresses these with geometry constraints, appearance memory, entropy thresholding, and human priors, but it does not eliminate them. The historical lineage suggests that the most durable contribution is the integration; the most fragile contribution is the self-training loop.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory of mind for this system means modeling the tracker as an agent that must infer hidden player states while simultaneously managing its own uncertainty, temporal deadline, and fallback habits. The hidden state includes player position, identity, pose, and motion continuity. The observations are images, point clouds, detections, and past memory-bank features. The paper’s design contains three recursive modes: crisis habit regression, counter-trap correction, and clock enforcement.

Crisis habit regression occurs when the system enters an ill-posed situation and falls back to a simpler policy. The most obvious crisis is occlusion. The paper says appearance features can be unreliable when projection causes occlusion, and it checks 2D IoU to decide whether a projected box is occluded [PAGE 4]. In that regime, the system regresses to geometry. It also explicitly defaults to 3D DIoU when appearance is not reliable: “[PAGE 4] we use the 3D DIoU score as the default value.” This is a habit regression: geometry becomes the fallback because it is less semantically ambiguous than appearance. The risk is that close players in a scrum produce nearly overlapping point clouds, so geometry itself becomes ambiguous. The paper acknowledges this: “[PAGE 2] LiDAR-based systems are prone to tracking errors due to poor detection performance for extremely close objects.”

Counter-trap correction is the system’s attempt to avoid the failure caused by that regression. The trajectory regain module is the counter-trap. It keeps unmatched trajectories alive and stores appearance features in a memory bank [PAGE 4][PAGE 5]. When a broken trajectory reappears, the system does not immediately create a new identity; it searches for a match under geometry constraints. The paper says, “[PAGE 5] Instead of deleting the potential broken trajectories, we keep tracking them and retain their appearance features in the memory bank.” This is a counter-trap because it resists the common tracker failure of treating re-detected objects as new identities. The ablation shows the value: the regain module reduces ID switches from 64 to 26 in the reported ablation, and adding geometry constraints reduces them further to 24 [PAGE 7]. The recursive logic is: if the tracker would normally forget, the regain module remembers; if the tracker would normally reassign, the module tests continuity before reassigning.

Clock enforcement is the real-time constraint. The system records at 10 Hz [PAGE 7], and the paper emphasizes “real-time performance” as a contribution [PAGE 2]. The clock matters because the tracker uses Kalman prediction and a feature memory bank over past frames [PAGE 4][PAGE 5]. If computation exceeds the frame interval, predictions become stale, and association must work with older state estimates. At high speed, clock delay is expensive: a 0.1 s frame interval already allows nearly one meter of movement at 9.6 m/s. If the system’s internal update latency is comparable to the frame interval, the tracker may associate detections based on outdated positions. The paper does not provide a full latency breakdown, so the real-time claim should be treated as plausible but not fully quantified.

The recursive theory of mind also applies to the pose estimator. In unsupervised adaptation, the model teaches itself using pseudo labels. The paper selects pseudo 3D poses when entropy is below threshold: “[PAGE 6] we select the predicted pose ep whose entropy value is less than λ as the pseudo 3D pose label.” This is a recursive loop: the model’s current confident predictions become future supervision. If the model is confidently wrong, the loop reinforces error. The human prior loss is the counter-trap: it penalizes asymmetric limb lengths, implausible joint angles, and implausible bone lengths [PAGE 6]. However, the prior only helps if implausible outputs are actually outside the learned prior. Basketball contains extreme poses: jumping, twisting, contesting shots, and falling. A prior tuned to ordinary upright human motion may falsely penalize valid athletic poses, or it may fail to penalize subtle self-training errors that remain anatomically possible.

The deepest recursive risk is metric-driven behavior. The paper emphasizes HOTA and AssA because they better reflect long-term tracking [PAGE 7]. If future system design over-optimizes HOTA, it may favor conservative identity preservation even when a new player actually entered. Conversely, if it over-optimizes detection recall, it may create false identities. The system must therefore balance detection sensitivity, association stability, and temporal continuity. This is not a one-shot inference problem; it is a closed-loop control problem where the estimator’s past decisions constrain its future evidence.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is a formal decision rule: if a lower-layer physical condition is violated, the higher-layer output should not be accepted as valid sports analytics. I adjudicate three veto layers: geometric-calibration veto, biomechanical veto, and environmental-sensing veto.

Geometric-calibration veto. The image-to-BEV projection assumes a calibrated camera and a ground plane. The paper says, “[PAGE 4] Assuming that the ground is horizontal, we can get the correspondence between the pixels of multiview images and the coordinates of ground point with the extrinsic and intrinsic parameters.” Let \(K_i\) be intrinsics, \((R_i,t_i)\) extrinsics, and \(\pi_i\) the projection from 3D ground coordinates to image pixels. If calibration error produces reprojection error \(\epsilon_{proj}\) greater than the pose voxel size \(q=31.25\) mm [PAGE 7], then image evidence can be placed in the wrong voxel. This is a veto condition:
\[
\epsilon_{proj} > q \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{reject fused BEV evidence as spatially valid.}
\]
The synchronization condition is also quantifiable. The sensors are synchronized with “∼5 ms accuracy” [PAGE 7]. At the reported top speed of 9.6 m/s [PAGE 8], a 5 ms temporal offset corresponds to
\[
9.6\ \mathrm{m/s}\times0.005\ \mathrm{s}=0.048\ \mathrm{m}=48\ \mathrm{mm}.
\]
This exceeds the 31.25 mm voxel size. Therefore, if cross-modal temporal misalignment approaches 5 ms during fast motion, the image and point cloud may disagree by more than one voxel. The veto threshold can be expressed as
\[
\epsilon_{sync} > \frac{q}{v_{max}} = \frac{0.03125}{9.6}\ \mathrm{s} \approx 3.26\ \mathrm{ms}.
\]
If synchronization error exceeds approximately 3.26 ms at maximal speed, geometric fusion should be vetoed for high-speed events unless additional motion compensation is used.

Biomechanical veto. The system converts joint positions into sports statistics, including speed, sprint distance, and jogging time [PAGE 8][PAGE 9]. Let \(p_i(t)\) be the tracked 3D position of player \(i\). Instantaneous speed is approximated over frame interval \(\Delta t=0.1\) s by
\[
v_i(t)=\frac{\|p_i(t+\Delta t)-p_i(t)\|}{\Delta t}.
\]
If this speed exceeds plausible human motion, or if acceleration implied between frames exceeds plausible human acceleration, the trajectory should be vetoed. The paper’s own reported top speed is 9.6 m/s [PAGE 8], and sprint is defined as more than 6 m/s [PAGE 9]. A basketball player can move rapidly, but an estimated speed of, say, 20 m/s over several frames would indicate identity switch, detection jump, or pose root error. The biomechanical veto can be expressed as:
\[
v_i(t) > v_{bio} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{reject trajectory segment.}
\]
The threshold \(v_{bio}\) should be sport-specific, but the existence of a threshold is justified by the physics of human locomotion. Similarly, bone-length constraints are enforced by the prior loss, which penalizes “implausible bone lengths” and “implausible joint angles” [PAGE 6]. If a pose estimate violates skeletal length or joint range beyond tolerance, it should be vetoed before being used to drive an avatar.

Environmental-sensing veto. The paper acknowledges that RGB cameras suffer from “poor lighting, limited ability to identify foreground, similar appearance, and inaccurate depth estimations” [PAGE 2]. LiDAR helps with metric depth but is sparse and can fail for very close objects [PAGE 2]. In outdoor scenario II, weather and illumination may degrade camera appearance. If camera exposure, glare, rain, or low light reduces appearance feature reliability, the appearance affinity should be downweighted or vetoed. The paper already includes an occlusion fallback [PAGE 4], but environmental degradation is broader than occlusion. If the image feature norm collapses or becomes noisy, cosine similarity may still be numerically valid but semantically unreliable. A veto rule is:
\[
\text{If } \mathrm{SNR}_{image} < \tau_{img} \text{ or point density } N_{LiDAR} < \tau_{pc}, \text{ reduce or reject the corresponding modality.}
\]
The reported nominal LiDAR density is “about 30,000 points per frame for each node” [PAGE 7]. If adverse weather reduces density below the level needed to populate a player’s voxel volume, pose estimation becomes underconstrained. The source notes that sparse point clouds alone are insufficient: “[PAGE 8] Livox point cloud information alone is unable to accurately extract 3D human skeleton due to its sparsity.”

Proof-style adjudication. The system’s valid output requires a conjunction of conditions: calibrated projection, temporal alignment, sufficient point density, reliable image features, and anatomical plausibility. If any condition fails, the higher-layer claim is not admissible. Therefore:
\[
\text{Valid sports analytics} \iff C_{cal}\land C_{sync}\land C_{image}\land C_{LiDAR}\land C_{bio}.
\]
This is not a guarantee of perfect accuracy; it is a admissibility proof. It says that outputs failing the physical conditions should be vetoed rather than smoothed into plausible-looking but false analytics. This adjudication is essential because the paper’s downstream uses include performance statistics and immersive visualization, where visual smoothness can mask incorrect inference.

For this audit, RLVR means a verifiable-reward protocol: an automated or human-verifiable check must confirm that a counterfactual policy or training signal is valid. Three veto scenarios are testable.

Veto Scenario 1: Calibration or synchronization drift.
Counterfactual condition: The extrinsic calibration or sensor synchronization drifts beyond the geometric tolerance. Using the derived threshold \(q/v_{max}\approx3.26\) ms at 9.6 m/s, a sync error above roughly 3.26 ms should trigger a veto for high-speed cross-modal fusion. A calibration error that produces BEV reprojection error greater than 31.25 mm should also trigger a veto.
Prediction: The fused tracker’s association quality degrades toward the weaker modality. In the paper’s ablation, point-cloud-only tracking reports HOTA 32.86 and appearance-only reports HOTA 29.42, while the full multimodal system reports HOTA 55.48 [PAGE 7]. If calibration fails, the full system should fall substantially below 55.48, with increased ID switches and lower AssA. Verifiable check: compare HOTA, AssA, and IDS before and after injecting a known extrinsic perturbation. If HOTA drops sharply and IDS rises, the veto is confirmed.

Veto Scenario 2: Entropy-selected pseudo-label collapse.
Counterfactual condition: The unsupervised adaptation loop selects confident but wrong 3D poses because entropy is low while anatomy is invalid. The paper admits that entropy-selected pseudo 3D pose loss “may generate low-entropy invalid 3D poses” [PAGE 8]. If the human prior loss is insufficient to reject those poses, self-training reinforces error.
Prediction: Unsupervised MPJPE or PA-MPJPE worsens after adding entropy-selected pseudo 3D supervision, or improves on training-like synthetic data but degrades on real out-of-distribution sequences. The paper reports unsupervised “Ours” MPJPE 22.00 and PA-MPJPE 15.96 on Panoptic, and MPJPE 72.92 and PA-MPJPE 62.72 on Player-Sync [PAGE 8]. A veto is triggered if entropy selection causes a measurable regression relative to the 2D-projection-only baseline, or if the selected pseudo poses violate bone-length constraints beyond a preset tolerance. Verifiable check: log accepted pseudo poses, compute bone-length error and joint-angle feasibility, and compare validation MPJPE with and without entropy selection.

Veto Scenario 3: Appearance degradation from weather, lighting, or occlusion.
Counterfactual condition: Camera images become unreliable due to low light, glare, rain, severe occlusion, or motion blur. The appearance affinity becomes noisy even if cosine similarity remains numerically defined.
Prediction: The system should shift toward geometry-only behavior. Because the paper reports that geometry-only tracking outperforms appearance-only tracking in HOTA, the geometry fallback may remain functional but less accurate than full multimodal fusion. If appearance degradation is severe, HOTA should approach the point-cloud-only regime rather than the multimodal regime. The reported multimodal HOTA gain over single-modal tracking is “more than 22.62%” [PAGE 7]. If degraded images remove that gain, the veto is confirmed. Verifiable check: measure image feature norm distribution, feature variance, and association entropy; if feature norms collapse or association variance spikes, reject appearance affinity and fall back to geometry with a flag.

These vetoes are not speculative; each maps to a source-documented failure mode. The calibration veto maps to the ground-plane projection assumption [PAGE 4]. The pseudo-label veto maps to entropy selection and the paper’s own caveat about low-entropy invalid poses [PAGE 6][PAGE 8]. The appearance-degradation veto maps to the paper’s statement that poor lighting and appearance similarity impair camera-based tracking [PAGE 2]. In an RLVR loop, these checks should be treated as hard constraints: if the check fails, the system should not emit high-confidence sports statistics, regardless of how visually plausible the rendered avatars appear.
