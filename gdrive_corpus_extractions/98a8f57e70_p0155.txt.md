<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The formalism presented in Halmos's page is deeply rooted in the early 20th-century shift from geometric intuition to axiomatic set theory. The lineage traces back to Émile Borel’s initial definitions of measurable sets on the real line, which were subsequently abstracted by Henri Lebesgue to accommodate integration. The transition to general abstract measurable spaces was solidified by Andrey Kolmogorov in the 1930s, who rigorously axiomatized probability theory using σ-algebras (or σ-rings, depending on whether the whole space is included). 
The concept of the Cartesian product of measure spaces was historically driven by the need to define joint distributions and multiple integrals in a coordinate-independent manner. Fichtenholz and Kantorovich worked extensively on function spaces where product structures naturally emerged. The explicit use of "measurable rectangles" as generators mirrors the historical development of topology, where product topologies are generated by cylinder sets. 
Historically, the "multi-week trap" associated with product spaces lies in confusing the Borel σ-algebra of a product topological space, `\mathcal{B}(X \times Y)`, with the product of the individual Borel σ-algebras, `\mathcal{B}(X) \times \mathcal{B}(Y)`. While they coincide for second-countable spaces (like Euclidean spaces), they diverge in more complex topological settings (e.g., uncountable discrete spaces). Halmos’s rigorous distinction between the two equivalent but initially separate definitions of a measurable rectangle `[PAGE 1]` is designed precisely to inoculate the reader against this classical trap, ensuring the measure theorist builds intuition from the purely set-theoretic algebraic generation rather than assuming topological coincidence.
