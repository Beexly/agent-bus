<THINK_TRACE>
The audit chain begins with the paper’s central causal claim: transcranial electrical stimulation (TES) is said to modulate cortical neural activity and/or excitability, enhance brain–nerve–muscle connections, and thereby improve motor performance [PAGE 1]. The paper then narrows to rock climbing psychological fatigue and proposes a “machine learning-based and transcranial electrical stimulation model” [PAGE 1]. A multi-hop reading separates four different claims that the prose often blends: (i) TES changes cortical excitability or oscillatory dynamics; (ii) those changes alter EEG motor-imagery classification accuracy; (iii) classification accuracy is a proxy for psychological recovery from climbing fatigue; and (iv) the intervention improves actual climbing performance. The source supports claim (ii) much more directly than claim (iv). The experimental endpoint is left- versus right-hand motor-imagery accuracy after tDCS or tACS, not time-to-failure on a wall, grip force, fall rate, route completion, or competition rank. The paper’s own Table 3 reports mean accuracy rising from 82.07% at baseline to 88.71% after tDCS and 87.07% after tACS, while pseudostimulation is 82.66% [PAGE 9]. That is a within-subject, single-blind design with ten climbers, one excluded for noise, leaving nine [PAGE 8]. The reported ANOVA is \(F(3,24)=10.436, p &lt; 0.05\), but the pairwise pattern is the critical gray zone: tDCS is significant versus prestimulation and pseudostimulation, while tACS is not significant versus either, despite individual responders [PAGE 10]. The phrase “tACS group compared to tDCS compared to the prestimulus group experiment, \(p_1=0.08 &gt; 0.05\) and \(p_2=0.002 &lt; 0.05\)” is grammatically and statistically tangled; the reader cannot reconstruct exactly which two groups produced which p-value without the raw data [PAGE 10]. That ambiguity matters because multiple pairwise tests without a stated correction inflate false-positive risk.

A second gray-zone audit concerns the “machine learning” language. The pipeline actually described is subject-specific band selection by \(r^2\), common spatial pattern (CSP) feature extraction, and linear discriminant analysis (LDA) classification [PAGE 5–PAGE 7]. Figure 2 shows a softmax/back-propagation schematic, but the methods section does not specify a trained deep network, layer sizes, regularization, or training/validation split for that network [PAGE 5]. The classification accuracy is therefore best interpreted as CSP+LDA decoding of motor imagery, not as evidence for a general psychological-fatigue model. The source’s own feature-extraction table admits CSP “Requires multiple leads for analysis and is susceptible to noise interference” [PAGE 6]. With nine analyzed subjects and high-dimensional EEG, covariance estimates and LDA scatter matrices are vulnerable to overfitting. The \(r^2\) equation’s denominator can approach zero when the pooled standard deviation of power spectra is small, making the band-selection statistic unstable [PAGE 5]. The CSP covariance trace denominator can approach zero for near-silent or badly scaled channels [PAGE 6]. The mixed covariance matrix is assumed positive definite [PAGE 7], but in practice rank deficiency, artifacts, or channel dropout can make the smallest eigenvalue near zero, causing the whitening operation to blow up numerically. LDA’s objective also has a singularity regime when \(\Sigma_0+\Sigma_1\) is singular or ill-conditioned [PAGE 7]. Thus the reported accuracy differences are not automatically transportable to climbing performance or to a new climber. The paper also states that tDCS after-effects last “not more than 10 min” and that 10 Hz, 1 mA tACS after-effects last “30min,” with at least 24 h between conditions [PAGE 8]. That washout is plausible for the stated after-effects, but the paper concedes tACS after-effects “has not been systematically studied” [PAGE 8]. A 24 h interval is a clock assumption, not a proven biological reset. Finally, the source’s rock-climbing psychology discussion is rich but mostly descriptive: fear is tied to perceived obstacle difficulty and trust in protection [PAGE 4], while fatigue is said to cause “inattention, irritability, and decrease in the level of will” and “move slowly, have stiff muscles, and panic in their hands and feet” [PAGE 4]. Those are exactly the states a cortical excitability intervention might influence, but they are also states that physical workload, weather, and biomechanical limits can override. The gray-zone verdict is therefore: the paper demonstrates a small, short-term, subject-variable EEG decoding effect, strongest for tDCS in this sample; it does not prove a durable psychological-fatigue cure or a rock-climbing performance effect.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s formal operators are band-selection statistics, covariance operators, spatial filtering, linear discriminant projection, and stimulation-dose parameters. Units must be kept explicit: EEG power spectral density in \(\mu\mathrm{V}^2/\mathrm{Hz}\), covariance and variance in \(\mu\mathrm{V}^2\), current in A, electrode area in \(\mathrm{m}^2\) or \(\mathrm{cm}^2\), current density in \(\mathrm{A}/\mathrm{m}^2\), frequency in Hz, time in s, and classification accuracy as a probability in \([0,1]\).

The paper’s subject-specific band statistic is given on [PAGE 5]:
\[
r^{2}=\frac{\sqrt{N_{1}N_{2}}}{N_{1}+N_{2}}
\left[
\frac{\mathrm{MEAN}(P_{1})-\mathrm{MEAN}(P_{2})}
{\mathrm{STD}(P_{1}\cup P_{2})}
\right]^{2}.
\]
Here \(N_1,N_2\) are trial counts and \(P_1,P_2\) are power spectra. The ratio is dimensionless if \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) share units. The source says: “The larger the value of r2, the greater the energy diﬀerence between the EEG data of left- and right-handed motor imagery tasks in that frequency band” [PAGE 5]. Assumptions: two task classes, comparable spectra, and nonzero pooled standard deviation. Breakdown regimes: \(N_1+N_2=0\); one class absent; \(P_1=P_2\) gives zero numerator; \(\mathrm{STD}(P_1\cup P_2)\to 0\) gives a singularity or explosive statistic; severe artifact can make the selected band unstable.

The CSP covariance operators are on [PAGE 6]:
\[
R_{1}=\frac{X_{1}X_{1}^{\mathsf T}}{\mathrm{tr}(X_{1}X_{1}^{\mathsf T})},
\qquad
R_{2}=\frac{X_{2}X_{2}^{\mathsf T}}{\mathrm{tr}(X_{2}X_{2}^{\mathsf T})},
\]
with \(X_i\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times T}\), \(N\) channels, \(T\) samples. The paper states: “N must be less than T to satisfy the condition of calculating the covariance matrix” [PAGE 6]. The averaged covariances are \(\bar R_1=(1/n_1)\sum_i R_{1i}\), \(\bar R_2=(1/n_2)\sum_i R_{2i}\), and the mixed covariance is \(R=\bar R_1+\bar R_2\) [PAGE 6]. Then \(R=U\Lambda U^{\mathsf T}\), and the whitening matrix is written as \(P=\Lambda^{-1/2}U^{\mathsf T}\) in the paper’s notation [PAGE 7]. The source justifies: “Since the obtained mixed-space covariance matrix R is a positive deﬁnite matrix, the eigenvalue decomposition of the obtained mixed-space covariance matrix R is performed according to the singular value theorem as follows” [PAGE 7]. The CSP feature is
\[
Z_i=WX_i,
\qquad
f_i=\frac{\operatorname{var}(Z_i)}{\sum_j \operatorname{var}(Z_j)},
\]
which is dimensionless and sums to one over retained components [PAGE 7]. Breakdown regimes: \(\mathrm{tr}(X_iX_i^{\mathsf T})\to 0\); rank-deficient \(R\); \(\lambda_{\min}\le 0\) or \(\lambda_{\min}\to 0\), making \(\Lambda^{-1/2}\) unbounded; \(N\ge T\); channel dropout; artifact-driven covariance mismatch.

The LDA objective is on [PAGE 7]:
\[
w^{*}=\arg\max_{w}
\frac{w^{\mathsf T}(\mu_0-\mu_1)(\mu_0-\mu_1)^{\mathsf T}w}
{w^{\mathsf T}(\Sigma_0+\Sigma_1)w}.
\]
The numerator is a squared projected class separation, and the denominator is within-class projected scatter. Both are \(\mu\mathrm{V}^2\)-scaled before normalization; the ratio is dimensionless. Assumptions: two classes, Gaussian-like conditional distributions, shared or pooled covariance approximation, and nonsingular \(\Sigma_0+\Sigma_1\). Breakdown regimes: singular within-class scatter, small \(n\), class imbalance, outliers, and overfitting when feature dimension is large relative to trials. The source’s qualitative warning is that CSP is “susceptible to noise interference” [PAGE 6].

The stimulation dose parameters are on [PAGE 8]: tDCS at \(1\,\mathrm{mA}\), tACS at \(10\,\mathrm{Hz}\), both for \(10\,\mathrm{min}\); electrodes are \(5\times 7\,\mathrm{cm}\) saline-soaked sponges. With \(A=35\,\mathrm{cm}^2=3.5\times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}^2\) and \(I=1\times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{A}\),
\[
J=\frac{I}{A}
=\frac{1\times 10^{-3}}{3.5\times 10^{-3}}
=0.286\,\mathrm{A}/\mathrm{m}^2
=28.6\,\mu\mathrm{A}/\mathrm{cm}^2.
\]
This is a dose descriptor, not proof of efficacy. The after-effect clock is explicit: “The duration of the after-eﬀects of transcranial direct current stimulation is not more than 10 min, and the duration of the after-eﬀects of transcranial alternating current stimulation has not been systematically studied, but the duration of the after-eﬀects of 10 Hz, 1 mA alternating current stimulation, is 30min; so, the interval between each stimulation is guaranteed to be more than 24 h to avoid the after-eﬀects from aﬀecting the results of the subsequent experiments” [PAGE 8]. The statistical operator is the repeated-measures ANOVA: “F ð3, 24Þ = 10:436, p &lt; 0:05, indicating a signiﬁcant main eﬀect” [PAGE 10]. For binary decoding, accuracy is
\[
\hat a=\frac{n_{\mathrm{correct}}}{N_{\mathrm{trials}}},
\qquad
\mathrm{SE}(\hat a)=\sqrt{\frac{\hat a(1-\hat a)}{N_{\mathrm{trials}}}},
\]
in probability units. If balanced binary classes and symmetric errors are assumed, information transfer is
\[
I(X;Y)=H(Y)-H(Y\mid X)=1-H_b(\hat a)\ \mathrm{bits},
\]
where \(H_b\) is binary entropy. Breakdown regimes: \(N_{\mathrm{trials}}\) small; accuracy at 0 or 1; class imbalance; multiple pairwise comparisons without correction; and interpreting EEG-decoding bits as climbing-performance bits.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is retrocausal in the sense that the later sports-performance claim reads back into earlier clinical TES work and makes it look like a natural performance pipeline. The source itself traces the movement: TES is first described as a noninvasive brain stimulation method with tDCS, tACS, and transcranial random noise stimulation, transitioning “from laboratory research to applied research in sports science” [PAGE 1]. It then lists clinical rehabilitation and psychiatric use before sports use: “Cur-rently, TES is more frequently used in the rehabilitation of patients with neurological impairment or psychiatric disorders” and “In recent years, some researchers have gradually applied TES to the ﬁeld of sports science” [PAGE 3]. The scientific genealogy is therefore clinical neuromodulation → cortical excitability and plasticity models → motor-imagery EEG decoding → sports-psychology application. The paper’s mechanistic lineage includes changing cortical excitability, increasing synaptic plasticity, altering local cerebral blood flow, and modulating local cortical and brain-network connectivity [PAGE 3]. The decoding lineage is EEG event-related desynchronization/synchronization → CSP spatial filtering → LDA classification [PAGE 4–PAGE 7]. The rock-climbing lineage is mountaineering psychological training → attention, arousal, representational training, and fear management [PAGE 2–PAGE 4]. 

The multi-week traps are numerous. First, the after-effect clock is short: tDCS after-effects are stated as “not more than 10 min,” tACS at 10 Hz and 1 mA as “30min,” and the design uses “more than 24 h” between conditions [PAGE 8]. That protects against acute carryover but does not establish multi-week durability. Second, the design is “a single-blind experiment” [PAGE 8], so demand characteristics and expectancy are not fully controlled. Third, the sample is ten climbers, one excluded, leaving nine [PAGE 8]; small-sample responder patterns can look like trait effects and disappear on replication. Fourth, the paper’s own individual data show tACS decreases in Subject 5 and Subject 8, while Subject 1 reaches 98.75% after tACS [PAGE 9–PAGE 10]. A responder genealogy must explain both directions, not just the highest value. Fifth, the psychological-training schedule is not a single session: the source says preparation for specific competitions “generally in the two or three months before the competition to start practicing” [PAGE 2]. Any claim that a 10 min stimulation session replaces that multi-week training is genealogically unsupported. Sixth, the statistical genealogy includes multiple pairwise tests after a significant omnibus test, with an explicitly ambiguous sentence around \(p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4\) [PAGE 10]. That is a classic multi-week trap: a single significant contrast gets remembered as the whole result, while the null tACS contrasts and individual regressions are forgotten. Seventh, publication and citation drift can turn “tDCS improved motor-imagery accuracy in nine climbers” into “TES improves rock-climbing performance,” which the source does not directly test. The correct retrocausal reading is therefore narrower: the paper is a bridge study between clinical TES and sports psychology, not a completed causal genealogy from cortical current to podium performance.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Under crisis, the climber’s recursive model is not a simple fatigue meter. The athlete represents the wall, the protection, the coach’s expectations, the competitor’s position, and the self as an object being evaluated. The source describes the failure cascade: “due to the rapid consumption of physical energy, it often causes a rapid decline in the athletes’ functional reserve and central nervous fatigue, which leads to a series of bad emotions such as inattention, irritability, and decrease in the level of will” [PAGE 4]. It then adds that worsening emotion makes athletes “move slowly, have stiﬀmuscles, and panic in their hands and feet” [PAGE 4]. That is crisis habit regression: the dominant overlearned policy — overgrip, breath-hold, look down, rush the clip, freeze before a dynamic move — reasserts itself because the metacognitive controller is overloaded. A TES-induced shift in cortical excitability may raise the gain on motor imagery, but it does not automatically rewrite the athlete’s valuation of risk or the habit policy.

The counter-trap is the athlete’s attempted correction that becomes a new failure mode. If the climber tells himself “do not overgrip,” he may undergrip and slip; if he tells himself “relax,” he may lose body tension; if he tells himself “do not look down,” he may become rigid and miss the foot placement. The source’s fear analysis supports this: fear is tied to “the climber’s understanding of the degree of obstacles and the climber’s level of trust in protective events and protection personnel” [PAGE 4]. A counter-trap is therefore a belief-level intervention that changes the behavioral policy in the wrong direction. The recursive model can be written in probability units as a belief update,
\[
P(h_t\mid o_{1:t})\propto
P(o_t\mid h_t)\sum_{h_{t-1}}P(h_t\mid h_{t-1})P(h_{t-1}\mid o_{1:t-1}),
\]
where \(h_t\) is the hidden load/fear state and \(o_t\) is interoceptive and visual evidence. The equation is not a toy ratio; it states the assumption that the climber integrates evidence over time. If the evidence is “hands sweating, forearm burning, protection far below,” the posterior shifts toward threat, and the habit policy takes over.

The clock is the third control layer. The source’s stopwatch training asks athletes to focus on the rotation of a stopwatch and record attentiveness time [PAGE 4]; the competition-preparation clock is two to three months [PAGE 2]; the stimulation after-effect clock is 10 min for tDCS and 30 min for 10 Hz, 1 mA tACS [PAGE 8]. These clocks are not interchangeable. A 10 min excitability shift cannot substitute for a multi-week psychological-training block, and a 30 min tACS after-effect cannot be assumed to cover a competition day. The recursive theory-of-mind failure is when the athlete, coach, or researcher collapses all three clocks into one: “I felt different after stimulation” becomes “I am ready for the competition.” The source’s own emphasis on self-regulation, arousal regulation, and representational training [PAGE 4] implies that the durable intervention is metacognitive rehearsal, not a single current dose. TES may be a transient gain modulator, but crisis habit regression, counter-trap correction, and clock mismatch remain psychological and ecological problems.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical and biomechanical vetoes outrank neurocognitive modulation. The source itself says the wall’s “ever-changing weather are all huge obstacles to climbing” and that “If you make a mistake, you may have the danger of slipping and falling” [PAGE 2]. The contact-mechanics veto is standard: a foot or hand remains on a hold only if the required tangential force does not exceed available friction,
\[
F_t\le \mu_s F_n,
\]
where \(F_t,F_n\) are in N and \(\mu_s\) is dimensionless. If rain, humidity, chalk loss, or polished rock reduces \(\mu_s\), then even a perfectly excited motor cortex cannot create adhesion. The climber slips when \(F_t&gt;\mu_sF_n\). This is a hard veto on any claim that TES directly improves climbing performance in wet or contaminated conditions. The source’s “ever-changing weather” phrase [PAGE 2] is thus not decorative; it is a boundary condition.

The biomechanical veto is tissue-level. Finger flexor tendon, pulley, and muscle load can be represented as
\[
\sigma=\frac{F}{A}\le \sigma_{\mathrm{fail}},
\]
with \(\sigma\) in Pa, \(F\) in N, and \(A\) in \(\mathrm{m}^2\). The source notes “rapid consumption of physical energy” and “central nervous fatigue” [PAGE 4], but fatigue also changes motor coordination and load distribution. If a climber’s required finger force exceeds tissue tolerance, or if fatigue causes a sudden load spike, cortical stimulation cannot prevent structural failure. The paper’s own “muscle stiﬀness” and “panic in their hands and feet” description [PAGE 4] is a coordination failure that can increase peak load. A TES effect on EEG decoding accuracy does not adjudicate tendon safety.

The neurophysiological layer has its own dose and clock vetoes. The study uses 1 mA tDCS and 10 Hz tACS for 10 min with \(5\times7\) cm electrodes [PAGE 8], giving roughly \(28.6\,\mu\mathrm{A}/\mathrm{cm}^2\) for the stated current and area. That is a specific dose, not a universal performance dose. The after-effect window is 10 min for tDCS and 30 min for 10 Hz, 1 mA tACS, with a 24 h washout [PAGE 8]. If a competition occurs after the after-effect window, the neurophysiological rationale expires. If the washout is shorter than an unknown tACS after-effect, the next condition is contaminated. These are clock vetoes, not merely limitations.

The cross-layer adjudication is therefore: physical contact and weather can veto the movement; biomechanical load can veto the tissue; the after-effect clock can veto the intervention timing; only then does the cortical-excitability layer get to contribute. The source’s strong psychological framing — “80% is psychological factors” for senior athletes [PAGE 1] — must not be read as a license to ignore friction, tendon stress, or weather. The paper’s own rock-climbing description includes “dangerous, diﬃcult, and beautiful” traits and “slipping and falling” risk [PAGE 2], which are physical facts. A valid TES claim must specify the layer it is licensed to affect: EEG decoding or motor preparation, not adhesion, not tendon strength, not route safety.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Verifier: preregister \(n=60\), randomize tDCS, tACS, and sham, double-blind, primary endpoint = time-to-failure on a standardized indoor route; secondary endpoint = left/right motor-imagery accuracy. Prediction: if the source effect is a decoding effect, tDCS will raise motor-imagery accuracy by roughly 4–7 percentage points over sham but will not produce a 95% confidence interval for actual climbing time-to-failure that excludes zero. Veto: if motor-imagery accuracy improves while actual climbing time-to-failure is null, the claim “TES improves rock-climbing performance” is vetoed; if actual climbing improves without any EEG change, the proposed cortical mechanism is vetoed. This directly tests the source’s proxy gap between Table 3 accuracy [PAGE 9] and real climbing.

Verifier: preregister a responder model using baseline accuracy, baseline \(\mu\)-band power, and electrode-to-M1 anatomy; test the nine-subject pattern in a new sample. Prediction: low baseline accuracy should predict larger tDCS gains, and the tACS nonresponders resembling Subject 5 and Subject 8 [PAGE 9] should either replicate with an identifiable baseline/anatomical predictor or disappear as regression-to-the-mean noise. Prediction: the paper’s stated 10 min tDCS and 30 min tACS after-effects [PAGE 8] should produce no significant carryover at 24 h, while wet holds should reduce \(\mu_s\) enough to increase slip rate and eliminate any TES advantage. Veto: if a significant tACS carryover remains at 24 h, the washout assumption is vetoed; if TES improves dry-hold completion but not wet-hold completion, the physical-friction layer is confirmed as a veto; if TES improves wet-hold performance as much as dry, the friction-veto model is falsified. These three scenarios are verifiable because they specify randomization, blinding, endpoints, time intervals, and a data-release rule, and they predict where the source’s cortical-decoding effect should stop and where physical or clock constraints should override it.
