The paper’s causal chain is: discrete event data supplies positions \(x^i_{\mathrm{ball}}\) and \(x^i_j\) at event times; tracking data supplies the training target velocities \(v^i_j\) at 25 Hz; a learned operator maps a short event history to all-player velocities; those velocities then feed PPCF/OBSO space evaluation. The abstract states the motivating gap: “event-based positional data typically lacks the continuous temporal information needed to directly calculate crucial properties such as velocity” [PAGE 1]. The introduction sharpens the problem: “events such as passes and shots occur irregularly, event data is discrete concerning time. This makes it difficult to accurately calculate player velocities” [PAGE 2]. The methodological move is not position imputation but velocity completion with positions assumed known: “We present a new task to complete player velocity from player positional information only at the time of the event in team sports” [PAGE 3].

Formally, for target event \(i\), the input is \(x^i=(x^i_{\mathrm{ball}},x^i_1,\dots,x^i_N)\), and the output is \(\tilde v^i=(\tilde v^i_1,\dots,\tilde v^i_N)\). For temporal models the input is the sequence \((x^{i-k+1},\dots,x^i)\) with \(k=10\) [PAGE 6]. The paper explicitly notes a key gray zone: “we do not have information on player jersey numbers to distinguish the players” [PAGE 6]. Thus the model cannot maintain persistent player-specific speed priors; it sees roles such as teammate, ball holder, goalkeeper, and attack direction, but not identity. This matters because a fast fullback and a slow center-back may occupy similar graph positions in different events.

The main quantitative result is that GRNN lowers RMSE to 1.90 m/s versus the rule-based 4.51 m/s, and the authors write that “the GRNN reduces the error by 58% compared to the rule-based method” [PAGE 11]. The ordering is informative: MLP 2.29, GNN 2.27, VAE 2.37, VGNN 2.31, RNN 1.98, VRNN 2.27, GRNN 1.90, GVRNN 2.21; additional-feature variants are GNNadd 2.23, VGNNdec_add 2.24, GRNNdec_add 1.93, GVRNNdec_add 2.15 [PAGE 11]. Temporal state helps more than graph structure alone, and extra event-action/end-ball features do not improve the best GRNN. The VAE variants underperform; the paper says this likely stems from the Gaussian assumption, and “the true velocity distribution (Fig. 3) is not the Gaussian distribution” [PAGE 18], because “Football players do not frequently run fast, but walk and jog” [PAGE 19].

A multi-hop audit must trace error propagation into space evaluation. PPCF is a control-probability field driven by player velocity, reaction time, maximum speed, and control rate; OBSO integrates scoring probability over the pitch. The paper’s PPCF equation uses \(s\), \(\lambda_j\), and \(\tau_{\exp}\), and states that “we set the values of \(\lambda_j\), \(s\), reaction time, and maximum speed to the shared GitHub defaults” [PAGE 18]. Therefore, velocity error is not merely an RMSE issue: it changes interception-time estimates and thus PPCF/OBSO. The paper validates this downstream: GRNN mean PPCF error \(0.0302\) versus rule-based \(0.0570\), and mean OBSO error \(0.000499\) versus \(0.000817\) [PAGE 14]. However, Table 5 shows rule-based was smaller in 99 of 2,958 PPCF cases and 277 of 2,958 OBSO cases [PAGE 14]. So the claim is aggregate improvement, not universal dominance.

Gray-zone audit points: (1) the task is restricted to the attacking third to compare with prior work [PAGE 6], so domain shift outside that region is untested; (2) the model assumes all player and ball positions are known, but the conclusion notes that many public datasets “do not include complete positional data” [PAGE 16]; (3) aggregate RMSE can hide high-speed underprediction, and the paper concedes “It is difficult for any model to predict when the true velocities were high. One possible reason may be the small amount of data at higher velocities” [PAGE 12]; (4) the loss equations subtract a dimensionless KL term from a squared-velocity reconstruction term, which is dimensionally non-homogeneous unless an implicit unit-bearing weight is assumed. These are not fatal, but they define the valid inference envelope: use GRNN for aggregate event-time space evaluation inside the attacking third, not for sprint-level biomechanical claims or incomplete-position datasets without further validation.

Task operator. The paper defines completion of all player velocities from positions:
\[
\tilde v^i = F_\theta(x^{i-k+1},\dots,x^i),\qquad
x^i=(x^i_{\mathrm{ball}},x^i_1,\dots,x^i_N),\quad
v^i=(v^i_1,\dots,v^i_N),
\]
with \(x\in\mathbb{R}^2\) in pitch coordinates and \(v\in\mathbb{R}^2\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). For the non-temporal form, \(\tilde v^i=F_\theta(x^i)\); for the temporal form, \(k=10\) [PAGE 6]. The task is restricted to events in the attacking third [PAGE 6].

RMSE operator. The reported metric is
\[
\mathrm{RMSE}_{D}=
\frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{D}
\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{N}\left|\tilde v^i-v^i\right|^2},
\qquad
\mathrm{RMSE}_{D^*}=
\frac{1}{|D^*|}\sum_{D^*}
\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{N}\left|\tilde v^i-v^i\right|^2},
\]
with units \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) [PAGE 6]. Breakdown regimes: undefined if \(|D|=0\), \(|D^*|=0\), or \(N=0\); zero iff every player’s velocity is exactly recovered at every event; aggregate averaging can mask per-player and high-speed tail errors. The paper’s test sets are matched in size at 2,958 events [PAGE 9].

GNN operator. The node-to-edge and edge-to-node maps are
\[
e=f_e(o),\qquad o'=f_o(e),\qquad \tilde v^i=\mathrm{GNN}(x^i),
\]
where \(o=(o_{\mathrm{ball}},o_1,\dots,o_N)\) are node features and \(e\) are edge features [PAGE 7]. Role features include “whether or not the player was a teammate and whether or not the player was a ball holder” on edges, and ball/player status, attack direction, ball-holder status, and goalkeeper status on nodes [PAGE 7]. With additional event action \(a^i\) and end-ball position \(x^i_{\mathrm{end}}\),
\[
\tilde v^i=\mathrm{GNN}_{\mathrm{add}}\left(x^i,f_a(a^i),f_{x_{\mathrm{end}}}(x^i_{\mathrm{end}})\right).
\]
Breakdown regimes: no explicit physical speed bound is imposed; if the graph lacks useful role contrasts, the GNN degenerates toward an MLP; identity is unavailable because jersey numbers are absent [PAGE 6].

RNN and GRNN operators. The recurrent encoder/decoder is
\[
z^t=f_{\mathrm{enc}}(x^t,h^{t-1}),\qquad
\tilde v^t=f_{\mathrm{dec}}(z^t,h^{t-1}),\qquad
h^t=f_{\mathrm{rnn}}(x^t,z^t,h^{t-1}),
\]
and the graph-recurrent version is
\[
z^t=\mathrm{GNN}_{\mathrm{enc}}(x^t,h^{t-1}),\qquad
\tilde v^t=\mathrm{GNN}_{\mathrm{dec}}(z^t,h^{t-1}),\qquad
h^t=f_{\mathrm{rnn}}(x^t,z^t,h^{t-1}),
\]
with an additional-feature decoder form
\[
\tilde v^t=\mathrm{GNN}_{\mathrm{dec\_add}}\left(z^t,f_a(a^t),f_{x_{\mathrm{end}}}(x^t_{\mathrm{end}}),h^{t-1}\right)
\]
[PAGE 8]. Breakdown regimes: \(k=10\) is a finite temporal horizon; too short a window cannot resolve acceleration, while longer windows reduce usable sequences because of missing information, reflected in \(D^*\) training 14,616 versus \(D\) training 17,118 [PAGE 9]. If \(h^{t-1}\) is uninformative or the sequence is missing, the GRNN collapses toward the GNN/MLP regime.

Loss operators. Non-recurrent loss is
\[
L_{\mathrm{MSE-D}}=
\frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{D}
\left[
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{N}\left|\tilde v^i-v^i\right|^2
-L_{\mathrm{KL-D}}
\right],
\]
with
\[
L_{\mathrm{KL-D}}
=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q_\phi(z^i\mid x^i,v^i)\,\|\,p_\theta(z^i\mid x^i)\right),
\]
and the recurrent loss is
\[
L_{\mathrm{MSE-D^*}}=
\frac{1}{|D^*|}\sum_{D^*}
\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=i-k+1}^{i}
\left[
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{N}\left|\tilde v^t-v^t\right|^2
-L_{\mathrm{KL-D^*}}
\right],
\]
with
\[
L_{\mathrm{KL-D^*}}
=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q_\phi(z^{\le t}\mid x^{\le t},v^{\le t},z^{<t})\,\|\,p_\theta(z^{\le t}\mid x^{\le t},z^{<t})\right)
\]
[PAGE 10-11]. Units: squared-velocity reconstruction is \(\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}\); KL divergence is in nats and dimensionless. The subtraction is dimensionally non-homogeneous unless an implicit unit-bearing weight is assumed; this is a formal caveat. Breakdown regimes: KL can dominate or collapse if the Gaussian posterior is misspecified; the paper reports VAE-structured models poorly predict velocity because the true distribution is non-Gaussian [PAGE 18-19].

Space-evaluation operators. The event-level absolute errors are
\[
E^{i}_{\mathrm{ppcf}}=
\frac{1}{|R\times R|}
\sum_{r\in R\times R}
\left|
\widehat{\mathrm{PPCF}}^i_r-\mathrm{PPCF}^i_r
\right|,
\qquad
E^{i}_{\mathrm{obso}}=
\frac{1}{|R\times R|}
\sum_{r\in R\times R}
\left|
\widehat{\mathrm{OBSO}}^i_r-\mathrm{OBSO}^i_r
\right|,
\]
with \(50\times 32=1600\) grid cells and dimensionless outputs [PAGE 13]. OBSO is
\[
P(G\mid D)=
\sum_{r\in R\times R}
P(G_r\mid C_r,T_r,D)\,
P(C_r\mid T_r,D)\,
P(T_r\mid D),
\]
and PPCF satisfies
\[
\frac{d\mathrm{PPCF}_j}{dT}(t,\vec r,T\mid s,\lambda_j)
=
\left(1-\sum_k \mathrm{PPCF}_k(t,\vec r,T\mid s,\lambda_j)\right)
f_j(t,\vec r,T\mid s)\lambda_j,
\]
\[
f_j(t,\vec r,T\mid s)
=
\left[
1+e^{-\pi\frac{T-\tau_{\exp}(t,\vec r)}{\sqrt{3}s}}
\right]^{-1}.
\]
Units: \(T,s,\tau_{\exp}\) in seconds; \(\lambda_j\) in \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\); \(f_j\) and PPCF dimensionless; \(d\mathrm{PPCF}/dT\) in \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) [PAGE 18]. Singularity/breakdown regimes: \(s=0\) makes the exponent undefined; \(\lambda_j=0\) freezes control; \(\sum_k \mathrm{PPCF}_k=1\) saturates the derivative to zero; \(T=\tau_{\exp}\) gives \(f_j=0.5\); the paper uses shared GitHub defaults for \(\lambda_j\), \(s\), reaction time, and maximum speed [PAGE 18]. The empirical downstream errors are mean PPCF \(0.0302\) for GRNN versus \(0.0570\) for rule-based, and mean OBSO \(0.000499\) versus \(0.000817\) [PAGE 14].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is retrocausal in the sense that the downstream metric creates the need for the upstream completion: PPCF/OBSO require velocities, event data do not directly provide them, so velocity completion is inserted to preserve the older evaluation program. Spearman’s OBSO/PPCF framework is the historical anchor; the paper notes that PPCF “quantifies a player’s ball control rate and necessitates both positional and velocity information” [PAGE 12]. Earlier work by Umemoto and Fujii used rule-based velocities when applying OBSO to event data, and the present paper explicitly compares against that rule-based baseline because “the calculation of OBSO in that study uses rule-based velocities due to the discrete nature of the event data” [PAGE 4-5]. The scientific genealogy is therefore: tracking-based space evaluation → event-based defensive/offensive metrics → rule-based velocity patch → learned velocity completion → GRNN with graph and temporal structure → re-validation against PPCF/OBSO.

The adjacent lineage includes position imputation and trajectory completion. The paper reviews non-autoregressive imputation, broadcast-video trajectory completion, derivative-based hybrid prediction, and graph-temporal models [PAGE 3-4]. It distinguishes itself from Penn et al. and Everett et al. because those predict positions at discrete intervals, whereas this work “directly fills in the velocity not present in the test dataset” [PAGE 4]. That distinction is important: velocity is a derivative-like quantity, so errors are amplified by event irregularity and by missing positions. The paper’s tracking data at 25 Hz provides the supervision signal [PAGE 9], but the deployed input is event-based and sparse.

Multi-week traps. First, the “tracking-data-first” trap: assuming high-frequency tracking is the only legitimate ground truth and treating event data as merely degraded tracking. The paper instead treats event data as a public, lower-cost data type and asks what can be recovered from it [PAGE 2, PAGE 4]. Second, the “architecture-chasing” trap: assuming VAE/GVRNN complexity must win because it won in prior multi-agent settings. The results contradict that: VAE 2.37, VGNN 2.31, VRNN 2.27, GVRNN 2.21, all worse than GRNN 1.90 [PAGE 11]. The Gaussian latent assumption is the likely culprit because “the true velocity distribution (Fig. 3) is not the Gaussian distribution” [PAGE 18]. Third, the “aggregate-metric” trap: optimizing mean RMSE while ignoring high-speed tails. The paper warns that high velocities are difficult to predict and data are sparse there [PAGE 12], and it notes that even the best 1.90 m/s error “is still relatively large compared to previous sprint studies” [PAGE 15]. Fourth, the “identity” trap: because jersey numbers are unavailable, a model cannot learn player-specific speed profiles [PAGE 6]; any multi-week project that assumes identity-aware priors will overstate deployability. Fifth, the “public-data completeness” trap: the method assumes all player positions are known, yet many public datasets “do not include complete positional data” [PAGE 16]. Sixth, the “attacking-third portability” trap: the task is deliberately restricted to the attacking third for comparison [PAGE 6], so extending to all events or defensive-third transitions is an open validation problem, not a free generalization.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
At the player level, the paper’s premise is that team-sport behavior arises from “multiple interacting agents” and “autonomous decision-making” [PAGE 1-2]. A player’s velocity at an event is not an isolated scalar; it is a response to the ball, teammates, opponents, and the anticipated next event. The model cannot observe beliefs or intentions, only positions at event times. Its graph features approximate role-aware theory of mind: edges encode teammate and ball-holder relations, and nodes encode ball/player status, attack direction, ball-holder status, and goalkeeper status [PAGE 7]. This is a shallow, observable proxy for recursive reasoning, not a full belief-state model. Because jersey identities are absent [PAGE 6], the model also cannot maintain a persistent “this specific opponent is fast” representation across events; it must infer from roles and geometry.

At the analyst level, recursive theory of mind appears in model selection under downstream evaluation. The analyst knows that RMSE is not the final objective: PPCF and OBSO will be consumed by coaches or scouts, and those consumers will interpret space-control maps as if velocities were true. The paper anticipates this by evaluating both velocity error and space-metric error [PAGE 13-14]. The recursive loop is: model predicts velocity → metric assigns control/scoring probability → evaluator compares to ground truth → model is judged by the evaluator’s error, not only by its own loss. This is why the GRNN result matters beyond 1.90 m/s: it reduces mean PPCF and OBSO errors relative to rule-based [PAGE 14].

Crisis habit regression. Under sparse, irregular event data, the habitual response is the rule-based velocity patch used in prior OBSO work. That habit is understandable: it is interpretable, cheap, and preserves the old metric pipeline. But its RMSE is 4.51 m/s [PAGE 11], and its PPCF/OBSO errors are roughly double the GRNN’s means [PAGE 14]. The habit regresses precisely when the clock is short and data are incomplete.

Counter-trap. The overcorrection is to add the most complex latent-variable machinery—VAE, VRNN, GVRNN—on the assumption that missing information must be generated probabilistically. The paper’s results show this counter-trap fails under Gaussian assumptions: VAE 2.37, VRNN 2.27, GVRNN 2.21, all worse than GRNN 1.90 [PAGE 11], and the true velocity distribution is non-Gaussian [PAGE 18]. The counter-trap mistakes distributional modeling for physical realism.

Clock. The clock is both the event sequence length \(k=10\) and the irregular arrival of events. A too-short window cannot resolve acceleration; a too-long window increases missingness and reduces \(D^*\) from 17,118 training events to 14,616 [PAGE 9]. The correct compromise is not “more model” but “right temporal horizon plus role-aware graph state,” which is what GRNN approximates. The recursive lesson: use the simplest temporally informed model that survives downstream space-metric validation, and explicitly flag high-speed tail uncertainty rather than letting a Gaussian decoder hallucinate sprint speeds.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto. Velocity is a physical quantity in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Any completed velocity used in PPCF must be compatible with the maximum-speed and reaction-time parameters in the PPCF calculation; the paper states those are set to shared GitHub defaults [PAGE 18]. If a model outputs a velocity that exceeds the configured maximum speed or implies an impossible acceleration over the event interval, the downstream PPCF/OBSO value should be vetoed even if the aggregate RMSE is low. The paper does not impose an explicit hard physical bound in the GRNN, so this veto is an external adjudication layer, not an internal guarantee. The valid proof is therefore conditional: given the PPCF maximum-speed parameter, reject any completed velocity field that violates it; absent that check, the metric can be physically inconsistent.

Biomechanical layer veto. The paper’s distributional evidence is a biomechanical veto against Gaussian decoders for sprint-sensitive use. It states that “Football players do not frequently run fast, but walk and jog” [PAGE 19], and that the true velocity distribution is not Gaussian [PAGE 18]. The VAE-structured models’ worse RMSE [PAGE 11] is consistent with a mismatch between Gaussian latent assumptions and a heavy-tailed, low-mean speed distribution. A biomechanical veto should trigger if a model’s predicted speed distribution has substantial mass at unrealistic sprint speeds, negative speed magnitude, or an implausible acceleration profile. The paper also concedes high-velocity underprediction: “It is difficult for any model to predict when the true velocities were high. One possible reason may be the small amount of data at higher velocities” [PAGE 12]. Therefore, a sprint-scouting use case is vetoed unless the high-speed stratum is separately validated; the aggregate 1.90 m/s result is not sufficient.

Weather layer veto. The source contains no weather variables, no temperature, no precipitation, no wind, and no pitch-state metadata. The dataset is described only as event and tracking data from “55 games of the 2019 Meiji J1 League season” with tracking at 25 Hz [PAGE 8-9]. Therefore a weather-layer veto cannot be proven from this corpus. The correct adjudication is “unidentifiable”: any claim that weather-conditioned velocity completion is validated would be unsupported. If weather is joined externally, the veto rule should be: if weather changes the PPCF maximum-speed or reaction-time parameters, or changes ball roll and player traction, then the velocity completion and PPCF parameters must be re-estimated; otherwise the measured space-metric error conflates model error with weather-induced physical changes. The paper’s shared-default parameter choice [PAGE 18] means the current results are weather-agnostic, not weather-robust.

Cross-layer adjudication order. First, physical plausibility: reject impossible speeds/accelerations. Second, biomechanical distribution: reject Gaussian or tail-blind outputs for sprint claims. Third, downstream metric consistency: require GRNN mean PPCF error below 0.0570 and mean OBSO error below 0.000817 versus rule-based [PAGE 14], while acknowledging the 99 and 277 cases where rule-based was closer [PAGE 14]. Fourth, data-completeness: reject deployment on datasets without complete player positions, because the method assumes known positions and public datasets often lack them [PAGE 16]. Fifth, weather: mark unidentifiable unless external weather covariates are supplied. This ordering prevents a low RMSE from overriding a physical or biomechanical impossibility.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
