The counter-trap is second-order. If the defense models the kicker as regressing under pressure, it can bias coverage toward the anticipated miss (short middle or opposite sideline), turning the kicker's own crisis habit into a structured error. The offense's meta-counter is to randomize the kick direction or to use a quick-kick formation that shortens the blocker's decision window t_b, collapsing \mathcal{B} to the empty set.

Clock coupling: Let \tau(t) denote remaining game time in seconds. Early-game utility favors field position (maximize \delta_y constraint satisfaction). Late-game utility adds clock management: a kick that stays in bounds may keep the clock running, while certain boundary outcomes alter stoppage rules. Because [PAGE 120] is silent on clock rules (1921 regulations differ from modern), any \tau-dependent payoff matrix is an external imposition. The recursive theory therefore predicts three nested regimes: (1) early-game, skill-dominant, sideline geometry applies; (2) crisis, habit regression raises \sigma_y and risks veto; (3) counter-trap active, where the defense's expectation of regression shifts the optimal launch distribution away from the sideline even if the kicker is physically capable.
