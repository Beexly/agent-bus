<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s central reasoning move is to replace an implausible independence prior in multi-object tracking with a traffic-specific predictive prior. The standard Labeled Multi-Bernoulli filter represents the posterior as a set of labeled Bernoulli components, each with existence probability and spatial density, and assumes that single-target motion is conditionally independent of other targets [PAGE 1] “the standard LMB filter assumes that all objects move independently” [PAGE 2]. In traffic, that assumption is weak: vehicles respond to leading vehicles, and their yaw dynamics are constrained by drivable-area geometry. The source therefore states the problem directly: [PAGE 1] “This paper proposes an approach adapting a multi-object tracking system to model interaction between vehicles, and the current road geometry.” The resulting architecture is not a full joint Bayesian dependency model. It is a prediction-step correction scheme: the LMB filter remains structurally labeled-Bernoulli, but the unscented prediction of each Gaussian mixture component is locally warped by interaction and road-map constraints.

The derivation chain is multi-hop. First, the multi-object posterior is approximated by an LMB RFS with parameters \( \{(r^{(\ell)}, p^{(\ell)})\}_{\ell \in \mathbb{L}} \), where \(r^{(\ell)}\) is dimensionless existence probability and \(p^{(\ell)}(x)\) is a spatial density with units of inverse state-volume [PAGE 2]. Second, each spatial density is implemented as a Gaussian mixture, \(p^{(\ell)}(x)=\sum_j w^{(\ell,j)}\mathcal{N}(x;\hat{x}^{(\ell,j)},P^{(\ell,j)})\), with dimensionless mixture weights and covariance units determined by the state components [PAGE 3]. Third, because vehicle motion is nonlinear, each Gaussian component is propagated through an unscented transform using sigma points around an augmented mean and covariance [PAGE 3]. Fourth, the nominal motion operator is a Constant Turn Rate and Velocity model, with state vector \([x,y,v,\phi,\omega]^T\), where \(x,y\) are meters, \(v\) is meters per second, \(\phi\) is radians, and \(\omega\) is radians per second [PAGE 4]. Fifth, the model’s nominal constant-velocity and constant-turn-rate assumptions are explicitly declared to fail in traffic: [PAGE 4] “In many scenarios these assumptions are not fulﬁlled.” Sixth, the failure is repaired by two sigma-point-level corrections. The velocity sigma point is modified by an Intelligent Driver Model deceleration term when a leading track is detected [PAGE 5], and the turn-rate sigma point is modified toward the orientation of the road rectangle containing that sigma point [PAGE 5].

The gray-zone audit is important because the paper’s empirical improvement is obtained by introducing dependencies only inside the prediction step. The filter does not convert the LMB posterior into a full δ-GLMB multi-hypothesis dependency graph, even though the paper notes that the LMB approximation loses dependence information [PAGE 1]. Instead, each track component “sees” other tracks only as estimated mean states satisfying an existence threshold and only when spatial association through rectangles is satisfied [PAGE 4]–[PAGE 5]. This creates a subtle circularity: if the interacting track’s mean is wrong, the interaction correction can reinforce the wrong motion prior. The road-map correction has a similar issue: rectangles are an approximation of a high-precision map, so rectangle orientation becomes a synthetic prior that may be locally wrong at curved transitions, occlusions, or lane changes [PAGE 4]. The intersection treatment introduces another gray zone: when a component lies in multiple rectangles, the filter splits the component and divides its weight uniformly, thereby creating multiple hypotheses [PAGE 5]. That improves multi-hypothesis behavior but can also dilute component weights if rectangle association is noisy. Finally, the numerical results show large single-object RMSE improvements, but the dense-traffic multi-object evaluation is explicitly qualitative because ground truth is unavailable [PAGE 6]. Thus the source is strong as a domain-informed prediction correction, but it is not a proof of globally optimal interaction-aware Bayes estimation.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
1. Labeled Bernoulli and LMB structure.
A Bernoulli RFS is either empty with probability \(1-r\) or a singleton with probability \(r\) and spatial density \(p(x)\) [PAGE 2]. The labeled multi-Bernoulli density is written as
\[
\pi(X)=\delta_{|X|}(|\mathbb{L}(X)|) w(\mathbb{L}(X)) p_X,
\]
with label projection \(\mathbb{L}((x,\ell))=\ell\) [PAGE 2]. The weights are dimensionless, and the spatial density has units inverse to the single-target state-space measure. For a state \([x,y,v,\phi,\omega]^T\), the density units are formally \(\mathrm{m}^{-2}(\mathrm{m/s})^{-1}\mathrm{rad}^{-1}(\mathrm{rad/s})^{-1}\) if all components are treated as independent coordinates.

The LMB prediction is
\[
\pi_+(X)=
\big\{(r^{(\ell)}_{+,S},p^{(\ell)}_{+,S})\big\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}}
\cup
\big\{(r^{(\ell)}_B,p^{(\ell)}_B)\big\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{B}},
\]
with
\[
r^{(\ell)}_{+,S}=\eta_S(\ell)r^{(\ell)},
\]
\[
p^{(\ell)}_{+,S}=
\frac{\langle p_S(\cdot,\ell) f(x|\cdot,\ell),p(\cdot,\ell)\rangle}
{\eta_S(\ell)},
\]
\[
\eta_S(\ell)=\int
\langle p_S(\cdot,\ell) f(x|\cdot,\ell),p(\cdot,\ell)\rangle dx,
\]
as given in [PAGE 2]. Here \(p_S\) is survival probability, dimensionless; \(f(x|\cdot,\ell)\) is a transition density with inverse-state-volume units; \(\eta_S(\ell)\) normalizes the predicted density. Breakdown regime: if survival probability or normalization becomes zero, the track’s predicted existence collapses; if the birth model assigns nonzero existence to invalid states, spurious tracks can be created.

2. Gaussian mixture implementation.
Each labeled track spatial density is
\[
p^{(\ell)}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{J^{(\ell)}} w^{(\ell,j)}
\mathcal{N}\big(x;\hat{x}^{(\ell,j)},P^{(\ell,j)}\big),
\]
with weights normalized to one [PAGE 3]. The weights are dimensionless; means carry state units; covariances carry squared state units. Breakdown regime: if covariance loses positive semidefiniteness, the matrix square root used for sigma points is invalid, and predicted covariance can become nonphysical.

3. Unscented prediction.
For each Gaussian component, the augmented mean and covariance are
\[
\mu^{(\ell,j)}_k=
\begin{bmatrix}
\hat{x}^{(\ell,j)}_{k-1}\\
0
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\Sigma^{(\ell,j)}_k=
\operatorname{diag}\big(P^{(\ell,j)}_{k-1},Q_{k-1}\big),
\]
where \(Q_{k-1}\) is process-noise covariance [PAGE 3]. Sigma points are
\[
\chi^{(\ell,j,0)}_k=\mu^{(\ell,j)}_k,
\]
\[
\chi^{(\ell,j,i)}_k=
\mu^{(\ell,j)}_k+
\left(\sqrt{(n+\kappa)\Sigma^{(\ell,j)}_k}\right)_i,
\]
\[
\chi^{(\ell,j,i+n)}_k=
\mu^{(\ell,j)}_k-
\left(\sqrt{(n+\kappa)\Sigma^{(\ell,j)}_k}\right)_i,
\]
with weights
\[
W^{(\ell,j,0)}=\frac{\kappa}{n+\kappa},
\qquad
W^{(\ell,j,i)}=W^{(\ell,j,i+n)}=\frac{\kappa}{2(n+\kappa)}.
\]
The equations are listed in [PAGE 3]. Units: sigma points carry state units; weights are dimensionless. Assumptions: \(n+\kappa\neq 0\) and \(\Sigma^{(\ell,j)}_k\) must admit a matrix square root. Breakdown regime: \(\kappa=-n\) makes the weights undefined; negative or indefinite covariance makes the sigma-point construction invalid.

4. CTRV motion model.
The state is
\[
x^{(\ell,j,i)}_{k-1}=[x,y,v,\phi,\omega]^T,
\]
with \(x,y\) in meters, \(v\) in meters per second, \(\phi\) in radians, and \(\omega\) in radians per second [PAGE 4]. The prediction is
\[
x^{(\ell,j,i)}_{k,+}=x^{(\ell,j,i)}_{k-1}+
\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{v}{\omega}\left(\sin(\phi+\omega T)-\sin(\phi)\right)\\
\frac{v}{\omega}\left(\cos(\phi)-\cos(\phi+\omega T)\right)\\
0\\
\omega T\\
0
\end{bmatrix},
\]
as in [PAGE 4]. Here \(T\) is the sampling time in seconds. Formal breakdown: the terms \(v/\omega\) are undefined as \(\omega\to 0\). A numerically safe implementation must replace them with the straight-line limit \(x+vT\cos\phi\), \(y+vT\sin\phi\). If this limit is not handled, a sigma point near zero turn rate can produce large numerical errors or NaNs.

5. Intelligent Driver Model interaction.
The source states that IDM describes longitudinal dynamics behind a leading vehicle [PAGE 3]. The interaction acceleration is
\[
\dot{v}^{\text{interaction}}_\alpha
=
-a\left(\frac{s^*(v_\alpha,\Delta v_\alpha)}{s_\alpha}\right)^2,
\]
and the desired distance is
\[
s^*(v_\alpha,\Delta v_\alpha)
=
s_0+v_\alpha T+
\frac{v_\alpha\Delta v_\alpha}{2\sqrt{ab}},
\]
as given in [PAGE 3]. Units: \(a\) and \(b\) are accelerations in \(\mathrm{m/s^2}\); \(s_0\) and \(s_\alpha\) are distances in meters; \(T\) is seconds; \(v_\alpha\) and \(\Delta v_\alpha\) are velocities in \(\mathrm{m/s}\). Assumptions: \(a>0\), \(b>0\), \(s_0>0\), and \(s_\alpha>0\). Singularities: \(s_\alpha=0\) gives divergence; \(s_\alpha<0\) is nonphysical; \(a\) or \(b\leq 0\) invalidates the square root and the sign convention.

6. Sigma-point velocity correction.
The paper writes the adapted velocity component as
\[
v^{(\ell,j,i)}
=
\dot{v}^{(\ell,j,i)}\cdot T
=
-a\left(
\frac{s^*(v^{(\ell,j,i)},\Delta v^{(\ell,j,i)})}
{s^{(\ell,j,i)}}
\right)^2,
\]
labeled as equation (25) in [PAGE 5]. Unit audit: the left-hand side is a velocity, \(\mathrm{m/s}\). If the right-hand side is exactly the printed expression \(-a(s^*/s)^2\), its unit is \(\mathrm{m/s^2}\), not \(\mathrm{m/s}\), unless \(T=1\ \mathrm{s}\) is implicitly absorbed or \(a\) is reinterpreted as an impulse-like coefficient. A unit-consistent reading of “acceleration multiplied by sampling time” is
\[
\Delta v_{\text{int}}
=
-aT\left(\frac{s^*}{s}\right)^2,
\]
which has units \(\mathrm{m/s}\). This is a formal gray-zone issue, not a betting-style ratio; it is a dimensional consistency issue in the predictive operator.

7. Road-map turn-rate correction.
For each sigma point, the orientation mismatch is
\[
\Delta\phi^{(\ell,j,i)}
=
\phi^{(i)}_R-\phi^{(\ell,j,i)},
\]
with \(\phi\) in radians [PAGE 5]. The paper then writes
\[
\omega^{(\ell,j,i)}
=
\Delta\phi^{(\ell,j,i)}
\cdot
\frac{s}{v^{(\ell,j,i)}},
\]
labeled as equation (27) in [PAGE 5], and explains: [PAGE 5] “So, the last part of equation (27) can be interpreted as the time how long the sigma point will be located in the current rectangle.” Here \(s\) is meters and \(v\) is meters per second, so \(s/v\) is seconds. Unit audit: multiplying an angle in radians by a time in seconds yields \(\mathrm{rad\cdot s}\), not a turn rate in \(\mathrm{rad/s}\). If the intended operator is a turn rate that removes the angle mismatch over the remaining traversal time \(s/v\), the unit-consistent form is
\[
\omega^{(\ell,j,i)}
=
\frac{\Delta\phi^{(\ell,j,i)}}{s/v^{(\ell,j,i)}}
=
\Delta\phi^{(\ell,j,i)}\frac{v^{(\ell,j,i)}}{s}.
\]
If the source instead intended a finite yaw correction over one prediction interval, then the symbol \(\omega\) should be treated as an angle increment rather than a physical turn rate. Breakdown regimes: \(v^{(\ell,j,i)}=0\) makes the time-to-border infinite or undefined; \(s=0\) makes the correction singular; poor rectangle orientation makes the correction biased.

8. Numerical implementation thresholds.
The practical implementation uses IDM parameters \(T=1.6\ \mathrm{s}\), \(a=0.73\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\), \(b=1.67\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\), process-noise normalized values of \(5\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\) for velocity and \(0.1\ \mathrm{rad/s^2}\) for turn rate, persistence probability \(0.99\), radar detection probability \(0.85\), camera detection probability \(0.75\), clutter intensities \(10^{-5}\) and \(0.01\), and extraction/pruning thresholds [PAGE 5]: [PAGE 5] “Tracks are extracted with an existence probability greater than 0.2 and are pruned with a probability less than 0.01.” These are dimensionless probabilities except for the process-noise units. Breakdown regime: if existence thresholds are too low, clutter becomes tracks; if too high, occluded interacting vehicles are dropped before the interaction model can correct them.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of the paper is layered. At the top is random finite set multi-object filtering, tracing to Mahler’s finite-set statistics and Vo/Vo labeled RFS conjugacy [PAGE 1]. The δ-GLMB filter provides exact multi-hypothesis labeled filtering but suffers exponential complexity [PAGE 1]. The LMB filter approximates the δ-GLMB posterior with independent Bernoulli components, gaining efficiency but losing inter-object dependence [PAGE 1]. The present work inherits this tradeoff and tries to repair it by domain knowledge rather than by exact joint modeling.

Below the RFS layer is recursive Bayesian filtering: Kalman filtering for linear Gaussian models, extended and unscented Kalman filtering for nonlinear motion [PAGE 1], [PAGE 3]. The source selects the unscented Kalman filter because it permits sigma-point-level modifications: [PAGE 3] “The UKF approach facilitates adapting individual sigma points using interaction between objects and road map information.” This is a key lineage point: the paper’s novelty is not a new RFS conjugacy proof, but an operator-level injection of traffic semantics into the unscented prediction.

The traffic-model lineage runs through social-force pedestrian models, Generalized Force Models, Helly, and IDM. The paper explicitly contrasts force-based pedestrian models with vehicle car-following models, noting that social-force virtual forces are difficult to interpret physically for vehicles [PAGE 1]. It chooses IDM because it is a car-following model with longitudinal acceleration depending on velocity, gap, and velocity difference [PAGE 3]. The retro-causal trap here is to reuse pedestrian force models for vehicles without reinterpreting forces as accelerations, distances, and physical lane constraints. The paper avoids the worst of that trap by using IDM parameters in SI units and by applying interaction only along road-rectangle topology.

The road-map lineage comes from high-precision digital maps, DGPS reference lines, polygonal approximation, and lane-level rectangles [PAGE 4]. The source converts dense reference-line points into rectangles using the iterative endpoint-fit algorithm [PAGE 4]. This lineage creates a multi-week engineering trap: a map that is excellent for localization may be poor as a dynamic prior if its rectangles are too coarse, if orientation changes abruptly, or if intersection overlap creates ambiguous rectangle association. The paper’s answer is to split components across rectangles at intersections [PAGE 5], but that answer depends on correct rectangle indexing and weight normalization.

Multi-week traps also include label management. The paper notes that the LMB filter implicitly models data-association uncertainty, but labels must remain stable for interaction logic to work [PAGE 2]. If existence probability falls below the extraction threshold, interaction disappears; if a track is reinitialized, the label changes, and the interaction graph can break. The reported label-error interpretation is sensitive to this: [PAGE 6] the label error is zero if the reference vehicle is not tracked, increases by one if a new track is initialized, and the best result is a constant one. Thus historical label genealogy matters: track continuity is not merely cosmetic; it determines whether the interaction prior has a stable object to condition on.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In this tracker, the “mind” is not human intent but a recursive predictive model of other dynamic objects and the static environment. The standard LMB filter treats other vehicles as nuisance or independent clutter-like processes, except for measurement association. That is the habit-regression baseline: when a vehicle occludes, slows, or turns, the filter’s default habit is to extrapolate the last CTRV state. In crisis conditions—occlusion, dense traffic, roundabouts, intersections—the habit regression fails because the CTRV prior assumes constant velocity and turn rate while the actual vehicle is reacting to a leader or road geometry.

The counter-trap is the paper’s interaction model. It recursively asks: if this track is behind another track, how should its velocity change? The operator is IDM-based: [PAGE 3] “The Intelligent Driver Model (IDM) [14] is a car-following model which describes the longitudinal dynamics of a vehicle following a leading vehicle along a single lane.” If a leading track is found in the same or following rectangle, the velocity sigma point is decelerated according to gap and relative velocity [PAGE 4]–[PAGE 5]. This counters the habit regression by replacing blind constant-velocity extrapolation with a car-following prior. Similarly, the road-map counter-trap asks: if this sigma point is in a road rectangle, how should its orientation evolve? The answer is to pull the yaw state toward rectangle orientation [PAGE 5]. The source claims this yields more realistic covariance: [PAGE 6] “By adjusting sigma points (22) using road map information, not only the mean (23) is affected, but also the covariance (24) implicitly.”

The clock is the third recursive element. The prediction operates over sampling intervals and existence-propagation cycles. The IDM time gap \(T=1.6\ \mathrm{s}\) is not necessarily the same as the filter sampling interval, although both are temporal scales; the source uses \(T\) for IDM desired time gap and also for CTRV sampling time [PAGE 3]–[PAGE 5]. This symbol reuse is a modeling hazard. If the actual measurement cadence is slower than the interaction dynamics, the IDM correction may be stale; if faster, the correction may overreact to noisy gaps. In occlusion, the clock becomes decisive because the track must survive multiple intervals without measurements. The road-map prior helps maintain orientation and existence, while the interaction prior helps maintain plausible velocity. But if the clock is misaligned—latency, delayed track fusion, asynchronous radar/camera detections—the recursive model can believe a leading vehicle is closer or farther than it truly is.

The recursive trap is that the model treats estimated tracks as if they were known objects. The interaction search uses “all other existing tracks with a speciﬁc existence probability” [PAGE 4], and the road association uses estimated component means. If those estimates are biased, the tracker’s model of other minds becomes self-referential: bad estimates create bad interaction corrections, which then stabilize the bad estimates. The paper’s qualitative multi-object evaluation acknowledges this by relying on camera view and laser measurements rather than full ground truth [PAGE 6].

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer adjudication separates physical, kinematic, map, and sensor layers. The source’s operators are strong as soft Bayesian priors but weak as hard vetoes.

Physical layer. Vehicles have finite acceleration, tire friction, and collision constraints. The IDM uses maximum acceleration \(a\) and comfortable deceleration \(b\) [PAGE 3], [PAGE 5]. However, the model does not enforce a hard no-collision constraint. Formal proof sketch: let \(s_\alpha\) be the predicted gap to a leading track. IDM produces a negative acceleration term that grows as \(s_\alpha\) decreases. But if the subsequent measurement update assigns nonzero likelihood to a state with a smaller gap, the posterior can still move there because the filter has no projection onto a feasible set. Therefore IDM is a prior, not a safety veto. Breakdown regime: if \(s_\alpha\leq 0\), the operator is singular; if the required deceleration exceeds \(b\), the comfortable model underestimates emergency braking.

Biomechanical layer. The paper is vehicle-centric, not pedestrian-centric. The relevant “biomechanical” constraints are vehicle dynamics: yaw rate, lateral acceleration, and road curvature. The road-map turn-rate correction tries to align orientation with rectangle orientation [PAGE 5]. But the correction can demand a yaw change that exceeds plausible lateral acceleration if the rectangle is too sharp or the vehicle speed is high. A physically admissible turn rate satisfies approximately \(|\omega v| \leq a_{\text{lat,max}}\), where \(a_{\text{lat,max}}\) is a lateral acceleration limit. The source does not include this as a veto. Thus a map-based turn-rate prior can be dynamically implausible unless coupled to a curvature-speed consistency check.

Weather and sensor layer. The implementation uses radar and camera detection probabilities, clutter intensities, and process noise [PAGE 5]. In rain, fog, glare, or occlusion, detection probability falls and clutter may rise. The road map can still constrain position and orientation, but it cannot create measurements. The cross-layer veto here is evidential: if the sensor likelihood is too weak, existence probability may fall below the extraction threshold [PAGE 5], and the interaction model loses the track. Conversely, if clutter is high, rectangle-based interaction may associate a false track with a real leader. The paper’s persistence probability \(0.99\) helps maintain tracks, but it also risks prolonged ghost tracks if the update model is overconfident.

Adjudication proof. Let the true state space be constrained by physical feasibility set \(\mathcal{F}\), road feasibility set \(\mathcal{R}\), and sensor evidence set \(\mathcal{S}\). The proposed filter uses prior operators \(I_{\text{IDM}}\) and \(I_{\text{map}}\) to modify the prediction density:
\[
p_+(x|\ell) \propto
\int f_{\text{CTRV}}(x|x') 
\delta_{\text{IDM}}(v|x') 
\delta_{\text{map}}(\omega|x') 
p(x'|\ell) dx'.
\]
This is a soft reweighting, not a projection onto \(\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{R}\). Therefore, if a measurement likelihood is nonzero outside the map or interaction prior, the posterior may still accept it. A hard veto would require rejecting updates that violate constraints, or performing constrained optimization/data association. The source does not claim that. It claims improved accuracy and robustness under violated standard motion assumptions [PAGE 1], and the single-object RMSE table supports that claim for the evaluated scenario [PAGE 6].

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. IDM zero-gap singularity veto.
Counterfactual: a leading track is estimated so close that \(s^{(\ell,j,i)}\to 0\). The raw IDM interaction term,
\[
-a\left(\frac{s^*}{s}\right)^2,
\]
diverges. Verifiable test: evaluate the prediction on a synthetic pair with gap decreasing from \(10\ \mathrm{m}\) to \(0.01\ \mathrm{m}\), while keeping \(s^*>0\). Prediction: without a minimum-gap guard, the predicted velocity correction becomes nonfinite or produces an acceleration magnitude far outside the parameter range; covariance may collapse or explode. A robust implementation should veto the raw operator when \(s<s_{\min}>0\) and replace it with a saturated deceleration or track-deletion rule. This is verifiable by checking finiteness of \(\Delta v\), positive semidefiniteness of \(P\), and existence probability stability.

2. Unscented-weight degeneracy veto.
Counterfactual: the augmented state dimension is \(n\), and the design parameter satisfies \(\kappa=-n\). Then \(n+\kappa=0\), and the sigma-point weights
\[
W^{(0)}=\frac{\kappa}{n+\kappa},
\qquad
W^{(i)}=\frac{\kappa}{2(n+\kappa)}
\]
are undefined. Verifiable test: set \(\kappa=-n\) in a unit test and propagate a Gaussian component through the UKF. Prediction: NaNs or negative weights appear; predicted mean and covariance become invalid; the track’s spatial density cannot be evaluated. The veto condition is formal: require \(n+\kappa\neq 0\), preferably \(n+\kappa>0\) for standard positive weights, and require \(P\) and \(Q\) to be positive semidefinite before taking matrix square roots. This is verifiable by asserting finite weights, finite mean, and finite covariance trace.

3. Zero-velocity map-turn-rate veto.
Counterfactual: a vehicle is stationary or nearly stationary inside a road rectangle, perhaps due to occlusion or traffic jam. The map correction uses a time-to-border term \(s/v\). If \(v\to 0\), that term diverges; if the printed Eq. (27) is used as written, the dimensional ambiguity becomes operationally severe. Verifiable test: simulate a stationary track at the longitudinal border of a rectangle with \(v=0.01\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(s=0.1\ \mathrm{m}\), and a nonzero orientation mismatch \(\Delta\phi\). Prediction: the correction either becomes numerically unstable or, under the unit-consistent form \(\omega=\Delta\phi v/s\), becomes vanishingly small, so the orientation remains biased toward the wrong lane. The source’s bus example illustrates the value of map correction when the vehicle is moving, but it also exposes the stationary limit: road-map orientation helps yaw correction only when there is enough longitudinal motion to define a traversal time. The veto should trigger when \(v<v_{\min}\) or \(s<s_{\min}\), replacing the turn-rate correction with a direct orientation-assignment prior based on the containing rectangle.
