The text on page 220 deals with **Corollary VI**, a critical constraint on the additive nature of learning. Hull posits that if two stimuli (e.g., a tone and flashing lights) are presented together, their combined "habit strength" ($D$) should mathematically sum to produce a robust response. However, he observes a catastrophic failure: when presented separately later, each stimulus elicits a significantly weaker response—sometimes less than half of what simple summation would predict.

This is not merely a quirk of animal psychology; it is an early, phenomenological description of **gradient interference** and **crosstalk** in parallel processing channels. The "afferent neural interaction" Hull describes is the biological precursor to what modern information theory calls channel cross-talk or weight entanglement in neural networks. When two high-intensity signals enter a biological transducer simultaneously, they do not remain perfectly orthogonal; they collide, creating non-linear attenuation. The system optimizes for the dominant signal (the tone), effectively "overshadowing" the weaker one (the lamps).

From a Frontier Reasoning perspective, this is a profound lesson in **signal integrity under compound stress**. If we map this to a complex decision-making system—like a pilot managing multiple instrument failures or an AI parsing overlapping sensor data—the "lamps" represent secondary data streams that get completely suppressed by the "tone" of a primary alarm. The "habit strength" isn't just memory; it's the allocated computational bandwidth. Corollary VI dictates that shared bandwidth leads to degraded independent recall. The "less than half" threshold is the singularity point where the shadowed variable loses all autonomous predictive power. We must therefore treat Corollary VI not as a psychological footnote, but as a fundamental law of information degradation in coupled systems, applicable to biomechanics, market dynamics, and algorithmic stability.

To formalize Hull's qualitative description into a rigorous mathematical operator, we define the **Afferent Interaction Attenuation Operator**.

Let $S_a$ and $S_b$ be two distinct stimulus aggregates (e.g., tone and lamps) conditioned jointly to a reaction $R$. 
According to Hull's standard habit formation (Equation 14 in his work), the theoretical summed habit strength $D_{sum}$ at asymptote is:
$$ D_{sum} = D_{a} + D_{b} $$
Assuming equal conditioning, $D_a = D_b = \frac{D_{sum}}{2}$.

However, Corollary VI introduces a non-linear interaction term $\Gamma_{int}$ representing afferent neural collision. The effective habit strength evoked by $S_a$ alone, denoted $D'_{a}$, is governed by:
$$ D'_{a} = f(D_a) \cdot (1 - \Gamma_{int}(S_a, S_b)) $$
Where $\Gamma_{int} \in [0, 1)$ represents the proportion of signal loss due to overshadowing. 

**Units:** $D$ is measured in **Habs** (Hull's unit of habit strength, roughly equivalent to probability of response per trial). $\Gamma_{int}$ is a dimensionless attenuation factor derived from sensory overlap metrics (SI units: proportional to Neural Firing Rate Crosstalk in spikes/s per Hz).

This constitutes the **Overshadowing Singularity**, where a theoretically conditioned stimulus becomes functionally inert upon isolation.

**Citation:** "If two stimulus aggregates... are later presented separately, the reaction strength evokable by each may be less than habit summation dynamics would indicate, and in extreme cases may be even less than half that of the compound originally conditioned..." [PAGE 220].
