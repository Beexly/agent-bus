The source’s inference chain is observational and multi-hop. First, the dataset is a shot-chart table: each row is a shot with “Shot Distance, Shot Zone, Location X, Location Y, Shot Made Flag” and game/time fields [PAGE 3], collected “during the last two regular seasons, namely, 2014-15 and 2015-16” [PAGE 2]. The first hop is therefore descriptive: aggregate attempts by distance. The paper warns that distance alone is ambiguous because the three-point mark has two radii, stating: “The distance of the three-point line is different at the corners (22 feet) and the crest (23.9 feet)” [PAGE 4]. A density peak near 24 feet cannot by itself separate corner twos from crest threes, so the author moves to spatial zones.

The second hop is a geometric null model. The paper compares the fraction of attempts in a one-foot band just outside the line against the fraction expected under uniform allocation. For corner threes the expected baseline is “50%” [PAGE 4]; for the crest the paper reports a “52%” baseline, writing that “one would expect 52% of the shots be just outside the line” [PAGE 5]. The key empirical result is not subtle: “The most startling result is that the difference between the actual fraction and the baseline is between 35-40%.” [PAGE 5]. The third hop checks whether this spatial discontinuity is mirrored by a make-probability discontinuity. It is not: “there is not any statistically significant difference” between the field-goal percentages just inside and just outside the line [PAGE 5]. That yields the paper’s central gray-zone claim: “two shots with the same level of difficulty can potentially be rewarded with different number of points” [PAGE 5]. The fourth hop converts this into expected points. If \(FGP\) is approximately equal across the boundary, then the outside shot has expected value near \(3FGP\), while the inside shot has expected value near \(2FGP\); the outside location dominates by a factor near \(1.5\). The paper formalizes this with court equity \(E(x,y)\) and states that equality requires “FPGOUT = 0.66 · FPGIN” [PAGE 6]. That is exactly the parity condition \(FGP_{out}=(2/3)FGP_{in}\). The fifth hop explains agent behavior: “players behave exactly as a rational agent would, i.e., bias their shots outside the three-point line” [PAGE 5]. The sixth hop quantifies unused space with fractal correlation dimension. The observed values are left corner \(D_2=0.67\), right corner \(0.7\), and crest \(1.17\), against theoretical/shuffled intervals \([0.96,0.99]\), \([0.97,0.99]\), and \([1.74,1.87]\) [PAGE 7]. The paper summarizes this as “approximately 30% reduction in the dimensionality” [PAGE 7].

The gray-zone audit must separate what is measured from what is inferred. The paper measures attempt fractions, binary makes, distance, and \(x,y\) zones. It does not measure defender distance, shot-clock pressure, score margin, player fatigue, release angle, or biomechanical load. Thus “same level of difficulty” is operationalized only as statistically similar \(FGP\), not as equal biomechanical or perceptual difficulty. A second audit point is the crest baseline: the sentence says the inside one-foot area “covers 52% of the total area examined” and then says one expects “52% of the shots be just outside the line” [PAGE 5]. If the inside area is 52% of the examined area, the outside no-bias share would ordinarily be 48%, not 52%. This may be a wording slip or a different normalization, but it is a real gray-zone item; the safest reading is that the paper uses a reported crest baseline near 52%, and any replication should re-derive the area ratio from the actual court geometry. A third audit point is statistical: “statistically identical” is a non-rejection result under \(\alpha=0.05\) [PAGE 5]; it is not proof of exact equality, and power depends on sample size within each one-foot band. A fourth audit point is the recommendation. The paper searches for a distance with a significant \(FGP\) discontinuity and finds “The only distance where there is a discontinuity (p-value &lt; 0.1) in the field goal percentage is 30 feet. The observed discontinuity (9% change) still provides more equity at the three-point shot, however, the differential is not reduced.” [PAGE 8]. Even if a 9% fall in outside \(FGP\) is assumed, parity requires \(FGP_{out}=0.66FGP_{in}\), i.e. about a 33.3% reduction if inside \(FGP\) is unchanged; a 9% change is not sufficient. The paper also warns that “players are expected to adopt to the new distance and this discontinuity might (quickly or slowly) disappear” [PAGE 8]. That adaptation caveat is the main reason the result should be treated as a design critique, not a permanent law of basketball.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source uses two formal operators. The first is the fractal correlation dimension on a point cloud \(S\) of shot locations. With \(N=|S|\) and indicator \(\mathbf{1}[\cdot]\), a standard estimator is
\[
C(r)=\frac{2}{N(N-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\mathbf{1}\!\left(\|x_i-x_j\|\leq r\right),
\]
where \(x_i,x_j\in S\), \(r\) is a distance in feet, \(C(r)\) is a dimensionless fraction of point pairs, and \(\|\cdot\|\) is Euclidean court distance. The source defines \(C(r)\) as “the fraction of pairs of points from \(S\) that have distance smaller or equal to \(r\)” and states the scaling law “C(r) ∝rD2” for \(r_1 \leq r \leq r_2\) [PAGE 3]. Formally,
\[
C(r)\propto r^{D_2},\qquad r_1\leq r\leq r_2,
\]
with \(D_2\) dimensionless. The source notes that “real objects are finite and hence, Equation (1) holds only over a specific range of scales” [PAGE 3]. For a uniform cloud in the unit square, the source gives \(D_2=2\) over \([r_{\min},1]\), where \(r_{\min}\) is the smallest pairwise distance [PAGE 3].

The second operator is court equity:
\[
E(x,y)=FGP(x,y)\left[2\left(1-I_{3PT}(x,y)\right)+3I_{3PT}(x,y)\right],
\]
where \(FGP(x,y)\in[0,1]\) is the field-goal probability at location \((x,y)\), \(I_{3PT}(x,y)\in\{0,1\}\) is an indicator for a three-point attempt, and \(E(x,y)\) has units of points per attempt. This is the paper’s Equation (2), quoted as “E(x, y) = FGP(x, y) · [(2 · (1 −13P T (x, y))) + (3 · 13P T (x, y))]” [PAGE 6]. If \(I_{3PT}=0\), \(E=2FGP\); if \(I_{3PT}=1\), \(E=3FGP\). Setting \(E_{out}=E_{in}\) and assuming equal \(FGP\) gives \(3FGP_{out}=2FGP_{in}\), hence
\[
FGP_{out}=\frac{2}{3}FGP_{in}\approx 0.66FGP_{in}.
\]
The source states the same condition as “FPGOUT = 0.66 · FPGIN” [PAGE 6].

The reported geometric baselines are: corner one-foot band, \(50\%\) outside versus \(50\%\) inside [PAGE 4]; crest band, a reported \(52\%\) outside baseline [PAGE 5]; and the 17-foot mid-range control, “given the arc shape and the radius of the zone this is 51.4%” [PAGE 9]. The reported fractal values are: left corner \(D_2=0.67\) versus \([0.96,0.99]\); right corner \(0.7\) versus \([0.97,0.99]\); crest \(1.17\) versus \([1.74,1.87]\) [PAGE 7]. The paper’s statistical convention is “a confidence level of α = 0.05 unless otherwise specified” [PAGE 5], while the 30-foot \(FGP\) discontinuity uses a weaker threshold, \(p&lt;0.1\) [PAGE 8].

Assumptions: shots are treated as point locations; the one-foot zones are geometrically comparable after area normalization; the no-bias counterfactual is uniform allocation within the examined area; \(FGP\) is approximately constant within each one-foot zone; shot outcomes are treated as independent enough for proportion tests; and the scaling range \(r_1\) to \(r_2\) is selected where the log-log relation is approximately linear. Breakdown regimes: (i) for \(r<r_{\min}\), \(C(r)=0\) and \(\log C(r)\) is undefined, so \(D_2\) cannot be estimated; (ii) for \(r\) approaching the zone diameter, \(C(r)\to 1\) and saturation biases \(D_2\) downward; (iii) in narrow corner zones the point cloud is nearly one-dimensional, so the shuffled baseline is not 2 but about \(0.96\) to \(0.99\) [PAGE 7]; (iv) small attempt counts create binomial variance in \(FGP\) and unstable \(D_2\); (v) multiple zones and seasons create multiple-comparison risk; (vi) “statistically identical” is a failure to reject, not an equivalence proof; (vii) the equity parity formula is undefined or unstable when \(FGP_{in}=0\), when a zone has zero attempts, or when \(FGP\) is near the 0/1 boundary.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s historical frame is explicit: it plots three-point attempts “since the 1980-81 season” and notes that the recent increase is comparable only to “1995 when the distance to the three-point line was shortened” [PAGE 1]. That is a retrocausal structure: the 2014-16 spatial equilibrium is a downstream response to a rule boundary fixed earlier, and the 1995 shortening is a natural experiment showing that players re-optimize when the boundary moves. The paper also ties the current regime to the Golden State Warriors’ three-point-heavy style and a record by Klay Thompson [PAGE 1], but the deeper genealogy is methodological. Spatial shot-chart analysis descends from Reich et al., Goldsberry’s CourtVision, and Chang et al. on shot quality [PAGE 9]; fractal analysis descends from Schroeder’s treatment of fractals, chaos, and power laws [PAGE 9]. The paper’s novelty is joining those lineages: shot charts supply the point cloud, and correlation dimension supplies a dimensionless measure of how fully the court’s two-dimensional surface is used.

The multi-week traps are adaptation, nonstationarity, and rule anticipation. First, a moved three-point boundary changes incentives immediately, but shooting skill, coaching schemes, and defensive counters adjust over weeks, seasons, and player cohorts. The paper itself says “players are expected to adopt to the new distance and this discontinuity might (quickly or slowly) disappear” [PAGE 8]. Therefore a short post-change window can overstate the new discontinuity, while a long window can erase it. Second, the paper’s two-season sample is long enough to show a stable bias but not long enough to identify a permanent law; the record-breaking seasons may be a transient equilibrium. Third, rule anticipation is a retrocausal trap: if teams expect a line move, they may begin practicing from the new distance before the rule takes effect, contaminating a simple before-after design. Fourth, physical constraints feed back: a 30-foot line would require court-dimension changes, “especially its width” [PAGE 8], so the rule cannot be evaluated independently of arena geometry. Fifth, the 30-foot candidate is based on a \(p&lt;0.1\) discontinuity and a 9% change [PAGE 8], which is weaker evidence than the \(\alpha=0.05\) standard used elsewhere [PAGE 5]; a multi-week or multi-season replication should pre-register the test and correct for multiple distances. Sixth, the control case at 17 feet is valuable because it shows the bias is not generic to all two-point areas: “the 95% confidence intervals of the observed fractions includes the baseline fraction!” [PAGE 9]. That control strengthens the three-point-line interpretation, but it is only one control distance; additional non-line arcs would be needed before claiming that only the three-point boundary produces this effect.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive layer has four agents: the shooter, the coach, the defender, and the league rule-setter. The shooter maximizes expected points; the coach selects schemes; the defender counters preferred locations; the league chooses the boundary that all three respond to. The paper already adopts a coach’s perspective, saying the behavior “is a response to game design inefficiencies and is completely rational from a coaching perspective” [PAGE 2], and it describes the player as a “rational agent” who biases shots outside the line [PAGE 5]. A recursive model therefore predicts a strategic equilibrium: shooters drift to the high-reward side, defenders overplay that side, and coaches search for counter-locations. The observed 35-40% excess outside the line [PAGE 5] is the net aggregate of that recursion, not necessarily a pure preference.

“Crisis habit regression” is the first counter-trap. Under late-clock or high-pressure situations, players may revert to familiar mid-range habits or take forced shots that do not reflect season-long optimization. If those crisis shots cluster inside the line, they would attenuate the measured outside bias. The dataset contains “Period, Minutes Remaining, Seconds Remaining” [PAGE 2], so a clock-stratified audit is technically possible, but the paper does not report one; it also does not include score margin or defender distance, so “crisis” cannot be fully identified. The observed bias is therefore an average over strategic and forced attempts. A second counter-trap is the line move itself: if the league moves the boundary to 30 feet, the old discontinuity does not simply translate; players and defenders re-optimize, and the paper warns the discontinuity “might (quickly or slowly) disappear” [PAGE 8]. The third factor is the clock. End-of-period attempts are a mixture of designed plays and desperation heaves; without separating them, one cannot know whether the fractal reduction is a structural spatial preference or partly a time-pressure artifact. The recursive prediction is conditional: if the bias is incentive-driven, it should survive clock controls and reappear around any newly priced boundary; if it is a habit or clock artifact, it should weaken when those strata are removed. The paper’s 17-foot control, where the confidence interval includes the 51.4% baseline [PAGE 9], supports the incentive interpretation, but it does not substitute for a full clock-and-score stratified model.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical-layer veto is partly allowed and partly decisive. The court geometry is real: corners are 22 feet, the crest is 23.9 feet [PAGE 4], and a 30-foot recommendation would require court-dimension changes, “especially its width” [PAGE 8]. Any rule proposal that ignores the physical boundary is vetoed by the source’s own caveat. The biomechanical-layer veto is stricter. The source tuple includes shot distance, zone, \(x,y\), and make flag [PAGE 3], but it does not include release angle, joint load, fatigue, muscle activation, defender proximity, or player-tracking kinematics. Therefore any claim that the inside and outside shots are “biomechanically identical” is vetoed: the paper only shows statistically similar \(FGP\), which is a coarse outcome probability, not a biomechanical equivalence. The weather-layer veto is absolute for this source: the data are NBA indoor regular-season shot charts, and no weather, temperature, humidity, or arena-environment variable appears in the tuple [PAGE 2-3]. A weather explanation for the spatial bias is therefore unsupported and should be rejected rather than treated as a competing hypothesis. The statistical-layer veto is also important: “there is not any statistically significant difference” [PAGE 5] cannot be upgraded to “the probabilities are equal.” The paper’s own language is “statistically identical” [PAGE 1], which is a non-rejection statement under \(\alpha=0.05\) [PAGE 5]; it does not exclude a small true difference. The fractal-layer veto is similarly bounded: \(D_2\) is a dimensionless description of point-cloud scaling, not a causal biomechanical or psychological variable. The observed 30% reduction [PAGE 7] is valid only inside the chosen scaling range and zone geometry; it cannot by itself prove that the line “causes” the reduction. The cross-layer verdict is therefore: physical court geometry can veto a rule change; biomechanical and weather explanations are vetoed by missing or inapplicable data; and causal claims beyond expected-point incentives are not licensed by the source.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Line relocation with adaptation. Counterfactual: move the three-point boundary to 30 feet and collect two or more subsequent seasons of one-foot inside/outside zones. Verifiable test: pre-register a two-proportion test at \(\alpha=0.05\) for the fraction of attempts outside versus the geometric no-bias baseline, and estimate \(D_2\) against a uniform shuffle baseline. Prediction if the paper’s incentive mechanism is stable: the outside fraction should initially exceed baseline and \(D_2\) should remain below the shuffled interval, with an effect in the paper’s 35-40% range [PAGE 5] or a dimensionality reduction near 30% [PAGE 7]. Veto prediction: if the 95% confidence interval includes the baseline and \(D_2\) falls inside the shuffled interval after adaptation, then the strong “line distance causes a persistent discontinuity” claim is vetoed or must be restated as a transient effect. This is exactly the adaptation trap the paper flags: the discontinuity “might (quickly or slowly) disappear” [PAGE 8].

Scenario 2: Mid-range specificity control. Counterfactual: repeat the one-foot zone test around the 17-foot arc and around additional non-line distances with the same area-normalized baseline method. Verifiable test: the paper reports a 51.4% baseline and a 95% confidence interval that includes it at 17 feet [PAGE 9]. Prediction if the effect is three-point-line-specific: non-line arcs should also include their geometric baselines, while the three-point zones should show the 35-40% excess [PAGE 5]. Veto prediction: if a non-line control produces the same large excess with a confidence interval excluding its baseline, then “specific to the three-point line” is vetoed; the effect would instead look like a general mid-range or shot-selection artifact.

Scenario 3: Equity-parity test at a candidate boundary. Counterfactual: estimate \(FGP_{in}\) and \(FGP_{out}\) in adjacent one-foot bands at a proposed line and test \(H_0: FGP_{out}=(2/3)FGP_{in}\), equivalently \(E_{out}=E_{in}\). Verifiable prediction from the current data: because \(FGP\) just inside and outside the existing line is not significantly different [PAGE 5], the current equity ratio should be near \(3/2\) in favor of the outside shot; at 30 feet the reported 9% change [PAGE 8] is not enough for parity, since parity requires about a 33.3% lower outside \(FGP\) if inside \(FGP\) is unchanged. Veto prediction: if a candidate boundary yields a confidence interval for \(FGP_{out}/FGP_{in}\) that contains \(0.66\) and a confidence interval for \(E_{out}/E_{in}\) that contains 1, the equity critique is satisfied at that boundary; if not, the recommendation fails its own parity criterion. A second veto check is the \(p\)-value standard: a candidate supported only at \(p&lt;0.1\) [PAGE 8] should not be treated as equivalent to the \(\alpha=0.05\) evidence used elsewhere [PAGE 5] without pre-registration and replication.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
