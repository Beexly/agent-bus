Tolman’s finding—that animals who took the correct side more frequently (Class V) did not “more strongly ‘stamp in’” that habit, and broke it down just as easily as those with weaker initial preferences (Class I)—fundamentally contradicts Thorndike’s Law of Effect. The trial-and-error model assumes that each rewarded choice incrementally strengthens a fixed S-R bond (a cumulative summation process). If this were true, Class V animals should exhibit significantly greater resistance to extinction or forced reversal than Class I animals. The fact that they do not implies that frequency alone is insufficient to create a stable, rigid behavioral structure.

This forces a derivation of a hidden variable: cognitive mapping or expectation-based learning. The animal is not simply accumulating reinforcement tokens; it is forming a hypothesis about the environment's topology. When the reward contingency changes (as in the forced reversal experiments described), the animal isn't dismantling a deeply etched neural groove but rather updating an incorrect map. This aligns perfectly with modern information theory: the value of a learning episode is not the raw count of reinforcements, but the *reduction in uncertainty* (entropy) about the state space. A highly frequent, predictable action (like always going right when it works) provides minimal new information once the pattern is established, leading to informational saturation and a plateau in "habit strength." Conversely, an infrequent action that suddenly yields a reward provides high informational gain, rapidly updating the posterior probability of that action's efficacy.

The "forcing" experiments mentioned are the critical crucible. By compelling the animal to take the future "incorrect" path more often, Tolman severs the correlation between motor execution frequency and eventual correctness. This creates a dissociation between kinematic history and goal-directed utility. From a dynamical systems perspective, the system is being pushed across a bifurcation boundary. The attractor basin for the "forced" behavior is artificially deepened through repetition, yet it remains functionally fragile because it lacks congruence with the underlying reward geometry (the "cognitive map"). Thus, when the constraint is removed, the system doesn't exhibit the sluggish relaxation expected of a heavily reinforced habit; instead, it snaps back to the optimal trajectory almost instantaneously—a phase transition driven by the reassertion of the true topological gradient.

Let $S$ denote the set of possible states (maze configurations) and $A$ the set of actions (e.g., \{Left, Right\}). Under the classical Trial-and-Error (T&E) doctrine à la Thorndike, the "habit strength" $H(a)$ for action $a$ is postulated to increase monotonically with each reinforcement event $R$. Formally, the T&E update rule is:
$$ H_{t+1}(a) = H_t(a) + \alpha \cdot \mathbb{I}(R_t=1 \land A_t=a) $$
where $\alpha > 0$ is a fixed learning rate and $\mathbb{I}$ is the indicator function. This implies $H(a) \propto N_a$, the total count of times action $a$ was rewarded.

**Tolman's Empirical Anomaly:** Let $Class_V$ be subjects with high $N_{correct}$ (frequent initial correct choices) and $Class_I$ with low $N_{correct}$. The T&E model predicts that upon contingency reversal, the time to switch $T_{switch}$ should satisfy $T_{switch}(Class_V) \gg T_{switch}(Class_I)$ due to greater entrenched $H(correct)$. However, Tolman reports $T_{switch}(Class_V) \approx T_{switch}(Class_I)$, implying the derivative $\frac{\partial T_{switch}}{\partial N_a} \approx 0$. This singularity breaks the T&E equation's core assumption.

**Information-Theoretic Resolution:** We replace habit strength with a posterior probability distribution $P(A|S)$ over actions given state. Learning is driven by reduction in Shannon entropy $H(S) = -\sum P(s)\log P(s)$. The informational gain $\Delta I$ from an observation $(a, r)$ is:
$$ \Delta I = D_{KL}\left( P(A|S, \text{history}) \parallel P(A|S, \text{history} \cup \{(a,r)\}) \right) $$
where $D_{KL}$ is the Kullback-Leibler divergence. Crucially, $\Delta I$ diminishes as $P(a|s)$ approaches 1 (certainty), explaining why high-frequency correct choices yield diminishing returns in "strength."

**Forced Exploration & Bifurcation:** In the forced-path experiment, the agent's policy $\pi_t$ is externally constrained: $\pi_t(a_{forced}) = 1$. This generates a sequence of high-surprise events (low prior probability for $a_{forced}$ if the map was accurate). The cumulative surprise is bounded, but the resulting posterior over the environmental model (the "cognitive map") updates significantly. Upon release, the agent executes the action maximizing expected utility under the updated map. The transition is discontinuous: define order parameter $m = P(A_{correct})$. At $t_{release}$, $m$ jumps from near 0 (during forcing) to near 1 (post-release) without gradual decay proportional to forcing duration—a hallmark of first-order phase transitions in decision landscapes.

**Units & Assumptions:**
- $H(a)$: Arbitrary psychological units (abandoned due to falsification).
- $P(A|S)$: Probability (dimensionless, [0,1]).
- $\Delta I$: Bits or nats of information.
- Assumption: The environment is stationary during a single block, allowing consistent posterior convergence. Breakdown regime occurs when environmental volatility exceeds the agent's ability to distinguish signal from noise, leading to chaotic flipping between policies.

When forced down the "incorrect" path, the rat does not update its motor program to favor that turn permanently. Instead, it maintains a counterfactual simulation: *"If I had chosen the other way earlier, I would have been rewarded."* This second-order reasoning—thinking about what could have been—is the bedrock of mental time travel and planning.

Even after repeated forced errors, they do not fall into a learned helplessness state or adopt the forced path as a new reflex. Their "purposefulness" acts as a counter-trap mechanism: the cognitive map provides a stable reference frame that overrides the immediate sensory-motor feedback loop demanding conformity to recent experience. The clock here is the pressure of extinction or punishment; the counter-trap is the persistence of the latent expectancy that guides behavior back to the goal state efficiently.
