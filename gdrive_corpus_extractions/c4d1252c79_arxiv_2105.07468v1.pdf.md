First, the paper states the static-world assumption that dominates dense mapping: “Most dense mapping systems, however, operate on the assumption of a static environment” [PAGE 1 of 7]. Second, it explains the standard object-centric alternative for multiple dynamic objects: each object is reconstructed as “a separate surfel cloud or Truncated Signed Distance Function (TSDF) volume” and the map is “a collection of multiple 3D models and their associated poses” [PAGE 1 of 7]. Fourth, the proposed TSDF++ changes the indexing layer rather than the basic TSDF measurement: a global map volume indexes per-object TSDF volumes, with each global voxel storing an active object model and, if present, an inactive or occluded object model [PAGE 4 of 7]. Fifth, the active/inactive state is maintained by an integer confidence score: agreement with the active object increases the score by one unit, disagreement decreases it by one unit, and at zero the incoming object becomes active with confidence re-initialized to one [PAGE 5 of 7]. Sixth, pose updates deactivate the moved object from global voxels, translate its TSDF values by the estimated rigid transform, and then activate the translated object at destination voxels [PAGE 5 of 7]. Seventh, the empirical demonstration isolates map representation from segmentation noise by using ground-truth segmentation on ToyCar3; the baseline TSDF shows “holes in the table surface” after occluders are removed, while TSDF++ reveals an intact tabletop [PAGE 6 of 7].

The gray-zone audit is where the source becomes more nuanced than a simple “layered TSDF solves occlusion” claim. The paper does not claim unbounded layer depth. It explicitly says, “we limit the number of object surfaces stored at each voxel to two” [PAGE 4 of 7]. Therefore any inference that TSDF++ handles arbitrary clutter is a counter-trap: the formulation is a two-surface active/inactive register, not an unbounded occlusion stack. The confidence score is also not described as a calibrated probability; it is an integer score that can be increased or decreased by one unit per fused observation [PAGE 5 of 7]. Thus treating it as a posterior probability or information-theoretic certainty is not supported by the text. A second gray zone is the fusion flip: the paper says if confidence reaches zero, the incoming object volume becomes the new active object volume and confidence is re-initialized to one [PAGE 5 of 7]. It does not, in the quoted fusion paragraph, spell out every bookkeeping detail of whether the previously active object is always demoted into the inactive slot at that exact instant. The two-layer structure implies a demotion path, but the source’s explicit wording is about the new active object and the re-initialized confidence. A third gray zone is geometric interpolation: translation of TSDF values within a discretized grid has “no guarantee of a one-to-one mapping between source and target voxel centers,” and trilinear interpolation is used only where “eight valid neighbouring voxels” exist [PAGE 5 of 7]. That is a hard support condition; large translations, thin surfaces, or missing data can create destination voxels with no valid interpolation support. Therefore the paper’s strongest occlusion-preservation evidence is conditional on accurate segmentation; it is not a blanket proof of robustness to Mask R-CNN contour errors, missed masks, articulated non-convex objects, or over-segmentation.

The formal operator chain must keep units separate. The TSDF value is a signed distance in meters, truncated at a threshold in meters. The paper reports a 1 cm voxel and truncation distance of ten times the voxel size, i.e. 0.1 m [PAGE 5 of 7]. The ICP objective is a sum of squared point-to-plane residuals in square meters. The confidence is an integer count, not a probability. The segmentation overlap threshold is a dimensionless fraction set at 80% [PAGE 4 of 7]. The runtime is approximately 1 Hz [PAGE 6 of 7]. There are no information-theoretic bits or calibrated probability densities stated in the source. The breakdown regimes are exact: two-surface capacity exhaustion when more than two object surfaces require the same voxel; confidence flip when repeated disagreeing observations drive the integer score to zero; ICP rank deficiency when correspondences or surface normals do not constrain the rigid transform; trilinear support failure when fewer than eight valid source neighbors exist; and segmentation failure when masks bleed, undershoot, or miss articulated objects [PAGE 4 of 7, PAGE 5 of 7, PAGE 6 of 7]. This is the correct audit frame: TSDF++ preserves occluded surfaces under its stated two-layer, rigid-object, known-camera-pose, sufficient-interpolation, and segmentation-accuracy assumptions, but it does not repeal those assumptions.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines the traditional TSDF representation as “a voxel grid in which each voxel stores the projective signed distance to the closest observed surface, truncated up to a threshold. The sign denotes whether the voxel center lies behind or in front of the surface” [PAGE 3 of 7]. Formally, for a voxel center \(v\), let \(d(v)\) be the projective signed distance in meters and let \(\mu\) be the truncation distance in meters. The source does not print a closed-form truncation function, so the defensible operator is:

\[
\operatorname{TSDF}(v)=\operatorname{trunc}\!\left(d(v);\mu\right),
\qquad
[d(v)]=\mathrm{m},\quad [\mu]=\mathrm{m}.
\]

The experimental setting fixes:

\[
\Delta x = 0.01\,\mathrm{m},\qquad
\mu = 10\,\Delta x = 0.1\,\mathrm{m},
\]

because “the voxel size has been set to 1 cm and the truncation distance to ten times the voxel size” [PAGE 5 of 7]. The breakdown regime for this operator is the truncation boundary: signed-distance information is saturated beyond \(\mu\), and the representation does not encode arbitrary far-field geometry. The source’s claimed advantage is not that truncation disappears; it is that a global map volume indexes “multiple smaller per-object TSDF volumes” so that “given a voxel in the global map volume, the corresponding TSDF voxel in one of the object volumes can be directly accessed using the exact same grid coordinates” [PAGE 4 of 7]. The per-object volumes “share the same resolution as the global map volume” [PAGE 4 of 7]. Thus the indexing operator is effectively identity in grid coordinates:

\[
v_{\text{obj}} = v_{\text{global}},
\qquad
\Delta x_{\text{obj}} = \Delta x_{\text{global}}.
\]

The active/inactive bookkeeping is stated as follows: “Each voxel in the global map volume keeps track of which object model is active at the corresponding 3D location and which object model, if any, is inactive or occluded. Additionally, for both the active and the inactive object, the voxel stores an associated integer confidence score” [PAGE 4 of 7]. Let \(a_t(v)\) be the active object identifier at voxel \(v\) and time \(t\), let \(\iota_t(v)\) be the incoming object identifier, and let \(c_t(v)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\) be the integer confidence. The source’s fusion rule is: “If the ID of the incoming object segment agrees with the ID of the currently active object model the corresponding confidence score is increased by one unit, else it is decreased by one unit. If the confidence reaches zero, the object volume corresponding to the incoming object ID becomes the new active object volume at that voxel, and the corresponding confidence score is re-initialized to one” [PAGE 5 of 7]. A faithful piecewise representation is:

\[
\left(c_{t+1}(v),a_{t+1}(v)\right)
=
\left\{
\begin{array}{l}
\left(c_t(v)+1,\;a_t(v)\right),\quad \text{if } \iota_t(v)=a_t(v),\\
\left(c_t(v)-1,\;a_t(v)\right),\quad \text{if } \iota_t(v)\ne a_t(v)\text{ and }c_t(v)>1,\\
\left(1,\;\iota_t(v)\right),\quad \text{if } \iota_t(v)\ne a_t(v)\text{ and }c_t(v)=1.
\end{array}
\right.
\]

Here \([c_t(v)]\) is an integer count, not a probability and not an information quantity. The breakdown regime is the zero-confidence flip: repeated wrong or missing segmentations can drive the integer score downward and change the active object; the source explicitly warns that the representation’s “ability to reconstruct multiple implicit object surfaces at any given location in the map comes at the cost of decreased robustness towards wrong or missing per-frame object segmentations” [PAGE 6 of 7]. A second capacity breakdown is exact: “we limit the number of object surfaces stored at each voxel to two” [PAGE 4 of 7]. If three or more distinct object surfaces require the same voxel, the two-layer active/inactive register is insufficient, even though the paper notes the representation “can be easily extended to store more layers” [PAGE 4 of 7].

Pose tracking uses the point-to-plane ICP objective printed as Equation (1):

\[
E(T_O)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\left(T_O s_k-o_k\right)\cdot n_{o_k}\right)^2.
\]

The source defines \(s_k\) as “a point in the 3D segment,” \(o_k\) and \(n_{o_k}\) as “the corresponding vertex and local surface normal in the mesh extracted from the object model TSDF,” and \(N\) as “the total number of points in the segment point cloud for which a corresponding point could be identified in the target set of mesh vertices” [PAGE 5 of 7]. Units: \(s_k,o_k\in\mathbb{R}^3\) in meters; \(T_O\) is a rigid transform mapping meters to meters; \(n_{o_k}\) is a dimensionless unit normal; each dot product has units of meters; each squared residual has units of \(\mathrm{m}^2\); therefore \([E(T_O)]=\mathrm{m}^2\). The optimizer is Levenberg-Marquardt [PAGE 5 of 7]. Breakdown regimes: no correspondences, insufficient normal diversity, planar or symmetric geometry causing rank deficiency in the normal system, and non-rigid deformation that violates the single rigid \(T_O\) assumption.

Map translation is not a continuous exact resampling. The source states: “When translating TSDF values from a source location to a destination location within a discretized grid, there is no guarantee of a one-to-one mapping between source and target voxel centers” [PAGE 5 of 7]. It adopts “trilinear interpolation ... at those destination voxel centers for which the corresponding source location offers eight valid neighbouring voxels required by the computation” [PAGE 5 of 7]. The support condition is therefore:

\[
\mathcal{N}_{8}(v_{\text{source}}) \text{ must be valid for destination } v_{\text{dest}},
\]

where \(\mathcal{N}_8\) denotes the eight neighboring voxel samples needed by trilinear interpolation. Breakdown: if fewer than eight valid neighbors exist, no destination TSDF value is computed by that rule, producing holes or missing translated geometry. The pose-update order is also explicit: first the object is “deactivated from all of the voxels in the global map volume,” then the object volume TSDF values are translated, then “the global map voxels in correspondence of the destination location are all updated to deactivate the previously active object model and instead activate the translated one” [PAGE 5 of 7].

The segmentation fusion operator uses a combined geometric-semantic scheme. The RGB mask comes from Mask R-CNN, while the depth frame is processed by a “purely geometric normal-based approach” [PAGE 4 of 7]. The two outputs are fused by pairwise 2D overlap normalized by segment-cloud point count:

\[
\rho_{i,j}=\frac{\left|P_i\cap M_j\right|}{\left|P_i\right|},
\qquad
\rho_{i,j}\ge \tau_{\text{overlap}}=0.80,
\]

where \(P_i\) is the segment cloud and \(M_j\) is the predicted object-instance mask; \(\rho_{i,j}\) is dimensionless. The source says the overlap is “normalized by the number of points in the segment cloud” and that “whenever such overlap is higher than a threshold \(\tau_{\text{overlap}}\), which we set at 80%, the segmented cloud is matched to the corresponding object instance” [PAGE 4 of 7]. Breakdown: Mask R-CNN masks “either bleed into the background or undershoot with respect to the true outline of an object,” while geometric segments “often exhibit over-segmentation of non-convex articulated object categories” [PAGE 4 of 7]. The paper’s controlled occlusion experiment uses “the accompanying ground truth per-frame segmentation” to isolate map representation from segmentation noise [PAGE 5 of 7], so the strongest TSDF++ result is conditional on that isolation.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is traceable in the references and related-work section. The foundational volumetric ancestor is Curless and Levoy’s TSDF-style range-image fusion [8], cited in the related work as the implicit volumetric representation underlying KinectFusion [PAGE 2 of 7, PAGE 7 of 7]. KinectFusion [1] is described as the first framework to demonstrate commodity RGB-D indoor reconstruction in real time on a GPU, relying on “an implicit TSDF-based volumetric representation” [PAGE 2 of 7]. Voxblox [22] is the implementation ancestor for the TSDF-based voxel hashing extension used by TSDF++ [PAGE 4 of 7, PAGE 7 of 7]. The instance-aware static-object ancestor is Grinvald et al. [14], which augmented TSDF voxels with object-instance information for multiple static objects in a single volume; TSDF++ explicitly extends that idea to dynamic objects by translating TSDF values within the volume [PAGE 3 of 7, PAGE 4 of 7]. The dynamic multi-object lineage includes Co-Fusion [2], MaskFusion [3], MID-Fusion [4], EM-Fusion [5], and larger outdoor dynamic mapping [6], [7]; the paper summarizes that these store objects in “separate and independent reconstruction volumes” [PAGE 3 of 7]. The non-rigid lineage is separate: DynamicFusion [16] and follow-ups [17]–[20] reconstruct a single deformable body, not multiple rigid objects [PAGE 2 of 7]. Thus TSDF++ is retrocausally positioned between static instance-aware TSDF mapping and multi-object dynamic mapping: it keeps the single-volume indexing idea from the static lineage and adds active/inactive object layers for temporarily occluded surfaces in the dynamic lineage.

The multi-week traps are the places where a reader can over-extend the genealogy. Trap one: assuming that because TSDF++ is “the first multi-object TSDF formulation” that can store multiple implicit surfaces at a voxel [PAGE 2 of 7], it has unlimited object-layer depth. The paper immediately bounds this: two surfaces per voxel [PAGE 4 of 7]. Trap two: assuming that a single global volume eliminates all scaling problems. The paper argues that it avoids repeated ray casting through every object volume and removes explicit occlusion handling [PAGE 2 of 7, PAGE 3 of 7], but it still uses a scene-size global map volume and per-object volumes, and the reported runtime is approximately 1 Hz [PAGE 6 of 7]. Trap three: reading the ToyCar3 result as a full-pipeline segmentation robustness result. The occlusion-preservation experiment uses ground-truth segmentation [PAGE 5 of 7]; the full pipeline is “heavily impacted” by segmentation inaccuracies [PAGE 6 of 7]. Trap four: conflating TSDF++ with non-rigid DynamicFusion-style reconstruction. TSDF++ tracks a rigid \(T_O\) per object [PAGE 5 of 7]; it does not model deformable geometry. Trap five: treating the confidence score as a probabilistic data-association scheme. EM-Fusion is described as offering “a probabilistic data association scheme” [PAGE 2 of 7], but TSDF++’s score is an integer confidence increment/decrement rule [PAGE 5 of 7]. These traps are multi-week because they survive superficial reading: the high-level phrase “multiple object surfaces at each voxel” sounds unbounded, while the actual operator is a two-layer active/inactive register with an integer flip rule.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. The default habit in dense mapping is the static-world assumption: “Most dense mapping systems, however, operate on the assumption of a static environment” [PAGE 1 of 7]. Under crisis, that habit regresses to the standard TSDF behavior: moving object geometry is translated into the map and overwrites whatever surface was stored at the destination, so a previously reconstructed tabletop is corrupted by a moving laptop or toy car. The paper’s baseline experiment shows exactly this: the standard TSDF map reveals “holes in the table surface” after the occluding objects are removed [PAGE 6 of 7]. The recursive failure mode is that the system’s own past reconstruction is treated as disposable once a new observation arrives. TSDF++ is designed to counter that regression by keeping an inactive layer for the occluded surface and re-activating it when the occluder moves away [PAGE 4 of 7, PAGE 5 of 7]. But the habit can still regress when segmentation is wrong: if the incoming object ID disagrees with the active ID, the confidence decreases; at zero, the incoming object becomes active and confidence resets to one [PAGE 5 of 7]. A sustained wrong segmentation can therefore teach the map the wrong active surface, and the “memory” of the occluded surface is only as good as the two-layer bookkeeping and the confidence history.

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is over-trusting the layer metaphor. A reader may imagine that every occluded surface is preserved indefinitely, but the paper says “we limit the number of object surfaces stored at each voxel to two” [PAGE 4 of 7]. The second counter-trap is treating the integer confidence as a calibrated probability. The source calls it “an associated integer confidence score” [PAGE 4 of 7] and defines only unit increments and decrements [PAGE 5 of 7]; it does not give a likelihood model, a probability density, or an information-theoretic unit. A third counter-trap is assuming the system can resolve occlusion without any visibility logic. TSDF++ removes the need for an “explicit occlusion handling strategy” at the object-volume level [PAGE 2 of 7, PAGE 3 of 7], but it still relies on active/inactive indexing, confidence flips, and the known camera pose. The recursive theory-of-mind point is that the operator’s model of the system must include the system’s model of occlusion: TSDF++ does not know “visibility” as a semantic fact; it knows active and inactive object IDs at voxels, plus confidence counts.

Clock. The temporal clock matters in three places. First, pose update order: deactivate the moved object, translate its TSDF values, then activate the translated object at destination voxels [PAGE 5 of 7]. If the clock is interrupted between deactivation and activation, the global map has a temporary state where the object is not active; the paper’s pipeline assumes the update completes in order. Second, fusion clock: confidence is updated per incoming frame, and a flip occurs only when the integer reaches zero [PAGE 5 of 7]. A single wrong frame does not necessarily flip the active object; repeated wrong frames can. Third, runtime clock: the full pipeline processes 960x540 RGB-D frames at “approximately 1 Hz” [PAGE 6 of 7]. At that rate, an object can move substantially between frames, making the trilinear interpolation support condition and ICP correspondence step more fragile. The recursive self-model is therefore: the system’s memory of an occluded surface is not timeless; it is a function of update order, confidence accumulation, frame rate, and segmentation quality. The correct counter-trap is to ask not “does TSDF++ preserve occlusion?” but “under which clock, layer count, confidence history, and segmentation regime does it preserve occlusion?”
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto proof. The physical layer imposes hard constraints that no map formulation can repeal. The TSDF is a truncated signed distance in meters, with \(\mu=0.1\,\mathrm{m}\) in the reported experiment [PAGE 5 of 7]. The global map volume indexes per-object volumes at the same resolution and “exact same grid coordinates” [PAGE 4 of 7]. The trilinear translation requires “eight valid neighbouring voxels” at the source location [PAGE 5 of 7]. Therefore a physical veto applies when a destination voxel has fewer than eight valid source neighbors, when the object translation is large relative to the grid, or when the required surface is beyond the truncation band. The two-layer capacity is also a physical/representational veto: if more than two distinct object surfaces occupy the same voxel, the stated implementation cannot preserve all of them because “we limit the number of object surfaces stored at each voxel to two” [PAGE 4 of 7]. The paper’s own baseline comparison proves the failure mode of the non-layered TSDF: after removing the toy cars, the standard TSDF shows “holes in the table surface” despite prior reconstruction [PAGE 6 of 7]. TSDF++ avoids that specific failure under its assumptions, but the physical veto remains for >2 layers, missing interpolation support, and truncation saturation.

Biomechanical veto proof. The paper’s tracking operator is a single rigid transform \(T_O\) per object, estimated by point-to-plane ICP [PAGE 5 of 7]. The objective \(E(T_O)\) assumes that the segment points \(s_k\) are registered to a rigid object model by one \(T_O\). Therefore non-rigid deformation, articulated motion within a single object, or soft-body shape change is outside the operator. The paper itself notes that the geometric segmentation scheme “often exhibit[s] over-segmentation of non-convex articulated object categories” and that Mask R-CNN masks can bleed or undershoot [PAGE 4 of 7]. The related-work lineage separates non-rigid reconstruction into DynamicFusion-style methods that capture “a single deformable body moving in a non-rigid manner” [PAGE 2 of 7]. Thus a biomechanical veto applies: if the target is a human body, a deforming cloth, or an articulated object whose parts move relative to one another, TSDF++’s rigid \(T_O\) and per-object volume model are not a validated representation. The paper’s experiments are on the synthetic ToyCar3 sequence with rigid toy cars and a tabletop [PAGE 5 of 7, PAGE 6 of 7], so there is no biomechanical evidence to override this veto.

Weather veto proof. The source provides no weather model, no outdoor weather dataset, and no rain, fog, snow, dust, or illumination-degradation experiment. The evaluation is on “the synthetic ToyCar3 sequence” [PAGE 5 of 7] and the full pipeline runtime is reported on that sequence [PAGE 6 of 7]. The related work mentions larger outdoor dynamic reconstruction [6], [7], but TSDF++ itself is not evaluated under weather. Therefore a weather claim is vetoed as out-of-domain: the paper cannot support an assertion that TSDF++ preserves occluded surfaces under weather-induced depth noise, scattering, wet surfaces, or lighting extremes. The only defensible statement is that weather would enter through the RGB-D sensor and segmentation layers, and the paper already shows that segmentation inaccuracies “heavily impact” reconstruction and decrease tracking performance [PAGE 6 of 7]. That is a sensor/segmentation coupling veto, not evidence of weather robustness.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Two-layer capacity counterfactual. Construct a tabletop with three rigid occluders that sequentially cover the same global voxel region, then remove all three and compare the reconstructed tabletop against the pre-occlusion mesh. The source states the implementation stores at most two object surfaces per voxel: “we limit the number of object surfaces stored at each voxel to two” [PAGE 4 of 7]. Prediction: TSDF++ will preserve the tabletop through one or two occluders better than the standard TSDF, but under three distinct surfaces requiring the same voxel, at least one previously stored surface will not be recoverable from the two-layer active/inactive register. Verifiable metric: count global voxels where more than two distinct object IDs are observed across the sequence; after removal, measure tabletop completeness and surface error against the pre-occlusion reconstruction. Veto prediction: if the three-surface voxel count is nonzero, full preservation is falsified; TSDF++ may still outperform baseline but cannot satisfy an unbounded-layer claim.

Scenario 2: Segmentation-error counterfactual. Replace the ground-truth ToyCar3 segmentation used in the controlled experiment with Mask R-CNN masks and deliberately induce the failure modes named in the paper: masks that “bleed into the background or undershoot” and geometric over-segmentation of “articulated, non-convex objects” [PAGE 4 of 7]. Prediction: confidence updates will receive wrong incoming IDs; disagreement decreases the integer score by one unit, and at zero the incoming object becomes active with confidence reset to one [PAGE 5 of 7]. This can flip the active object at voxels where the occluded surface should remain inactive, and the ICP registration will degrade because the segment points no longer correspond to the true rigid object. Verifiable metric: per-frame object pose error against ground truth, number of active-ID flips at previously stable voxels, and tabletop completeness after occluder removal. The source already predicts this direction: “the reconstruction results are heavily impacted by the inaccuracies introduced by the per-frame segmentation scheme, which in turn decreases the tracking performance” [PAGE 6 of 7]. Veto prediction: under missing or bleeding masks, TSDF++ occlusion preservation is not robust; the controlled ground-truth result does not transfer unchanged.

Scenario 3: Trilinear-support and ICP-degeneracy counterfactual. Simulate a rapid object translation or a thin planar object whose motion places destination voxels outside the eight-neighbor source support, and simultaneously make the object surface nearly planar so point-to-plane ICP has weak normal diversity. The source requires “eight valid neighbouring voxels” for trilinear interpolation [PAGE 5 of 7], and Equation (1) minimizes point-to-plane residuals using surface normals [PAGE 5 of 7]. Prediction: destination TSDF values will be missing where the eight-neighbor support fails, and \(T_O\) will be underconstrained or biased where the normal system is rank deficient. Verifiable metric: count destination voxels with fewer than eight valid source neighbors; compute the rank or condition number of the point-to-plane normal system; measure pose error and post-translation surface holes. Veto prediction: if either the interpolation support count is below eight or the ICP normal system is rank deficient, the map update cannot satisfy the paper’s stated translation and tracking assumptions; the correct adjudication is a geometric-support veto, not an occlusion-preservation success.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
