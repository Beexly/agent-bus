Let Δt = 0.1 s, because tracking is recorded at 10 Hz [PAGE 1]. Define the frame-count response as
\[
\delta_i=\frac{t_i^{\text{snap}}-t_i^{\text{motion}}}{\Delta t}\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\},
\]
which matches the paper’s definition of snap timing as “the number of frames elapsed between tmotion i and tsnap i” [PAGE 6]. In SI seconds, \(\delta_i^{\text{s}}=\Delta t\,\delta_i\). The fitted Gamma model treats the frame count as a positive continuous response:
\[
f(\delta_i\mid \mu_i,\alpha_i)
=
\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha_i)}
\left(\frac{\alpha_i}{\mu_i}\right)^{\alpha_i}
\delta_i^{\alpha_i-1}
\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha_i\delta_i}{\mu_i}\right),
\qquad \delta_i>0,\ \mu_i>0,\ \alpha_i>0.
\]
The moments are
\[
\mathbb{E}[\delta_i]=\mu_i,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}[\delta_i]=\frac{\mu_i^2}{\alpha_i},
\qquad
\operatorname{CV}[\delta_i]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_i}}.
\]
Here µ_i is in frames, Var is in frames squared, α_i is dimensionless, and CV is dimensionless. If converted to seconds, µ_i scales by Δt and Var scales by Δt², while α_i is unchanged.

The multilevel equations are
\[
\log \mu_i
=
\gamma_0+\beta^{\top}X_i+b_{q[i]}+b_{m[i]}+b_{d[i]},
\]
\[
b_q\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_q^2),
\qquad
b_m\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_m^2),
\qquad
b_d\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_d^2),
\]
\[
\log \alpha_i=\psi_0+u_{q[i]},
\qquad
u_q\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tau_q^2).
\]
The \(b\) terms are additive effects on the log mean, so \(\sigma_q,\sigma_m,\sigma_d\) are log-scale standard deviations. The \(u_q\) term is an additive effect on the log shape, so \(\tau_q\) is a log-shape standard deviation. The paper’s prior specification is “σq ∼ half-t3, σm ∼ half-t3, σd ∼ half-t3, τq ∼ half-t3” [PAGE 8], with half-t3 described as “the absolute value of a Student-t distribution centered at zero with 3 degrees of freedom” [PAGE 8]. Fixed effects \(X_i\) include down indicators, play clock at motion, timeouts remaining, motion-player position, number of motion players since the scrimmage boundary, and Gaussian-mixture motion-cluster labels [PAGE 7-8; PAGE 11]. The posterior summaries in Table 2 include play clock at motion \(0.029\) with interval \([0.022,0.035]\), one-timeout indicator \(-0.278\) with interval \([-0.534,-0.024]\), third-down indicator \(0.192\) with interval \([0.113,0.272]\), tight-end position \(0.531\) with interval \([0.405,0.659]\), and wide-receiver position \(0.320\) with interval \([0.214,0.426]\) [PAGE 11]. Table 3 gives \(\hat{\sigma}_m=0.151\), \(\hat{\sigma}_q=0.093\), \(\hat{\sigma}_d=0.029\), and \(\hat{\tau}_q=0.297\) [PAGE 11]. These are log-scale or log-shape units, not raw frame units.

The clustering operator is a Gaussian mixture model. The paper selects “a mixture of G∗= 6 ellipsoidal multivariate Gaussian distributions with varying volume, shape, and orientation (VVV)” by BIC [PAGE 10]. Cluster labels are then conditioned as fixed effects in the mean equation so that quarterback shape estimates are not confounded by motion type [PAGE 10]. The validation operator is a havoc indicator: a play has havoc if it contains at least one of pass breakup, forced fumble, tackle for loss, interception, sack, or pressure [PAGE 12]. The paper then correlates posterior mean \(u_q\) with havoc rate across quarterbacks [PAGE 12].

Exact singularity and breakdown regimes:
1. \(\alpha_i \to 0\) with \(\mu_i\) fixed implies \(\operatorname{Var}[\delta_i]=\mu_i^2/\alpha_i\to\infty\); the Gamma density becomes heavy-tailed and MCMC geometry can become unstable. The log link \(\log\alpha_i=\psi_0+u_{q[i]}\) keeps \(\alpha_i>0\) algebraically, but a very negative posterior \(u_q\) pushes the model toward this breakdown.
2. \(\alpha_i\to\infty\) with \(\mu_i\) fixed implies \(\operatorname{Var}[\delta_i]\to0\); the Gamma law degenerates toward a point mass at \(\mu_i\). In this regime the shape random effect \(u_q\) becomes weakly identified because all quarterbacks look nearly deterministic.
3. \(\mu_i\to0\) pushes expected timing below one frame, i.e. below the 0.1 s physical resolution of 10 Hz tracking [PAGE 1]. The continuous Gamma approximation is then not meaningful for the quantized frame-count response.
4. \(\tau_q\to0\) collapses quarterback heterogeneity in the shape equation; the leaderboard is then non-identifiable because all \(u_q\) shrink to a common value. The paper acknowledges “considerable uncertainty” and “limited sample of motion plays” [PAGE 12].
5. Sparse quarterback histories activate partial pooling. The leaderboard is restricted to quarterbacks with at least 50 pass attempts on the considered motion plays [PAGE 12], so small-sample quarterbacks cannot be ranked reliably.
6. The Gaussian-mixture labels are conditioned as fixed, so clustering uncertainty is not propagated into \(\alpha_i\). If motion-cluster assignments shift, the mean equation changes and the shape random effect can shift.
7. The havoc criterion is a binary play-level aggregate, not a continuous physical force. The association between \(u_q\) and havoc is therefore an observational correlation, not a structural derivative.

A critical formal inconsistency must be recorded: under the density above, \(\alpha_i\) is inverse variance, not proportional to variance. The paper’s phrase “shape parameter α (which is proportional to the Gamma distribution’s variance)” [PAGE 7] conflicts with the standard moment equation \(\operatorname{Var}[\delta_i]=\mu_i^2/\alpha_i\). Likewise, the leaderboard statement that “a higher posterior mean values corresponds to greater variability in timing” [PAGE 12] requires a sign convention or parameterization check. The formal audit should therefore treat the direction of the quarterback ranking as requiring code-level confirmation.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive decision problem is a timing game. The offense chooses a distribution \(F_\delta\) over snap timing; the defense chooses rush timing and rush moves based on its predictive model of \(F_\delta\). If the defense’s predictive uncertainty is high, its timing advantage falls; if the offense becomes predictable, the defense can synchronize disruption. The paper’s hypothesis is consistent with this: “higher variability in snap timing is beneficial for the passing game, as it relates to facing less havoc created by the opposing defense” [PAGE 1]. Formally, let the predictive differential entropy of snap timing be
\[
h(F_\delta)=-\int_0^\infty f_\delta(x)\log f_\delta(x)\,dx,
\]
measured in nats when the logarithm is natural. For the Gamma law above,
\[
h(F_\delta)
=
\alpha+\log\left(\frac{\mu}{\alpha}\right)
+\log\Gamma(\alpha)
+(1-\alpha)\psi(\alpha),
\]
where \(\psi\) is the digamma function. Differences in \(h\) across quarterbacks are information-unit differences; probabilities remain dimensionless. Under the standard shape parameterization, lower \(\alpha\) means higher variance and generally higher entropy, so a quarterback who varies cadence imposes more predictive uncertainty on the defense. This is the counter-trap: the defense expects a habitual cadence, and the offense deliberately perturbs the timing distribution.

“Crisis habit regression” is the opposite regime. Under pressure, a quarterback may revert to a fixed cadence, shrinking variance and lowering entropy. In the standard Gamma parameterization, that corresponds to higher \(\alpha\) and lower \(h(F_\delta)\). The defense’s recursive model can then lock onto the cadence and time its rush. The paper’s contextual fixed effects are the “clock” layer that can override or amplify this game. The play-clock coefficient is \(0.029\) with interval \([0.022,0.035]\) [PAGE 11], meaning later play-clock values are associated with longer log mean snap timing. The one-timeout indicator is \(-0.278\) with interval \([-0.534,-0.024]\) [PAGE 11], and the paper notes that “the snap timing tends to be longer when there are no timeouts left for the offense” [PAGE 10]. Third down has coefficient \(0.192\) with interval \([0.113,0.272]\) [PAGE 11]. These are clock-urgency effects: they change the mean timing and can also change the quarterback’s willingness to vary cadence. The recursive theory-of-mind audit therefore separates three layers: crisis habit regression predicts low entropy under stress; counter-trap behavior predicts deliberately higher entropy to defeat anticipation; clock pressure predicts mean shifts and possible variance compression because delay-of-game risk rises. The model captures mean shifts through \(X_i\) and quarterback shape heterogeneity through \(u_q\), but it does not explicitly model the defense’s posterior over the quarterback’s cadence or the quarterback’s posterior over the defense’s rush timing. That missing strategic feedback is a theory-of-mind gap.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer imposes a hard resolution veto. Tracking is 10 Hz [PAGE 1], so the smallest observable timing increment is \(\Delta t=0.1\) s. If two quarterbacks’ posterior mean snap timings differ by less than one frame, or if their credible intervals are wider than the physically resolvable increment, the data cannot adjudicate a real physical difference. The paper’s own uncertainty language is relevant: “there is considerable uncertainty in our estimates” [PAGE 12], and the intervals for the mean-level quarterback and motion-player effects overlap between top and bottom players [PAGE 21]. The shape-level leaderboard is more discriminative in the paper’s presentation, but the parameter-direction issue in the shape equation must be resolved before a physical interpretation is accepted.

The biomechanical layer imposes a proxy veto. The motion-onset time is not directly observed; for many plays it is assigned by a speed threshold. The paper states, “We then choose the average ratio value of 0.45 as the threshold for determining the start of motion for the remaining plays. Specifically, we assign tmotion i as the first frame between line set and ball snap where the player reaches at least 45% of their top speed” [PAGE 6]. This is a kinematic proxy, not a force-plate or musculoskeletal measurement. If a receiver’s acceleration profile changes because of fatigue, injury, position, or surface friction, the inferred motion onset shifts even if the quarterback’s cadence does not. The paper acknowledges the limitation: “to identify the start of motion, we use a threshold-based criterion which relies on the moment the motion player achieves a certain fraction of their top speed before the snap” [PAGE 15]. There are no mass, force, joint-load, or muscle-activation variables in the model, so a biomechanical causal claim is vetoed by missing variables.

The weather and surface layer imposes an omitted-variable veto. The fixed-effect list includes down, play clock, timeouts, position, number of motion players, and motion cluster [PAGE 7-8; PAGE 11], but it does not include temperature, wind, precipitation, field surface, or dome status. Let \(W\) denote weather and surface, \(U=u_q\), \(\delta\) snap timing, and \(H\) the binary havoc event. The observed marginal association used in the paper is
\[
\mathbb{E}[H\mid U,X]
=
\int \mathbb{E}[H\mid U,X,W]\,dP(W\mid U,X).
\]
Unless \(W\) is independent of \(U\) and \(H\), or unless \(W\) is included in the conditioning set, the marginal association is not the causal effect of \(U\) on \(H\). Weather can affect both motion timing and pass-rush disruption, so the integral contains an omitted path. The paper’s own confounding warning is stronger than weather alone: the quarterback random effect is “certainly correlated with the team’s center and coach’s play-calling” [PAGE 15]. That means the cross-layer adjudication is:
- Descriptive association between posterior \(u_q\) and havoc rate: allowed by the model and reported by the paper [PAGE 12].
- Causal quarterback-skill claim: vetoed without center, coach, weather, surface, and opponent controls.
- Biomechanical mechanism claim: vetoed because motion onset is a 45% top-speed proxy [PAGE 6] and no force or fatigue variables are modeled.
- Physical resolution claim: vetoed when posterior differences are below the 0.1 s tracking increment [PAGE 1].
- Weather-invariant generalization claim: vetoed because weather and surface are absent from the fixed-effect list [PAGE 7-8].
The correct adjudication is therefore a layered one: the paper establishes a useful Bayesian measurement model and a descriptive posterior association, but it does not establish a physical, biomechanical, or weather-robust causal law.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

This prediction follows directly from the paper’s admission that quarterback effects are “certainly correlated with the team’s center and coach’s play-calling” [PAGE 15] and from the absence of weather and surface in the reported fixed effects [PAGE 7-8].

Verifiable test: fit the model on weeks 1-9 of the 2022 season, freeze the posterior \(u_q\) estimates, and predict havoc rate on weeks 10-18 or on a later season. Veto prediction: if the out-of-sample correlation between frozen \(u_q\) and future havoc rate is non-positive or fails a pre-registered minimum, the leaderboard is vetoed as a stable multi-week measure. This is predicted by the paper’s own early-season window [PAGE 3], limited sample of 2,198 plays [PAGE 5], selection bias [PAGE 15], and “limited sample of motion plays” warning [PAGE 12]. A successful prospective test would not prove causality, but a failed test would decisively veto generalization.
