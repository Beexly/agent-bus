To rigorously analyze Tolman’s distinction between mnemonization and memory, as well as the introduction of "inference" as a third mood of sign-gestalt-expectation, we must deconstruct the epistemic layers involved in animal behavior. The source text (page 140) posits that true memory requires not just expecting a past significate but expecting a "means-end-relation of pastness." This implies a cognitive architecture where an organism models temporal relations abstractly, rather than merely associating stimulus-response pairs based on contiguity.

First, consider the definition of *mnemonization*. In this context, it is the conditioned expectation derived from the actual past enjoyment of a sequence: given sign ($S$), means-end-relation ($ME$), and given significate ($\sigma$). This is essentially a learned trajectory in state space. Tolman argues that lower animals possess this capability. However, he distinguishes *memory* as a higher-order function requiring the expectation of "pastness" itself—a meta-cognitive grasp of the temporal relation. If an animal merely reacts to a sign because it previously led to a reward, it is mnemonizing. If it recognizes that the sign points to a past event specifically *because* it occurred in the past, it is remembering. Tolman asserts no evidence exists for the latter in non-human animals.

Second, the introduction of *inference* adds a layer of transitivity. An inferential sign-gestalt-expectation occurs when the release of a significate expectation is driven by other correlated sign-gestalt-expectations, not by direct sensory input or prior direct experience of that specific sequence. This suggests a combinatorial ability to manipulate mental maps without traversing them physically. For instance, if a rat knows A leads to B, and B leads to C, but has never gone directly from A to C, inferring A leads to C constitutes an inferential expectation. Tolman notes that the diagram for inference is topologically identical to that of mnemonization in two dimensions, implying that the difference lies in the internal generative process (direct experience vs. logical deduction) rather than the external observable path.

The gray-zone here involves defining the boundary between complex associative chaining (mnemonization) and genuine propositional inference. Modern neuroscience might map mnemonization to synaptic potentiation along a fixed path, while inference would require hippocampal-cortical interactions supporting "cognitive mapping" and off-line planning. The lack of a distinct two-dimensional representation for these states highlights a limitation in behavioral phenomenology: outwardly identical behaviors can stem from fundamentally different cognitive mechanisms. This necessitates a move beyond simple S-R paradigms toward information-theoretic models of internal state representation.

We formalize Tolman's three moods using Information Geometry and Stochastic Processes. Let the cognitive state be represented by a probability distribution $P$ over a latent space $\mathcal{Z}$ representing potential significates ($\sigma$). The means-end relation is modeled as a conditional transition kernel $T(\cdot | S)$ dependent on the current sign $S$.

**1. Mnemonization (Perceptual/Experienced Expectation):**
Let $H_{past}$ denote the history of experienced sequences. The expectation $E_M$ is the posterior distribution conditioned strictly on observed transitions:
$$ E_M(\sigma | S) = P(\sigma | S, H_{past}) $$
This is bounded by the mutual information between the sign and the significate within the experienced dataset. The breakdown regime occurs when $H_{past}$ is empty or contains disjoint manifolds, leading to singularities in the likelihood estimation (overfitting to noise).

**2. Inference (Transitive/Deductive Expectation):**
Here, the expectation $E_I$ is derived not from $H_{past}$ containing the triplet $(S, ME, \sigma)$, but from a set of correlated expectations $\{E_1, E_2, ..., E_n\}$. We model this as a compositional operator $\Phi$ acting on the space of distributions:
$$ E_I(\sigma | S) = \Phi_{k} \left( \bigotimes_{i=1}^{k} P_i(\cdot | S_i) \right) $$
where $\Phi_k$ represents a transitive closure operation over the graph of sign-gestalts. The unit of measure here is the reduction in entropy of $\sigma$ achieved without direct observation, measured in bits. The singularity arises if the correlation graph contains cycles or contradictory paths (e.g., A->B and A->not B), causing the inference engine to fail to converge to a unique predictive distribution.

**3. True Memory (Expectation of Pastness):**
This requires modeling time explicitly as a variable $t$. The expectation includes the temporal tag $\tau$:
$$ E_{Mem}(\sigma, \tau | S_{now}) = P(\sigma \text{ occurred at } \tau < t_{now} | S_{now}) $$
Formally, this demands a joint distribution over content and timestamp. The breakdown regime is the absence of a biological clock or temporal binding mechanism; without a distinct representation of $\tau$, the system collapses back to mere mnemonization where only the association strength matters, not the temporal distance.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage of Tolman's "sign-gestalt" traces back to Kantian intuition regarding synthetic a priori concepts and Husserl's phenomenology of time-consciousness, filtered through early 20th-century Gestalt psychology (Köhler, Wertheimer). Tolman sought to rescue purpose from the mechanistic determinism of Watsonian Behaviorism. By invoking "retrocausal" genealogy, we acknowledge that modern interpretations often view these expectations as forward-looking predictions, yet they are constrained by the past.
In multi-week experimental traps, organisms initially rely on rigid mnemonizations (habit learning). Over weeks, if environmental contingencies shift subtly, animals exhibiting "inferential" capabilities will adapt faster, bypassing the need to re-train every link in the chain. However, a trap exists: the " Einstellung effect," where strong mnemonizations veto novel inferences. The historical scientific trap was assuming that identical behavioral outputs (running straight to food) proved identical internal processes. Tolman’s contribution was insisting on the ontological difference between the *moods*, even if the 2D behavioral projection is identical.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
When an organism faces a novel maze configuration that breaks its habitual mnemonization, it undergoes a crisis. A low-order agent defaults to stochastic exploration or freezes (regression to simpler S-R loops). A higher-order agent engages Recursive Theory of Mind—not about other minds, but about its own cognitive map. It simulates the failure: "My past sequence A-B-C is blocked at B." It then recursively queries its store of correlated expectations: "Is there another path to C? I know A-D-E and E-C."
The counter-trap involves resisting the urge to force a mnemonic fit (trying to squeeze through wall B) and instead activating the inferential module. The "clock" here is metabolic pressure; the longer the deliberation, the higher the energy cost. Animals with robust inferential abilities minimize this clock penalty by pre-compiling maps (cognitive cartography), allowing rapid inference without real-time exhaustive search. They predict their own future confusion and act preemptively.
