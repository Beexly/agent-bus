However, he dismisses this theoretical impossibility because "the variations in weight are small compared to the weight itself." This is a crucial insight into the domain of validity for the Normal approximation. When the coefficient of variation (standard deviation divided by mean) is small, higher-order terms in the Taylor expansion of the scaling law become negligible, making the distribution appear approximately symmetric and normal even if the underlying mechanism is multiplicative.

This low coefficient of variation confirms that the distribution is tightly clustered, supporting his argument that the asymmetry predicted by cubic scaling is minimal within the observed range.

Finally, we must consider the broader implications. Galton’s use of the Normal Curve was not merely descriptive but normative. While physical traits like height and weight do tend toward normality under certain conditions, complex behavioral traits may not, due to social feedback loops, cultural transmission, and non-additive genetic effects. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Galton's invocation of the "Law of Frequency of Error" places him squarely within the intellectual lineage of Pierre-Simon Laplace and Carl Friedrich Gauss, who developed the method of least squares and identified the Normal distribution as the natural form for errors resulting from many small, independent causes. However, Galton diverged from this tradition by applying it to heredity and biological traits. His earlier work, *Hereditary Genius* (1869), attempted to quantify inherited intelligence using pedigree analysis, but faced criticism for insufficient data and methodological crudeness. The present text represents a maturation of his approach: instead of relying on subjective rankings, he uses precise anthropometric measurements (height, weight, etc.) to demonstrate the pervasiveness of the Normal Curve.

This move has deep roots in the 19th-century quest to apply mathematical rigor to social and biological phenomena. Adolphe Quetelet, a Belgian astronomer and statistician, had earlier proposed the "average man" (*l'homme moyen*) as a real entity whose characteristics followed the Normal distribution. Galton was influenced by Quetelet but went further by investigating regression to the mean and correlation. The "extraordinary coincidence" he observes across diverse faculties can be seen as an early validation of Quetelet's hypothesis, though Galton would later develop more sophisticated tools (e.g., correlation coefficient, regression lines) that Quetelet lacked.

The historical trap lies in the potential conflation of statistical regularity with teleological purpose. Galton's belief in eugenics stemmed partly from the perceived stability and predictability of these distributions. By showing that even complex traits like vision and swiftness conform to the same pattern as simple ones like height, he reinforced the idea that human qualities are governed by immutable natural laws. This perspective, while scientifically innovative, carries ethical risks if interpreted as justifying hierarchical judgments about human worth. Moreover, the subsequent misuse of biostatistics in the 20th century highlights the danger of extending Galton's physical findings to socio-economic domains without considering confounding factors like environment, culture, and systemic inequality.
