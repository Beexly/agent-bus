Clock. The model itself is on a degradation clock. Internal cross-validation showed minimal variance (AUPRC CV 4.2%), but temporal validation on the 2023–2024 cohort dropped to AUPRC 0.738 [PAGE 29, 32]. The authors state this “suggests potential overfitting despite cross-validation procedures, highlighting the need for continuous model updating” [PAGE 42]. Monthly retesting for dynamic risk monitoring is resource-intensive and “may not be sustainable across all competitive levels” [PAGE 40]. The clock therefore runs on two axes: (a) model staleness (AUPRC decay across seasons) and (b) intervention window (the observed 73% risk reduction was measured within four weeks; durability beyond that horizon is untested) [PAGE 2, 36]. Without continuous recalibration and in-season reassessment, the system regresses toward baseline statistical performance.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and environmental veto proof.

Physical veto (equipment and personnel). Isokinetic testing requires equipment costing €80,000–120,000, specialized technician training, and 45–60 minutes per individual assessment [PAGE 39–40]. Smaller clubs cannot sustain this; monthly retesting multiplies the burden. Veto condition: if a club lacks a Biodex System 4 Pro equivalent or dedicated medical staff, the full 18-variable strength-asymmetry feature block cannot be acquired, invalidating the model’s primary input layer [PAGE 14, 39].

Biomechanical veto. (a) Velocity dependence: low angular velocity (60°/s) isokinetic testing alone was previously shown insufficient as an injury predictor in professional soccer [PAGE 4, citation 6]; the present study’s added value comes from 180°/s and 300°/s velocities [PAGE 25]. Removing high-velocity testing removes the sprint-relevant signal where 76.5% of hamstring injuries occur [PAGE 22]. (b) Laterality blindness: the asymmetry formula cannot account for limb-dominance direction, and 72.4% of players were stronger in the kicking leg for extensors while 61.5% were stronger in the non-dominant limb for flexors [PAGE 21]. (c) Strength-as-proxy confound: weaker players exhibit greater bilateral asymmetry (r = −0.42, p<0.001), so asymmetry may partly index absolute strength deficiency rather than independent imbalance [PAGE 21]. (d) Literature conflict: van Dyk et al. [52] (614-player 4-year cohort) and Green et al. [53] (systematic review) found isokinetic deficits weak or non-predictive (AUC 0.54–0.56), challenging the causal centrality of asymmetry [PAGE 41–42].

Weather/seasonal veto (environmental-temporal). Although “weather” per se is not modeled, the data show strict seasonal veto windows: pre-season preparation (July–August) and congested winter fixtures (December–January) concentrate injury incidence at 7.24 and 6.81 per 1000 hours respectively [PAGE 22]. Fixture congestion is defined as ≥2 matches per week, typically from domestic-cup overlap with league schedules [PAGE 39]. A static preseason screen cannot veto risk arising mid-season from weather-affected pitch conditions, compressed recovery, or travel; the authors acknowledge in-season reassessment was precluded by player schedules and testing time [PAGE 42].

Adjudication. The model passes internal statistical scrutiny (calibration slope ~0.94, Brier-based calibration, decision-curve net benefit across 0.15–0.60 thresholds [PAGE 29]) but fails deployment vetoes in resource-constrained clubs, female populations (male-only cohort [PAGE 8, 42]), and any setting where high-velocity isokinetic testing or monthly retesting is logistically impossible. The veto hierarchy is: physical access > biomechanical validity > temporal dynamics > causal evidence.

Three verifiable reinforcement-learning-verifiable (RLVR) counterfactual veto scenarios with falsifiable predictions.

Veto Scenario 1 — External-population transport failure. Counterfactual: deploy the trained ensemble model unchanged to a cohort of female professional footballers or to clubs outside the original six European clubs. Source basis: the study explicitly limits generalizability to female players (different injury mechanisms and strength characteristics) and states true external validation across different organizations remains unestablished [PAGE 8, 32, 42]. Verifiable prediction: AUPRC will fall below 0.70 (ensemble internal 0.759; same-club temporal 0.738) and the calibration slope will deviate significantly from unity (outside 0.87–1.01) because sex-specific asymmetry–injury relationships and club-level testing protocols shift the joint distribution P(X, Y). Failure criterion: AUPRC < 0.65 or Hosmer–Lemeshow p < 0.05 in the external cohort.

Veto Scenario 2 — Removal of in-season dynamic reassessment (static-preseason-only constraint). Counterfactual: operators use only the baseline preseason isokinetic assessment and prohibit in-season retesting due to fixture congestion. Source basis: the authors note that practical constraints precluded in-season reassessment and that a single preseason assessment “does not capture the dynamic nature of neuromuscular status across a competitive season” [PAGE 42]; false negatives were driven by rapid onset within 30 days and high load volatility (CV 34.2% vs 24.6%) [PAGE 32]. Verifiable prediction: the false-negative rate will exceed the observed ~24.7% (100 − 75.3% sensitivity at 30% threshold [PAGE 29]) during the first eight weeks of the season, particularly for players with ACWR spikes >1.5, because acute load volatility overrides static baseline asymmetry. Failure criterion: missed-injury rate > 30% in the first quarter of the season.

Veto Scenario 3 — SMOTE-induced synthetic-manifold veto in low-prevalence deployment. Counterfactual: a club adopts the model but applies it to a lower-injury-prevalence roster (e.g., well-resourced squad with incidence <15%) while retaining SMOTE-trained decision thresholds. Source basis: class imbalance was addressed with SMOTE; evaluation was conducted on both SMOTE-enhanced training and original validation data to guard against artificially inflated performance [PAGE 15–16]; AUPRC is prevalence-sensitive [PAGE 29]. Verifiable prediction: positive predictive value (PPV) will collapse from the observed 67.4% at the 30% threshold [PAGE 29] to below 0.40 as prevalence drops, because the decision threshold optimized on 28.5% prevalence becomes over-sensitive; net benefit will fall below the “treat-none” reference line. Failure criterion: PPV < 0.40 or net benefit < 0 at the deployed threshold.
