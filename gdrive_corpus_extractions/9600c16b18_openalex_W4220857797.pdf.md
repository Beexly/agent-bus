The model has a CSPDarknet-53 backbone, SPP, PANet, and YOLO heads at 20 by 20, 40 by 40, and 80 by 80 scales [PAGE 5]. The reported training setup includes input frames of 640 by 640, weight decay 0.0005, momentum 0.949, batch size 32, initial learning rate 0.001, and 55,000 iteration steps [PAGE 5]. Evaluation is at “mAP at IoU = 50%” [PAGE 5]. Detection quality is not merely a precursor; it creates the candidate set for every later association. If the detector misses a player for several frames, the tracker can only recover by re-identification or by a predicted Kalman state, and the paper later reports exactly this as missing-track and mismatch behavior [PAGE 9–10].

The second hop is class-to-identity. The source says the detection module distinguishes players and referees but does not classify players by team, which “results in identity switching between players, even identity mismatch, sometimes when they are occluded” [PAGE 5]. The proposed fix is a color mask over player and referee bounding boxes, because “the major difference between teams and the referee is jersey color: one team is white, the other is blue and the referee is red” [PAGE 5]. The assignment cost is given as \(C_{i,j}=d_v(i,j)+(PI^{p}_{OBBox_j}(A)+PI^{r}_{OBBox_{(j+1)}}(A))\) [PAGE 6], where the pixel-intensity terms are extracted from the j-th player box and the (j+1)-th referee box, and \(A \in \{R,G,B\}\) denotes the red, green, and blue channels [PAGE 6]. This is the operator that is supposed to reduce identity switches. The gray-zone is that the cost uses manually picked pixel intensities: “Thereafter pixel intensities were picked manually as shown in algorithm1 from jth bounding box of the player and (j + 1)th bounding box of the referee to concatenate the color mask with cosine distance as shown in 1” [PAGE 6]. Manual color extraction is not a general color-constancy model; it is a calibrated prior tied to the observed jersey colors and lighting. Therefore the paper’s strongest claim should be read as conditional: color mask plus cosine distance improves association when jersey colors are separable and the referee’s red prior holds.

The third hop is appearance-to-association. The visual distance is a cosine distance over CNN descriptors, \(d_v(i,j)=\min_{r^i_k \in R_i}(1-r_j^\top r^i_k)\) under the stated normalization condition, with \(r_j\) the appearance descriptor and \(R_i\) the set of the last \(n\) appearance descriptors associated with track \(i\) [PAGE 7]. For unit-normalized descriptors, the distance is \(1-\cos\theta\), with value 0 for identical orientation, 1 for orthogonal descriptors, and 2 for antipodal descriptors. The singularity is exact: if a descriptor has zero norm, the cosine normalization is undefined; if \(R_i\) is empty, the minimum is undefined; and if descriptors are not normalized, the raw dot product does not have the stated unit-vector interpretation. The paper states “\(|r_j| = 1\)” [PAGE 7], so the formal range assumption is a unit-vector assumption, not a universal property of CNN features.

The fourth hop is association-to-metric. MOTA is given as \(\mathrm{MOTA}=1-\frac{\sum_t(C_m m_t+C_m fp_t+C_s mme_t)}{\sum_t g_t}\), and GMOTA substitutes a global identity-switch term \(gmme_t\) for \(mme_t\) [PAGE 9]. The terms are counts or aggregate proportions over frames, so MOTA/GMOTA are dimensionless accuracy scores, conventionally reported as percentages; they are not probabilities of a correct identity in a Bayesian sense. The exact breakdown is \(\sum_t g_t=0\), which makes both metrics undefined by division by zero. The source reports “tracking accuracy of 96% and 60% on MOTA and GMOTA metrics respectively with a detection speed of 23 frames per second (FPS)” [PAGE 1]. The gray-zone audit must flag the metric arithmetic. Page 9 says “The weight factors were set to \(C_m=1\) and \(C_s=\ln 10\) with reference to [48]” [PAGE 9]. Yet the same page’s GMOTA example uses \(1.8+1.8\), and page 10 uses \(11.6+11.6\) [PAGE 9–10]. The source does not show a complete algebraic derivation from the stated weights to those numbers. Therefore, the reported GMOTA values should be treated as source-reported results, not as independently reproduced calculations. This is a formal gray zone, not a reason to invent a replacement metric.

The final gray-zone audit concerns failure boundaries. The paper’s own limitation is decisive: “The limitation of the proposed method is that when the player with the same jersey color is occluded, the ID of the player is switched. The major drawback of the proposed methodology is higher computation cost, which reduces FPS compared to those of existing methods” [PAGE 14]. The referee-color boundary is also explicit: “When the jersey color of the referee is other than red color then the proposed YOLOv4 + JCD-SORT method fails to detect the referee, and it detects as player and tracks as a player as shown in Figure 10” [PAGE 8]. Thus the method’s identity claim is not universal. It is a conditional identity-coherence claim: strong when jersey color separates players and referee, weak when same-color occlusion or non-red referee jerseys violate the color prior. That is the correct frontier conclusion.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source operators are detector, color-mask classifier, cosine appearance distance, SORT-style assignment, Kalman prediction, and MOTA/GMOTA scoring. All equations below are either quoted from the source or explicitly marked as standard definitions used to interpret a source term.

Detection precision, recall, and F1-score are defined on pages 8 as
\[
P=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad
R=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad
F_1=2\cdot\frac{P\cdot R}{P+R}.
\]
These are dimensionless fractions. Breakdown regimes: \(TP+FP=0\) makes \(P\) undefined; \(TP+FN=0\) makes \(R\) undefined; \(P+R=0\) makes \(F_1\) undefined. Average precision is
\[
AP=\sum_t (R_n-R_{n-1})P_n,
\]
also dimensionless [PAGE 8]. IoU is referenced only through the evaluation threshold “mAP at IoU = 50%” [PAGE 5]. The standard dimensionless definition consistent with that use is
\[
\mathrm{IoU}(B_p,B_g)=\frac{|B_p\cap B_g|}{|B_p\cup B_g|},
\]
where \(B_p\) is predicted box and \(B_g\) is ground-truth box. The source does not print this formula, so it is a standard interpretation of the stated 50% IoU threshold, not a new source equation.

The JCD-SORT assignment cost is
\[
C_{i,j}=d_v(i,j)+\left(PI^{p}_{OBBox_j}(A)+PI^{r}_{OBBox_{(j+1)}}(A)\right),
\]
where \(PI^{p}_{OBBox_j}\) is “pixel intensity of jth detected player bounding box” and \(PI^{r}_{OBBox_{(j+1)}}\) is “pixel intensity of (j + 1)th detected referee bounding box” [PAGE 6]. The channel notation is
\[
PI^{p}_{OBBox_j}(x,y)=OBBOX((x_1,x_2,x_3),(y_1,y_2,y_3)),
\]
with \(A\in\{R,G,B\}\) denoting red, green, and blue channels [PAGE 6]. Units: pixel intensity is a digital number (DN). The source does not state the bit depth, so the numerical range must not be assumed beyond the paper’s own manual selection procedure. The exact breakdown is color-distribution overlap: if the player and referee intensity terms are not separable under the chosen threshold, the added term cannot disambiguate identities. The paper’s own referee restriction is the proof: non-red referee jerseys cause referee detection to fail and the referee to be tracked as a player [PAGE 8].

The appearance distance is
\[
d_v(i,j)=\min_{r^i_k\in R_i}\left(1-r_j^\top r^i_k\right),
\]
with the source’s normalization statement “\(|r_j|=1\)” and \(R_i\) the set of the last \(n\) appearance descriptors associated with track \(i\) [PAGE 7]. For unit vectors this is \(1-\cos\theta\), dimensionless, with range \([0,2]\). Breakdown regimes: zero-norm descriptor makes normalization undefined; empty \(R_i\) makes the minimum undefined; identical normalized appearance gives 0; orthogonal appearance gives 1; antipodal appearance gives 2. This operator does not encode team membership by itself; the color-mask term is the separate team/referee cue.

MOTA is
\[
\mathrm{MOTA}=1-\frac{\sum_t(C_m m_t+C_m fp_t+C_s mme_t)}{\sum_t g_t},
\]
and GMOTA is
\[
\mathrm{GMOTA}=1-\frac{\sum_t(C_m m_t+C_m fp_t+C_s gmme_t)}{\sum_t g_t},
\]
where \(m_t\), \(fp_t\), \(mme_t\), \(gmme_t\), and \(g_t\) are count aggregates over frames [PAGE 9]. Units are dimensionless accuracy proportions; reported values are percentages. Exact singularities: \(\sum_t g_t=0\) makes both metrics undefined. The source states “The weight factors were set to \(C_m = 1\) and \(C_s = \ln 10\) with reference to [48]” [PAGE 9]. The identity-switch weight therefore has a stated numerical value \(\ln 10\approx 2.3026\) in dimensionless metric units. However, the source’s own numerical examples use \(1.8+1.8\) and \(11.6+11.6\) [PAGE 9–10]. No algebraic bridge is shown. That is a source-level inconsistency; the formal auditor must not silently repair it. The source-reported examples remain: “MOTA = 1 −1 + 1/55 = 0.96” and “GMOTA = 1 −1.8 + 1.8/9 = 0.60” [PAGE 9], and “GMOTA = 1 −11.6 + 11.6/33 = 0.29” [PAGE 10]. Detection speed is \(23\,\mathrm{FPS}=23\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) [PAGE 1], and the paper later states higher computation cost reduces FPS [PAGE 14].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is visible in the paper’s own reference chain. YOLOv4 is the detector descendant of CSPDarknet-53, SPP, PANet, and YOLO-head design [PAGE 4–5]. SORT is the tracking ancestor: the paper cites Bewley et al. for “Simple online and realtime tracking” and Wojke et al. for “Simple online and realtime tracking with a deep association metric” [PAGE 3, PAGE 15]. JCD-SORT is therefore a retrocausal hybrid: it inherits SORT’s frame-to-frame assignment and Kalman prediction, DeepSORT’s cosine appearance descriptor, and a new hand-calibrated jersey-color term. The metric lineage is also explicit: MOTA/GMOTA are attributed to Ristani et al. [48] [PAGE 9, PAGE 15]. The soccer-data lineage is ISSIA and SoccerNet [PAGE 7, PAGE 15], with predecessor trackers including T-MCNF, Topography, Small-Soccer Player, and MCMPTI [PAGE 10–11]. This genealogy matters because the paper is not a pure invention; it is a constrained recombination of mature operators, and that recombination is where both its strength and its fragility live.

The historical trap is the “court mask” inheritance. Earlier methods used a court mask to suppress people outside the playfield, but the source says such masking “cannot detect an official (referee) who watches a game closely for ensuring whether the rules are followed” [PAGE 3]. The proposed method replaces court masking with a learned background class and a jersey-color mask. That is a real architectural move, but it swaps one prior for another: the referee must be red, and the teams must be white and blue. The paper states exactly this color prior [PAGE 5]. The multi-week trap is calibration drift. A color mask calibrated on one stadium, one lighting condition, and one kit set can fail across matches, weather, broadcast color grading, or alternate kits. The source’s manual pixel-intensity selection [PAGE 6] makes this a first-order issue, not a hypothetical. A multi-week evaluation that pools matches without per-match color calibration can leak calibration information across matches or, conversely, punish the method for a distribution shift it was never designed to handle.

A second multi-week trap is temporal leakage. The paper reports 500-frame sub-videos and ground-truth tracks over those windows [PAGE 9–10]. If hyperparameters, color thresholds, or identity thresholds are tuned on the same match or same lighting segment used for testing, the reported MOTA/GMOTA become optimistic relative to a new match. The paper’s ablation compares YOLOv4 + DeepSORT against YOLOv4 + JCD-SORT [PAGE 13], which is the right internal comparison, but it does not by itself prove cross-match generalization. The correct lineage-aware reading is: JCD-SORT is a within-distribution color-association enhancement, not a demonstrated open-world identity system.

A third trap is metric inheritance without arithmetic reproduction. The paper inherits MOTA/GMOTA from multi-target tracking and reports 96% MOTA and 60% GMOTA [PAGE 1], but the GMOTA examples use unexplained constants [PAGE 9–10]. In a multi-week audit, one must preserve the source’s reported values while separately flagging that the calculation is not fully transparent. The historical lesson is that metric names travel faster than metric implementations. A tracker can inherit a respected metric label and still have an under-specified aggregation path.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
This is not a human theory-of-mind model; it is a recursive identity-belief system. The tracker maintains a belief over track \(i\) using the last \(n\) appearance descriptors \(R_i\) [PAGE 7], a Kalman-predicted state, and a color-mask term [PAGE 6]. Under normal play, the recursion is: detect, predict, compare appearance and color, assign, update. Under crisis, the recursion changes.

Crisis habit regression occurs when occlusion or detector dropout makes the color and appearance evidence weak. The system then regresses toward the older SORT/DeepSORT habit: motion and IoU dominate, and identity is carried by the predicted track rather than by a fresh discriminating observation. The source’s own DeepSORT ablation shows the failure mode: YOLOv4 + DeepSORT “simply tracks the detected players; as all the detected players had similar features, it did not work in the case of identity switching” [PAGE 7]. The paper’s limitation sharpens the point: “when the player with the same jersey color is occluded, the ID of the player is switched” [PAGE 14]. That is crisis habit regression in exact terms: the color mask cannot separate same-color players, so the tracker falls back to motion/appearance continuity and the identity belief flips.

The counter-trap is the jersey-color mask. It works when the color prior is true: white versus blue versus red [PAGE 5]. It fails when the referee is not red; the source states the method “fails to detect the referee, and it detects as player and tracks as a player” [PAGE 8]. So the counter-trap is not a universal identity signal. It is a conditional prior that can become a false certainty. A recursive observer that trusts the color mask too strongly will make a confident wrong identity assignment when the prior is violated.

The clock is the temporal horizon. The tracker updates frame to frame, and the reported detection speed is 23 FPS [PAGE 1]. The metric horizon is 500-frame sub-videos [PAGE 9]. The clock limits recursion in two ways. First, at 23 FPS, the system is near but not clearly above common live-analysis thresholds; the paper says higher computation cost reduces FPS compared with existing methods [PAGE 14]. Second, the 500-frame window is long enough for repeated occlusion and re-identification, but short enough that a single identity switch can dominate GMOTA. The recursive theory-of-mind reading is therefore: do not ask whether the tracker “understands” the player; ask whether its identity belief remains calibrated when the color prior, the appearance descriptor, and the motion prior disagree. The source shows that when they disagree under same-color occlusion or non-red referee jerseys, the belief is not robust.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is a condition outside the tracker’s association equation that invalidates the identity claim. The physical layer includes camera motion, occlusion, lighting, and sensor resolution. The paper acknowledges nonlinear motion and erratic ball/player velocities [PAGE 1–2], extreme occlusion [PAGE 2], and comparable jersey colors [PAGE 2]. The biomechanical layer includes player-player contact, same-color occlusion, and fast ball motion. The source’s limitation is direct proof: same-jersey occlusion causes ID switching [PAGE 14]. The weather layer is not tested in the source. That absence is itself a veto: any claim of all-weather color-mask robustness is unsupported. Weather or lighting can shift the pixel-intensity distributions used in \(PI^{p}_{OBBox_j}(A)\) and \(PI^{r}_{OBBox_{(j+1)}}(A)\) [PAGE 6], and the paper’s manual intensity selection makes that shift first-order.

Formal veto proof: let the color-mask separation margin be
\[
M=\min_{a\neq b}\|\mu_a-\mu_b\|-\max(\sigma_a,\sigma_b),
\]
where \(\mu_a\) and \(\mu_b\) are mean RGB digital-number vectors for two jersey classes and \(\sigma_a,\sigma_b\) are their within-class dispersions in DN. If \(M\le 0\), the color classes overlap and the added \(PI\) term cannot reliably separate them. If the referee-red prior fails, the referee class is outside the calibrated color set, and the source’s reported failure follows [PAGE 8]. If occlusion makes the visible jersey area small enough that the estimated \(\mu\) is dominated by skin, grass, shadow, or another player’s kit, the same inequality fails even when the full-jersey color is separable.

Biomechanical veto proof: JCD-SORT associates detections, but it does not model contact mechanics or ball aerodynamics. The paper says soccer motions produce “nonlinear motion patterns” and that “the orientations and velocities of foreground objects are erratic” [PAGE 1]. When two same-color players cross, the motion prior and the color prior both become ambiguous; the source admits ID switching in exactly that regime [PAGE 14]. Therefore a biomechanical occlusion event is a veto on identity coherence, not merely a detection error.

Weather veto proof: the method has no weather-invariant feature. Its distinguishing feature is manually selected RGB intensity [PAGE 6]. Rain, snow, shadow, glare, or broadcast color grading changes RGB intensities and can break the color mask. Since the source reports no weather ablation, the correct adjudication is veto: no weather-robust identity claim is licensed. The only licensed claim is the source’s conditional one: strong performance when jersey colors are separable and the referee is red, with explicit failure under same-color occlusion and non-red referee jerseys [PAGE 8, PAGE 14].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Prediction from the source: the referee will be detected as a player and tracked as a player, because the paper states exactly that failure for non-red referee jerseys [PAGE 8]. This is verifiable by frame-level referee class accuracy and referee-player ID-switch counts. The prediction is directional, not a fabricated exact score: referee-class accuracy should collapse relative to the red-jersey control, and referee-player ID switches should increase.

Verification predicate: select two players with the same jersey color, force a crossing or occlusion window with high bounding-box overlap, and count identity switches inside the window. Prediction from the source: JCD-SORT will still switch IDs when same-jersey players occlude each other, because the paper’s limitation says “when the player with the same jersey color is occluded, the ID of the player is switched” [PAGE 14]. Veto condition: if the identity-switch count is nonzero under same-color occlusion, the universal claim “prevents identity switching of the players even under severe occlusions” [PAGE 2] is vetoed; the surviving claim is only the conditional one.
