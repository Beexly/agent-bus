<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
This derivation sits at the confluence of several intellectual lineages. Directly, it descends from Clark L. Hull’s drive-reduction theory, which sought to explain behavior via quantifiable habit strengths and instinctive reaction potentials. Hull borrowed heavily from Thorndike’s law of effect and Pavlovian conditioning models, translating them into mathematical formulations. The use of exponential decay mirrors radioactive decay laws in physics, reflecting the era’s confidence in analogical reasoning between natural sciences and psychology.

Historically, the concept of stimulus generalization originated with Pavlov’s observations that conditioned responses occurred to tones similar to the conditioned tone. Later, researchers like Gantt and Spence developed gradient curves, but Hull was the first to propose a precise mathematical form. His choice of the exponential function was influenced by both empirical fits to data (like Hovland’s) and theoretical parsimony—it arises naturally from first-order differential equations describing decay processes.

The multi-week trap inherent in this approach lies in its mechanistic reductionism. By reducing all generalization to distance along a single continuum, it ignores multidimensional stimulus spaces and categorical boundaries. Modern cognitive science shows that humans generalize based on abstract features and causal relationships, not merely surface similarity. Thus, while the Hullian equation accurately predicts performance on simple discrimination tasks (e.g., brightness gradients), it fails miserably on conceptual transfer problems (e.g., applying a learned rule to a new domain). This historical artifact demonstrates how powerful but narrow formalisms can dominate a field until disruptive paradigms emerge.

Furthermore, the reliance on j.n.d.’s ties this work to Fechnerian psychophysics, which assumed logarithmic scaling of sensation. However, Stevens’ power law later challenged this view, suggesting different scaling rules for different modalities. Consequently, the very definition of ‘distance’ in the equation may be modality-specific, limiting cross-domain comparability—a subtle flaw embedded in its genealogy.
