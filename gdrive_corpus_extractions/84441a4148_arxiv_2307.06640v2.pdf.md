<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage runs from equilibrium theory to dynamical systems to constrained polynomial optimization. The introduction contrasts Nash equilibrium with three problems: multiplicity and equilibrium selection, computational intractability, and the failure of standard learning dynamics to converge to Nash equilibria [PAGE 1]. It invokes Smale’s argument that “equilibrium theory is far from satisfactory” and proposes to study multiagent problems through differential equations [PAGE 1]. That gives the retrocausal root of SIAR: rather than infer an equilibrium, infer the vector field that generates observed play.

The game-dynamics root includes replicator dynamics, multiplicative weights, gradient descent, and Q-learning dynamics. The paper notes that such dynamics can fail to converge and “can even be formally chaotic” [PAGE 1]. The chaos lineage is explicit: Sato, Akiyama, and Farmer studied chaos in learning a simple two-person game [PAGE 11], and Hu et al. later analyzed Lyapunov spectra in asymmetric rock-paper-scissors [PAGE 11]. SIAR’s RPS experiment is therefore a descendant of that chaos literature, not an isolated benchmark.

The side-information root comes from shape-constrained regression and physics-informed learning. The paper says SOS optimization for regressors satisfying side-information constraints was introduced in shape-constrained regression and then extended by Ahmadi et al. to dynamical-system identification from limited data [PAGE 3]. It distinguishes its constraints from physical conservation laws: “the side-information constraints used by Ahmadi et al. are more tailored to physical systems ... and not relevant to systems of strategic agents” [PAGE 3]. The optimization lineage includes Parrilo, Lasserre, Laurent, and Putinar-type certificates [PAGE 8]. The comparative benchmark lineage includes SINDy, which relies on sparse identification from data [PAGE 9].

The multi-week traps are delayed-failure modes. First, a low-degree polynomial can fit five samples and look globally correct on a phase plot, yet fail when the initial condition moves. The matching-pennies phase-plot result shows SINDy is “accurate only around the given dataset,” while SIAR is nearly identical to the true phase plot [PAGE 10]. The trap is mistaking a local fit for a global field. Second, side-information can become a self-fulfilling prior. If a designer imposes Nash stationarity or positive correlation when the true Q-learning exploration rate is positive, the solution is constrained toward the wrong behavioral class. The paper itself notes QREs coincide with Nash equilibria only when \(T=0\) [PAGE 6]. Third, the equilibrium-selection problem has \(\binom{n}{n/2}\) equilibria [PAGE 10], so a method that works for small \(n\) may degrade as the equilibrium landscape grows. Fourth, finite-difference velocity estimates from noisy profiles can amplify noise; the paper uses noisy \(\tilde{x}(t_k)\sim N(x(t_k),0.01)\) and finite differences in one experiment [PAGE 9]. Fifth, chaotic divergence after several Lyapunov times is a clock-based trap: short-horizon success can coexist with long-horizon failure. Sixth, the approximation guarantee is an existence statement over degrees, so a deployment that fixes \(d\) without validation inherits a hidden model-selection risk.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
There are three nested levels of theory of mind in the source. At the player level, each player’s update policy \(f_i\) depends on its own mixed strategy \(x_i\), its instantaneous payoffs \(v_i(x)\), and, for Q-learning, an entropy or exploration term controlled by \(T\) [PAGE 6, PAGE 14]. At the mechanism-designer level, the designer assumes the game \(G\) is known and treats \(f\) as the unknown behavioral object. The paper’s motivating question is behavioral: “how myopically do the agents learn?” [PAGE 2]. At the side-information level, the designer encodes beliefs about strategic behavior: forward invariance, anonymity, uncoupled form, Nash stationarity, positive correlation, and elimination of dominated strategies [PAGE 5-7]. These are theory-of-mind priors about what kind of agents the players are.

The crisis habit regression appears when data are scarce and the designer falls back to an equilibrium habit. In a crisis, the temptation is to assume Nash equilibrium, or to use an unconstrained local regressor such as SINDy, because those are familiar. The source warns against this: the equilibrium point of view is “limited in terms of its real world applicability” [PAGE 1], and standard learning dynamics may not converge to Nash. A crisis habit regression would therefore mistake a short observed trajectory for convergence to a Nash point when the true system is cycling, chaotic, or converging to a QRE. The paper’s Q-learning result is the clearest counterexample: fixed points are QREs, not necessarily Nash, unless \(T=0\) [PAGE 6].

The counter-trap is over-imposing behavioral priors. If the designer believes players must satisfy positive correlation, anonymity, or Nash stationarity, SIAR can use those constraints to recover a field from five samples. That is the method’s strength. But the same constraints can force a wrong field if the true players violate them. The anonymity constraint is especially sharp: it is justified only when the game has anonymous players, defined by identical strategy spaces and permutation-invariant utilities [PAGE 5]. If the game is not anonymous, imposing equation (8) is a false theory-of-mind assumption. Similarly, imposing Nash stationarity when \(T>0\) is false because QREs need not be Nash [PAGE 6]. The counter-trap is therefore “constraint as confirmation bias”: the designer’s model of the players becomes the answer rather than a hypothesis to test.

The clock is both statistical and behavioral. Statistically, the clock is \(\Delta T=t_K-t_1\), the short-run duration. The paper’s benchmarks use five samples and short windows; the equilibrium-selection evaluation uses \(t=200\) and \(\epsilon=0.01\) [PAGE 21]. Behaviorally, the clock is the Lyapunov time. In the RPS experiment, the SIAR solution is compared over at least 8 Lyapunov times [PAGE 12]. That is a bounded window. If the policymaker must act after a short observation window, the recursive problem is: the players are adapting on their own clock, the designer is estimating on a shorter clock, and the policy intervention can change the players’ future theory of mind. The paper’s future-work section explicitly moves from identification to control, where “small changes” to dynamical behavior could select equilibria or control chaos [PAGE 12]. Once control is introduced, players may anticipate or react to the intervention, so the time-invariant \(f\) assumption can fail. That is the deepest recursive trap: the act of modeling and controlling the system can change the system’s model of the controller.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source contains no biomechanical operator and no weather operator. Its physical-layer example is a traffic network: drivers choose routes, congestion affects latency, and the static part is “the latency function on different roads based on their current load” [PAGE 2]. The paper assumes the static payoff structure is fixed and known: “This part we will consider fixed and known” [PAGE 1]. Therefore the cross-layer veto is not a generic physical veto; it is an assumption-violation veto.

Physical-layer veto proof: SIAR’s guarantee is conditional on a fixed game \(G\), a fixed strategy space \(X\), and a time-invariant \(f\) satisfying the imposed side-information. If an external physical variable \(w(t)\), such as weather or road condition, changes the latency function so that \(u_i=u_i(x,w(t))\), then the true dynamics are non-autonomous,
\[
\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t),w(t)),
\]
and no single time-invariant \(f(x)\) can match the observed flow for all \(t\). A polynomial \(p(x)\) fitted on the observed window can only approximate an average or a slice. The Corollary 1 guarantee, which is stated for a fixed \(f\in C^1(X)\) and fixed side-information [PAGE 9], does not apply. The veto condition is: if weather changes payoffs, strategy availability, or observation times within the fitting or deployment window, then cross-layer extrapolation is vetoed unless \(w(t)\) is included as a state variable or the model is re-identified.

Biomechanical-layer veto proof: the source assumes each player has a finite ordered strategy set \(A_i\) and mixed strategy simplex \(X_i\) [PAGE 3]. If a biomechanical constraint changes the feasible action set, the simplex, or the mapping from intended to executed strategies, then the estimated \(f\) on the old \(X\) is not the same object on the new \(X\). Anonymity and forward-invariance constraints may also become invalid if players no longer share identical strategy spaces [PAGE 5]. The veto is: any layer that alters \(X\), the payoff mapping \(u\), or the player identities breaks the side-information semantics, so the SIAR solution cannot be transported across that layer without re-specification.

Weather-layer veto proof: the source has no weather variable, so a direct weather veto is not inferable. Weather can act only through measurable channels: changed payoffs, changed noise, changed sampling intervals, or missing observations. The paper’s noisy experiment uses \(K=5\) samples with \(\tilde{x}(t_k)\sim N(x(t_k),0.01)\) and finite-difference velocities [PAGE 9]. If weather increases noise or creates irregular \(\Delta T\), the finite-difference velocity error grows, and the residual can be dominated by observation error rather than model error. The veto is therefore conditional: weather vetoes only if it violates the fixed-\(G\), fixed-\(X\), time-invariant-\(f\), or bounded-noise assumptions. A weather change that merely rescales time uniformly can be absorbed into the time unit; a weather change that alters relative payoffs cannot.
