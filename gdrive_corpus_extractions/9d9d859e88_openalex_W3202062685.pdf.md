The source is a survey of deep-learning-based entity linking (EL), so the deepest valid reasoning chain is not a sports or physical prediction chain; it is an information-processing chain from text mention to knowledge-base entity. The survey states the core task on [PAGE 1]: “Entity linking is the task to link entity mentions appearing in web text with their corresponding entities in a KB [5].” On [PAGE 2] the formal definition adds a document \(D\), recognized mentions \(M=\{m_1,\dots,m_{|M|}\}\), and a target KB \(E=\{e_1,\dots,e_{|E|}\}\), with the goal of mapping each \(m_i\) to its gold entity \(e_i\). The same page introduces the crucial gray-zone label: “A special label NIL is usually used to describe this kind of unlinkable entity mention.” Thus the real operator is not simply \(M\to E\); it is \(M\to E\cup\{\mathrm{NIL}\}\), and any pipeline that omits NIL has an a priori false-link regime.

The survey’s taxonomy on [PAGE 3] gives the multi-hop derivation: embedding \(\rightarrow\) feature \(\rightarrow\) algorithm. It says the three steps are “in a sequence manner, which means the output of the previous step is used as the input of the current step” [PAGE 3]. The first hop converts words, mentions, entities, and alignments into dense vectors. The second hop converts vector similarities and priors into features: prior popularity, surface-form similarity, type similarity, context similarity, and topical coherence [PAGE 3]. The third hop converts features into a ranking score and then chooses the highest-scoring candidate. The paper states on [PAGE 14]: “For each entity mention, the candidate entity with the highest ranking score is chosen as the output mapping entity.” This is the local argmax operator. If the system uses document-level coherence, the argmax is no longer independent per mention; it becomes a collective assignment problem. The survey explicitly warns on [PAGE 12] that “the optimization of this feature is an NP-hard problem” because the assignment of temporary mapping entities is unknown before EL is finished. That is the key multi-hop break: local scoring is decomposable, global coherence is combinatorial.

A gray-zone audit must start with ambiguity examples. On [PAGE 1] the survey uses “MJ” as a mention that may refer to “Michael Jordan,” “Michael Jackson,” “Mj Rodriguez,” or many other entities, and “NYC”/“Big Apple” as surface-form variation for “New York City.” This is not a toy ambiguity; it means a single surface string can have a candidate set with radically different types and popularity distributions. The survey further notes on [PAGE 8] that “the accuracy of using this prior popularity feature alone can reach 70% −85% [43].” That is a strong baseline, but it is also a trap: popular entities dominate, tail entities are suppressed, and short or noisy documents provide too little context to override the prior. The KORE50 dataset description on [PAGE 15] says “most entity mentions are first names referring to persons with high level of ambiguity,” which is exactly the gray zone where prior popularity is dangerous.

The second gray zone is document length. The survey reports on [PAGE 17] that WNED-CWEB has “approximately 1700 words per document,” “much longer than the maximum word length that BERT can deal with (i.e., 512 words).” Therefore a Transformer model that truncates to 512 tokens cannot see the full document, and its global-coherence feature is partially blind. The survey says Yamada et al. [72] “performs not well on WNED-CWEB” for this reason, while SGTB-BiBSG [52] is stronger because it “designs various hand-crafted features capturing document-level contextual information” [PAGE 17]. This is a direct multi-hop consequence: longer document \(\rightarrow\) truncation \(\rightarrow\) missing global evidence \(\rightarrow\) degraded collective disambiguation.

The third gray zone is evaluation. The survey defines micro and macro precision, recall, F1, and accuracy on [PAGE 16], and notes that most DL-based EL methods use micro-F1. A system can appear strong on micro-F1 because documents with many mentions dominate the aggregate, while its macro behavior on short or sparse documents is weak. The survey itself concludes on [PAGE 17]: “no perfect EL system can achieve the best results on all data sets.” That is the central audit result: EL performance is data- and domain-dependent, and a method that wins on AIDA-CoNLL may lose on KORE50, WNED-CWEB, or zero-shot settings. The gray-zone audit therefore rejects any single-score claim of universal superiority; it must condition on candidate generation, NIL handling, document length, KB/domain match, and metric aggregation.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source contains no physical SI units; its quantities are dimensionless real vectors, probabilities in \([0,1]\), anchor-text occurrence counts, and loss values in nats when natural logarithms are used. The formal EL operator from [PAGE 2] is:
\[
\mathrm{EL}:\{m_i\}_{i=1}^{|M|}\to \{e_i\}_{i=1}^{|M|},\qquad e_i\in E\cup\{\mathrm{NIL}\},
\]
with the assumption that mention boundaries are provided: “Most of the existing EL works assume that entity mention boundaries are provided by users or mention detection systems” [PAGE 2]. Breakdown: if the gold entity is absent from the candidate set \(C(m_i)\), no downstream ranking operator can recover it; if NIL is disallowed, unlinkable mentions are forced into \(E\).

The CNN mention-embedding operator on [PAGE 5] is:
\[
\mathrm{conv}(w_{1:n}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n-z}\max\{0,\,W w_{i:i+z}\},
\]
where \(w_{i:i+z}\) is a concatenation of word embeddings, \(W\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times dz}\) is a filter bank, \(k\) is the number of filter maps, \(d\) is word-embedding dimension, and \(z\) is convolution width. Units: vector entries and \(W\) are dimensionless; \(n,z,k,d\) are positive integers. Breakdown: if \(n<z\), the sum is empty; if embeddings are zero vectors, the resulting representation carries no lexical signal.

The prior-popularity feature on [PAGE 8] is:
\[
p(e|m)=\frac{\mathrm{count}(m,e)}{\mathrm{count}(m)}.
\]
Units: \(\mathrm{count}(m,e)\) and \(\mathrm{count}(m)\) are anchor-text occurrence counts; \(p(e|m)\) is dimensionless probability in \([0,1]\). Assumption: Wikipedia anchor-text statistics are representative of the target domain. Breakdown: \(\mathrm{count}(m)=0\) gives an undefined ratio; \(\mathrm{count}(m,e)=0\) gives zero prior for a valid but unseen candidate; domain shift breaks the estimate. The survey’s claim is empirical: “the accuracy of using this prior popularity feature alone can reach 70% −85% [43]” [PAGE 8].

The attention context embedding on [PAGE 10] is:
\[
c=F(U(V,A))V,
\]
where \(V\) is the value matrix, \(A\) is the attention-vector matrix, \(U(\cdot)\) computes attention weights, and \(F(\cdot)\) is usually softmax. Units: all matrices are dimensionless; \(c\) is a dimensionless context vector. Breakdown: if all attention weights collapse to one value, the context embedding ignores all other context words; if \(V\) is empty, no context representation exists.

The context-similarity features on [PAGE 10–11] are:
\[
f_{\mathrm{cnt}}(m,e)=\frac{c\cdot e}{\|c\|\|e\|},
\qquad
f_{\mathrm{cnt}}(m,e)=cWe^{T}.
\]
Units: cosine and bilinear scores are dimensionless. Assumptions: \(c\) and \(e\) are in compatible spaces; for cosine, nonzero norms. Breakdown: \(\|c\|=0\) or \(\|e\|=0\) makes cosine undefined; if word and entity embeddings are not aligned, the score is semantically meaningless. The paper states on [PAGE 7–8] that alignment embedding is necessary “so that similar words and entities are placed close to each other in a common space” [PAGE 8].

The topical-coherence operator on [PAGE 11] is:
\[
f_{\mathrm{coh}}(e|D)=\frac{1}{|M_s|}\sum_{m'\in M_s}\psi(e,e^*_{m'}),
\]
where \(M_s\) is the selected mention set in document \(D\), \(e^*_{m'}\) is a temporarily assigned mapping entity, and \(\psi(\cdot)\) is pairwise entity similarity. Units: dimensionless. Assumption: co-occurring mentions are topically coherent. Breakdown: \(|M_s|=0\) makes the average undefined; temporary assignments can be wrong; the survey says the optimization is “NP-hard” [PAGE 12] because the assignment \(e^*_{m'}\) is unknown before EL completes.

The max-margin loss on [PAGE 14] is:
\[
L=\max(0,\gamma-\Phi(m,e^+)+\Phi(m,e^-)),
\]
where \(\Phi\) is a dimensionless ranking score and \(\gamma\) is a dimensionless safety margin. Breakdown: if no negative entity \(e^-\) is sampled, the loss is not defined as a ranking contrast; if \(\gamma\) is too large, the model can be pushed toward unstable gradients. The binary cross-entropy loss on [PAGE 14] is:
\[
L=-(y\log\Phi(m,e)+(1-y)\log(1-\Phi(m,e))),
\]
with \(y\in\{0,1\}\). Units: nats per instance if natural log is used. Breakdown: \(\Phi=0\) with \(y=1\), or \(\Phi=1\) with \(y=0\), produces infinite loss; numerical clipping is required.

The evaluation metrics on [PAGE 16] are:
\[
P_{\mathrm{mic}}=\frac{|E_S\cap G_S|}{|G_S|},\quad
R_{\mathrm{mic}}=\frac{|E_S\cap G_S|}{|E_S|},\quad
F1_{\mathrm{mic}}=\frac{2P_{\mathrm{mic}}R_{\mathrm{mic}}}{P_{\mathrm{mic}}+R_{\mathrm{mic}}},
\]
and
\[
A_{\mathrm{mic}}=\frac{|E_S\cap G_S|}{|M_S|}.
\]
Units: proportions in \([0,1]\), often reported as percentages. Breakdown: if \(G_S=\varnothing\), precision is undefined; if \(E_S=\varnothing\), recall is undefined; micro aggregation can let long documents dominate.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The survey’s present taxonomy retroactively reorders a long lineage. Cucerzan’s 2007 large-scale named entity disambiguation based on Wikipedia [10] and Milne and Witten’s 2008 WLM [88] establish the prior-popularity and anchor-text relatedness tradition that the survey still uses in [PAGE 8] and [PAGE 11]. Hoffart et al.’s 2011 robust disambiguation work [15] and the AIDA-CoNLL dataset [PAGE 15] make collective, document-level disambiguation a benchmark center. Word2Vec [17], GloVe [19], ELMo [79], and BERT [83] are the embedding lineage that the survey’s “embedding” axis inherits; the paper explicitly notes on [PAGE 2] that DL methods use “Word2Vec [17], [18] and GloVe [19]” and on [PAGE 10] that BERT is “a widely used Transformers-based pre-trained language model in context embedding learning.” Ganea and Hofmann’s Deep-ED [43] becomes the anchor for bilinear context similarity and entity embeddings [PAGE 10–11], and Le and Titov [46], [55], Wu et al. [31], and Yamada et al. [72] extend it into latent relations, dynamic GCNs, and contextualized entity embeddings.

The retrocausal lesson is that today’s “deep” EL is not a clean break from classical EL; it is a re-encoding of classical features. Prior popularity remains in Eq. 9 [PAGE 8], surface-form similarity remains a feature [PAGE 8], type similarity remains a constraint [PAGE 8–9], and topical coherence remains the collective core [PAGE 11–13]. The deep models mainly change how those features are represented and optimized: learned embeddings replace hand-crafted strings, attention and GNNs replace fixed similarity matrices, and BERT replaces bag-of-words context. The multi-week trap is therefore benchmark overfitting: a team can spend weeks tuning a Transformer on AIDA-CoNLL and still fail on KORE50 or WNED-CWEB because the dataset properties differ. The survey states on [PAGE 17] that “no perfect EL system can achieve the best results on all data sets,” and on [PAGE 18] that “most existing DL based EL systems were designed and optimized for a speciﬁc domain.” A second multi-week trap is popularity bias: a model can look strong because the train/test distribution rewards famous entities, while tail entities and NIL cases remain weak. A third trap is long-document truncation: BERT’s 512-token limit [PAGE 17] silently deletes global evidence, and only cross-dataset testing reveals it. A fourth trap is error propagation in collective linking: early false links become “already disambiguated entities” and can contaminate later coherence features; the survey notes on [PAGE 13] that “to alleviate error propagation caused by falsely linked entities, a gate mechanism is introduced.” These are week-scale failures because they appear only after repeated evaluation across genres, lengths, and KBs.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind reading of EL has three levels. Level 0 is the writer’s surface choice: why this mention form rather than a full name? Level 1 is the reader/system’s local belief about the intended entity from the surrounding context. Level 2 is the document-level belief that co-occurring entities are topically coherent. Level 3 is the evaluator’s belief about whether the system generalizes beyond the benchmark. The survey’s pipeline mirrors these levels: mention embedding and context embedding are Level 1 [PAGE 4–5, 9–10], topical coherence is Level 2 [PAGE 11–13], and the dataset/tool analysis is Level 3 [PAGE 15–18].

The crisis habit regression is the collapse to prior popularity when context is missing, noisy, or truncated. Equation 9 on [PAGE 8] is a habit: \(p(e|m)\) is a strong prior, and the paper says it alone can reach 70%−85% accuracy. In a crisis—short tweet, ambiguous first name, unseen domain—the system regresses to the most popular candidate. This is not a bug in the prior; it is a rational Bayesian response when the likelihood is weak. The counter-trap is overcorrection: adding global coherence can make the system overconfident in a wrong document topic, and false links can propagate. The survey’s DCA gate on [PAGE 13] is an explicit counter-trap mechanism: it controls “which part of history cues should be inherited” to reduce error propagation. The clock is the hard constraint that limits recursion: BERT’s 512-token window [PAGE 17], the NP-hard coherence assignment [PAGE 12], and the sequential cost of RNNs versus Transformers [PAGE 10] all bound how much recursive context can be processed. Thus the correct system design is not “always use global coherence” or “always trust the prior”; it is a clock-aware arbitration between local likelihood, document coherence, and candidate-set coverage.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
No physical, biomechanical, or weather variables appear in [PAGE 1–20]. The source is a computational survey of text-to-KB linking, not a physical-system model. Therefore a physical/biomechanical/weather veto cannot be proven from this source; any such claim would be fabricated. The defensible cross-layer vetoes are information-theoretic and computational.

First veto: candidate generation. The survey describes candidate generation as the first stage on [PAGE 2], and the ranking stage can only rank entities already in \(C(m_i)\). If the gold entity is absent from \(C(m_i)\), the embedding layer, feature layer, and algorithm layer cannot recover it. This is a hard upper bound, not a tunable error. Second veto: NIL handling. The survey explicitly defines NIL for unlinkable mentions on [PAGE 2]. If NIL is disabled, unlinkable mentions must be forced to some KB entity, producing false links and reducing precision. Third veto: input-length truncation. BERT can take only 512 tokens [PAGE 17], while WNED-CWEB documents are about 1700 words [PAGE 17]. A Transformer that truncates cannot attend to the full document; its global-coherence feature is structurally incomplete. Fourth veto: domain/KB mismatch. The survey says on [PAGE 18] that most DL-based EL systems were “designed and optimized for a speciﬁc domain” and for a specific KB. A model trained on Wikipedia/Freebase news may not transfer to tweets, biomedical text, or another KB without retraining or alignment. Fifth veto: evaluation aggregation. Micro-F1 on [PAGE 16] can mask poor macro behavior on short or sparse documents. These five vetoes are sufficient to reject a universal EL claim; physical/biomechanical/weather layers are simply not identifiable in this source.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The RLVR-style counterfactual vetoes below are verifiable by public datasets and the survey’s metrics in Eq. 22–23 [PAGE 16]. The reward is binary: 1 if the observed metric direction matches the prediction, 0 otherwise.

Veto 1: prior-popularity ablation. Remove Eq. 9 [PAGE 8] and replace \(p(e|m)\) with a uniform prior, holding the rest of a deep EL model fixed. Prediction: micro-F1 will decrease on ambiguous short-text data such as KORE50, where the survey says “most entity mentions are first names referring to persons with high level of ambiguity” [PAGE 15], and the relative degradation will be larger on KORE50 than on AIDA-B because AIDA-B has many mentions per document and stronger document-level coherence. Verification: retrain and compare \(F1_{\mathrm{mic}}\) on KORE50 versus AIDA-B using the same candidate generation and encoder. This veto is directly tied to the survey’s claim that prior popularity alone can reach 70%−85% accuracy [PAGE 8].

Veto 2: 512-token truncation. Force a BERT-based EL model to read only the first 512 wordpieces of every document, then evaluate on WNED-CWEB. Prediction: the Transformer model will degrade on WNED-CWEB relative to a model with document-level hand-crafted features, because the survey reports that WNED-CWEB has “approximately 1700 words per document” and that BERT’s maximum is “512 words” [PAGE 17]. The survey already observes that Yamada et al. [72] “performs not well on WNED-CWEB” while SGTB-BiBSG [52] performs well through hand-crafted document-level features [PAGE 17]. Verification: compare truncated versus full-document or feature-augmented variants on \(F1_{\mathrm{mic}}\) for WNED-CWEB.

Veto 3: NIL disabled. Evaluate an EL system on a test set containing unlinkable mentions, then forbid the NIL label and force every mention into \(E\). Prediction: precision will drop because unlinkable mentions become false positives, while recall on linkable mentions will be approximately unchanged or slightly altered by candidate-set interactions. This follows directly from the NIL definition on [PAGE 2] and the precision/recall definitions on [PAGE 16]. Verification: compute \(P_{\mathrm{mic}}\), \(R_{\mathrm{mic}}\), and \(F1_{\mathrm{mic}}\) with and without NIL on a NIL-annotated split. A positive veto is recorded if precision falls in the NIL-disabled condition without a compensating recall gain.
