<THINK_TRACE>
The source’s argument is a multi-hop chain, not a single “load causes injury” statement. First, football data are generated from internal and external load streams: physiological and psychological markers on one side, and EPTS variables such as velocity, acceleration, and distance on the other; the paper states, “Load can be broadly classified into two types: internal and external” [PAGE 2]. Second, those streams are transformed into candidate features such as ACWR, MSWR, EWMA, session load, monotony, psychological ratings, previous injury, and anthropometric or biomechanical variables. Third, a supervised classifier maps the feature vector to an injury/non-injury label. Fourth, evaluation uses accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, F1-score, and AUC; the source notes that these metrics “are often expressed in percentages” and that AUC is “the probability curve of the true positive rate and false positive rate” [PAGE 3]. Fifth, a practitioner decides whether to alter training. Each hop has a failure mode, so the gray-zone audit must be layer-by-layer.

The first gray zone is construct validity. The paper explicitly warns that “the vast array of variables used in analysis as proxy indicators of player load, alongside differences in approach to key aspects of data treatment—such as response to data imbalance, model fitting, and a lack of multi-season data—limit a systematic evaluation of findings and the drawing of a unified conclusion” [PAGE 1]. Therefore, a high-performing model in one study cannot be treated as a universal injury law. The second gray zone is the workload–injury paradox: the source says “intense workloads may also be associated with injury resilience” [PAGE 2], while also reporting that “ACWR values exceeding 1.5 have been shown to lead to a 2–4 times greater injury risk over the following week, with an optimal range for ACWR suggested as between 0.85 and 1.35” [PAGE 2]. These two statements are not contradictory if ACWR is a context-dependent summary rather than a causal threshold. A practitioner who treats 1.5 as a hard biological boundary has collapsed a statistical association into a rule.

The third gray zone is imbalance. Injury datasets contain “a far higher number of negative examples (i.e. non-injuries) than positive examples (i.e. injuries)” [PAGE 3], and models “tend to learn from those data points present in the highest numbers . . . and subsequently predict those non-injuries well, but fail to predict injuries” [PAGE 3]. Accuracy can therefore look excellent while injury recall is poor. The paper’s Table 3 illustrates this asymmetry: one supervised principal components model reports accuracy 88.80, precision 55, and recall 33, while another reports accuracy 97.07, precision 19, and recall 20 [PAGE 9]. The fourth gray zone is leakage. The authors recommend that “any training and input data from the same day that an injury has occurred should be disregarded, because such data may be confounded by the injury occurrence” [PAGE 7]. If same-day post-injury data enter training or testing, the model learns the injury’s consequences, not its antecedents. The fifth gray zone is balancing leakage: “pre-processing techniques are applied to the training, validation, and test data, but balancing techniques are only applied to the training data” [PAGE 4]. Balancing the test set creates a world that does not exist at deployment.

The sixth gray zone is temporal transport. “Traditional machine learning algorithms assume that the underlying distribution of the data is the same” [PAGE 8], but football changes players, coaches, training regimes, and match schedules across seasons. The source therefore points toward “stream learning” for multi-season data [PAGE 8]. The seventh gray zone is interpretation. Black-box models may predict well but are opaque; SHAP and similar methods explain model output, not biomechanical causation. The paper notes that black-box algorithms require “additional post hoc methods . . . to interpret and understand their results” [PAGE 3]. Thus the correct audit conclusion is conditional: machine learning is a feature-ranking and risk-stratification aid, not a substitute for clinical examination, biomechanical screening, or a causal workload model. The model output should be vetoed when protocol validity, temporal validity, or physical red flags fail.

Below, each formalization is tied to the source description; where the source gives only a verbal definition, the equation is a standard representation of that definition, not a new claim from the paper.

1. Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio. Let \(L_t\) be training load at day or session \(t\), in arbitrary units (AU), with the same measurement scale across the window. The source defines ACWR as “the ratio of acute (i.e. rolling average of training load completed in the past week) to chronic (i.e. rolling average of training load completed in the past 4–6 weeks) workload” [PAGE 2]. A formal representation is
\[
A_t=\frac{1}{7}\sum_{k=0}^{6}L_{t-k},\qquad
C_t=\frac{1}{W}\sum_{k=0}^{W-1}L_{t-k},\qquad
R_t^{\mathrm{ACWR}}=\frac{A_t}{C_t},
\]
where \(W\in\{28,\ldots,42\}\) days if daily data are used. ACWR is dimensionless because AU cancels. Assumptions: complete daily load records, consistent units, no undocumented imputation, and a chronic window fixed before analysis. Breakdown regimes: \(C_t=0\) makes \(R_t^{\mathrm{ACWR}}\) undefined; very small \(C_t\) produces unstable ratios; mixing session-level and daily-level \(L_t\) changes the denominator; different \(W\) values are not comparable. The source itself notes “known limitations” of ACWR [PAGE 2].

2. Monotony and MSWR. The source defines monotony as “the ratio of the mean and standard deviation of training loads—the sum of all session loads—recorded each week” [PAGE 2]. For weekly session loads \(L_{w,1},\ldots,L_{w,n_w}\),
\[
\bar L_w=\frac{1}{n_w}\sum_{i=1}^{n_w}L_{w,i},\qquad
s_w=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_w-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_w}(L_{w,i}-\bar L_w)^2},\qquad
M_w=\frac{\bar L_w}{s_w}.
\]
\(M_w\) is dimensionless when mean and standard deviation are in the same AU. Assumptions: \(n_w>1\), \(s_w>0\), and weekly aggregation is defined identically across players. Breakdown regimes: \(s_w=0\) gives division by zero; small \(n_w\) makes \(s_w\) unstable; combining different session types without normalization makes the standard deviation depend on scheduling artifacts. The paper also reports MSWR, the “ratio of mean and standard deviation (MSWR)” [PAGE 5], as a model feature.

3. Session load. The source states: “Session load [18] is the product of RPE of training sessions and the duration of those sessions” [PAGE 2]. Formally,
\[
SL_{i,s}=\mathrm{RPE}_{i,s}\times D_{i,s},
\]
with RPE dimensionless on its chosen scale and \(D_{i,s}\) in minutes, so \(SL\) has units of RPE·min, often reported as AU. Assumptions: RPE and duration refer to the same session and player; RPE scale is not changed mid-dataset. Breakdown: missing RPE, zero duration, or a scale change makes session loads non-comparable.

4. Exponentially weighted moving average. The paper mentions “the exponentially moving average (EWMA) of each external training load variable” [PAGE 5] but does not give the recursion. A standard form is
\[
E_t=\alpha L_t+(1-\alpha)E_{t-1},\qquad 0<\alpha\le 1,
\]
or \(\alpha=1-e^{-\Delta t/\tau}\) for time step \(\Delta t\) and time constant \(\tau\). \(E_t\) has the same units as \(L_t\). Assumptions: initialization \(E_0\), equal or explicitly modeled time spacing, and stable measurement scale. Breakdown: poorly chosen \(\alpha\) overweights noise or lags injury-relevant spikes; missing sessions break the recursion; nonstationary load scales make the smoothing constant non-transportable.

5. Classification metrics. Let TP, FP, FN, and TN be counts for injury as the positive class. The source defines accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, and F1-score in verbal form [PAGE 3]. The standard equations are
\[
\mathrm{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN},\qquad
\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},
\]
\[
\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad
\mathrm{Specificity}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP},
\]
\[
F_1=\frac{2\,\mathrm{Precision}\,\mathrm{Recall}}{\mathrm{Precision}+\mathrm{Recall}}.
\]
These are dimensionless proportions in \([0,1]\). AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen injury case receives a higher score than a randomly chosen non-injury case, also dimensionless in \([0,1]\). Breakdown regimes: \(TP+FP=0\) makes precision undefined; \(TP+FN=0\) makes recall undefined; \(TN+FP=0\) makes specificity undefined; \(F_1\) is undefined when precision and recall are both zero; AUC is undefined if only one class is present. The source warns that AUC “has also been noted to be misleading with imbalanced data [29]” [PAGE 4], and that per-class metrics matter because an averaged score can hide poor minority-class performance [PAGE 4].

6. Imbalance. Let \(\pi_+=N_+/N\) be the injury prevalence. The source’s imbalanced-data problem occurs when \(\pi_+\ll 0.5\) and the classifier maximizes negative-class accuracy [PAGE 3]. Oversampling, undersampling, SMOTE, and ADASYN are resampling operators applied to the training set; the source states that “balancing techniques are only applied to the training data” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown: applying these operators to validation or test data changes the class prior and invalidates deployment estimates; synthetic injury points can distort feature geometry if generated without player-level constraints.

7. Model-output explanation. SHAP is mentioned as an interpretation method [PAGE 6], with an additive attribution form
\[
f(x)=\phi_0+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_j,
\]
where \(f(x)\) is the model output and \(\phi_j\) are feature attributions in model-output units such as log-odds. Assumptions: the model is fixed and the attribution method is applied consistently. Breakdown: correlated features can split or duplicate attribution, and SHAP values are not causal effects.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage in the source runs from physiological load models to ratio-based monitoring and then to machine learning. The paper traces an early systems idea in which Banister and colleagues “described differences between a positive fitness function and a negative fatigue function” [PAGE 2]. It then describes the “10% rule” as protection when “week-to-week workload changes do not exceed 10%” [PAGE 2], the ACWR model from Hulin and colleagues as the “most popular and well-researched model of the injury process” [PAGE 2], Foster’s session-load construct as “the product of RPE of training sessions and the duration of those sessions” [PAGE 2], and monotony as the mean-to-standard-deviation ratio of weekly loads [PAGE 2]. The machine-learning lineage is separate: supervised learning, classification, white-box versus black-box algorithms, train–validation split, cross-validation, hyperparameter optimization, and post hoc explainability [PAGE 3–4]. The paper’s “retrocausal” feature is that later ML work re-selects earlier load constructs as features: Rossi and colleagues use ACWR, MSWR, and EWMA [PAGE 5], while later studies use 29, 65, 151, 229, or fewer variables [PAGE 5–6]. Thus the historical construct becomes a model input, and the model’s performance retroactively determines which historical variable appears “important.”

Multi-week traps follow directly from the time scales. ACWR’s chronic window is 4–6 weeks [PAGE 2]; therefore a single day’s load cannot be interpreted without the preceding month. Vallance and colleagues explicitly model “the upcoming week” and “the following month” [PAGE 6], which means the prediction target is not one clock but two. The paper warns that same-day data after injury are confounded [PAGE 7], so a weekly model must separate pre-injury exposures from post-injury consequences. The single-season limitation is another trap: “the studies examined here are based on data collected across a single season” [PAGE 7], and “changes of coaches, managers, players, and training regimes across seasons mean that the underlying distribution and quality of data will vary from season to season” [PAGE 7]. A model trained on one season can therefore encode that season’s coaching regime as if it were biology. The proposed remedy is multi-season testing and “stream learning” [PAGE 8]. A final lineage trap is metric genealogy: studies do not use the same metrics, and “studies employ some but not all of, and not the same, metrics” [PAGE 4], so Table 3 comparisons are not like-for-like. The historical lesson is that ACWR and monotony are not replaced by ML; they become features whose meaning depends on window length, aggregation, and the model’s evaluation clock.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The practitioner’s crisis psychology has three modes. “Crisis habit regression” is the retreat to a single familiar rule under injury pressure: reduce load, check ACWR, apply the 10% rule, or trust a dashboard score. The source creates a legitimate basis for caution but also a trap: it reports an ACWR “optimal range” and a risk increase above 1.5 [PAGE 2], yet it also says “intense workloads may also be associated with injury resilience” [PAGE 2]. Habit regression selects the first half and ignores the second. It also ignores the source’s warning that current models “fail to sufficiently capture the nuances of this relationship” [PAGE 1]. The counter-trap is overcorrection: because high load may be protective, staff may dismiss load monitoring altogether, or because a black-box model reports a high probability, staff may treat the score as a clinical verdict. The source warns that black-box outputs are opaque and require post hoc interpretation [PAGE 3], and that SHAP-style explanation is interpretation, not causation [PAGE 6]. The disciplined counter-trap is to use ML as a ranking and triage layer while retaining clinical and biomechanical vetoes.

The “clock” is the recursive part. A prediction made at time \(t\) changes training at \(t+1\), which changes the load features and injury labels observed at \(t+2\). The model is therefore inside a performative loop: practitioners act on the score, and the action alters the future distribution. The source’s recommendation to disregard same-day post-injury data [PAGE 7] is a clock rule, and its discussion of stream learning [PAGE 8] is a distribution-shift rule. Vallance and colleagues’ week-versus-month models [PAGE 6] imply that the same athlete can have different risk rankings at different horizons. The recursive theory-of-mind conclusion is that a model’s authority must be time-indexed: a seven-day score cannot adjudicate a season-long loading plan, and a monthly score cannot justify ignoring an acute biomechanical red flag. The correct habit is not “trust the model” or “ignore the model,” but “state the horizon, state the class prior, state the leakage controls, and state the veto layer.”
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto means that a statistical injury probability cannot override a lower-layer physical, biomechanical, or environmental contraindication. The source supplies direct evidence that biomechanical and physical variables can dominate model rankings. Oliver and colleagues found that “only single leg counter movement jump (SLCMJ) peak vertical ground reaction force asymmetry remained a significant contributor to injury” in their multivariate logistic analysis [PAGE 6], and across their ensemble models “SLCMJ asymmetry figured prominently” [PAGE 6]. López-Valenciano and colleagues identified “history of lower extremity muscle injury in the last season, peak torque knee flexor concentric 300 degree dominant leg, and sport devaluation” among key variables [PAGE 5]. Ayala and colleagues identified “history of hamstring strain injury last season, sleep quality, reduced sense of accomplishment, and range of motion-passive hip flexion with the knee extended-dominant leg” [PAGE 5–6]. Venturelli and colleagues found “previous injuries, height, and percentage difference between two kinds of jumps (countermovement jump and squat jump)” to be significant [PAGE 6]. These are not merely “features”; they are physical or biomechanical findings that a clinician can examine. A model probability estimated without a full biomechanical examination is therefore conditionally valid at best.

Formal veto rule: let \(S_i(t)\in[0,1]\) be the model’s injury probability for player \(i\) at time \(t\), and let \(B_i(t)\in\{0,1\}\) be a clinically verified biomechanical red flag derived from an examination that is not fully represented in the model. If \(B_i(t)=1\), then \(S_i(t)\) cannot be used to clear the athlete for a load increment; the clinical flag vetoes the score. This is an audit rule, not a source-defined threshold, because the source reports significant contributors but not validated cutoffs. The same logic applies to previous injury history: the source repeatedly finds previous injury important [PAGE 5–6], so a model that omits or underweights it is structurally incomplete.

Weather requires a stricter adjudication. The source’s feature table includes “Surface condition” and “Footwear” among candidate variables [PAGE 8], but it does not present a validated meteorological model. Therefore a weather-based veto cannot be claimed from this paper as an empirical result. It can be justified only as an external-validity veto: if \(W(t)\) is weather or surface state and the model was trained on \(W(t)\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{train}}\), then a score is not transportable to \(W(t)\notin\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{train}}\) unless \(W\) is included or the model is reweighted. The source’s own warning that studies differ in load variables and lack consensus [PAGE 1] supports this conservative reading. The veto hierarchy is therefore: verified biomechanical or medical red flag first; missing or shifted environmental layer second; model probability last. A high model score cannot erase a positive biomechanical test, and a low model score cannot authorize exposure when the environmental or clinical layer is outside the training distribution.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR-style veto tests are verifiable protocol counterfactuals: if a model depends on a forbidden information channel, the counterfactual removes that channel and the predicted performance change is checked against the source’s rules.

Veto 1 — same-day leakage. Counterfactual: train and test the model once with same-day post-injury inputs retained, and once with all inputs from the injury day removed, following the source’s rule that “any training and input data from the same day that an injury has occurred should be disregarded, because such data may be confounded by the injury occurrence” [PAGE 7]. Verifiable prediction: the leaky model will show inflated apparent recall or AUC because it can learn post-injury consequences; the leak-free model will show lower but more prospective performance. Veto criterion: if the leak-free model’s minority-class recall collapses, the original claim of prospective injury prediction is vetoed and the model is reclassified as a concurrent injury detector.

Veto 2 — balancing leakage in validation or test. Counterfactual: apply SMOTE, ADASYN, random oversampling, or undersampling to the test set versus training-only balancing. The source is explicit: “balancing techniques are only applied to the training data” and “balancing of the validation or test data would be undesirable, because assessment of the trained model would not reflect its application and performance with real-world (and unbalanced) data” [PAGE 4]. Verifiable prediction: balanced-test evaluation will inflate accuracy and possibly recall relative to the real-world class prior; training-only balancing will yield lower but more honest deployment estimates. Veto criterion: any reported metric computed on a balanced test set is vetoed as a deployment estimate, though it may remain a diagnostic of ranking behavior.

Veto 3 — single-season transport and stream shift. Counterfactual: train on season 1 and test on season 2, versus random cross-validation within season 1. The source warns that “traditional machine learning algorithms assume that the underlying distribution of the data is the same” [PAGE 8] and that “changes of coaches, managers, players, and training regimes across seasons mean that the underlying distribution and quality of data will vary from season to season” [PAGE 7]. Verifiable prediction: temporal holdout will reduce AUC, recall, and calibration relative to within-season cross-validation, and SHAP feature rankings will be less stable. Veto criterion: if a model’s performance is reported only from within-season cross-validation and it fails the next-season temporal holdout, the claim of generalizable injury prediction is vetoed; the model must be reframed as season-specific or updated by stream learning [PAGE 8].
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
