<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Formal operators and equations, with units and page citations.

Let (x, y) be deviations from the respective means, measured in units of probable error (PE), so both coordinates are dimensionless counts of PE. Let ρ be the dimensionless product-moment correlation, −1 < ρ < 1. Let z be the frequency density; if x and y carry physical units, [z] = [x]⁻¹[y]⁻¹, but in PE units z is a dimensionless areal density per PE². The standard deviation and probable error obey
σ = PE / Φ⁻¹(0.75) ≈ PE / 0.67449,
so one PE ≈ 0.67449 σ.

Reconstructed from context on [PAGE 222]:
(1) Exponential kernel of the surface of frequency:
z(x,y) = C exp{ − [x² − 2ρ x y + y²] / [2(1 − ρ²)] },
with C = [2π(1 − ρ²)]⁻¹/² in PE units.

(2) Contour ellipse of constant z (ellipse of equal frequency):
x² − 2ρ x y + y² = κ², κ = const. > 0.

Implicit differentiation:
(2x − 2ρ y) dx + (−2ρ x + 2y) dy = 0,
equivalently (x − ρ y) + (y − ρ x) (dy/dx) = 0.

Line OM (coefficient of dy = 0; vertical tangents, parallel to axis of y):
y − ρ x = 0 ⇒ OM: y = ρ x.
The OCR text reads “on the line y = † x” [PAGE 222]; the dagger is the obscured regression multiplier, equal to ρ under equal-PE standardization.

Line OP (tangents parallel to axis of x; coefficient of dx = 0):
x − ρ y = 0 ⇒ OP: x = ρ y,
matching “tangents to these ellipses parallel to the axis of x meet them on the line x = gy, viz., on OP” [PAGE 222], with g = ρ.

Section parallel to plane xz (fix y = y₀):
z(x | y₀) ∝ exp{ − [x − ρ y₀]² / [2(1 − ρ²)] }.
This is a normal curve in x with conditional mean E[X | Y=y₀] = ρ y₀ (on OP) and conditional variance Var(X | Y=y₀) = 1 − ρ² in PE². Conditional probable error:
PE_{x|y} = 0.67449 √(1 − ρ²).
The locus of vertices over y₀ is x = ρ y, lifted into the vertical plane z OP, as stated: “the locus of their vertices lies in the plane z OP” [PAGE 222]. Galton quotes the marginal section probable error as 1·50 in his original units [PAGE 222].

Section parallel to plane yz (fix x = x₁), origin shifted onto OM, with x = x₁ and y = y₁ + y′ per (3) [PAGE 222]:
Completing the square in the exponential gives
z(y′ | x₁) ∝ exp{ − [y′ − (conditional offset)]² / [2(1 − ρ²)] },
so E[Y | X=x₁] = ρ x₁ (on OM) and Var(Y | X=x₁) = 1 − ρ². The conditional probable error e satisfies
e = 0.67449 √(1 − ρ²),
and therefore e / PE_{x|y} = 1 when the two marginals have equal variability—i.e. “the probable error of sections parallel to the plane of yz is 1 nearly times that of those parallel to the plane of xz” [PAGE 222]. Vertices lie in plane z OM.

Breakdown / singularity regime: as ρ → ±1, (1 − ρ²) → 0, C → ∞, the density concentrates on the line y = ±x, conditional variance → 0, and the Gaussian surface and its elliptical sections degenerate; the differentiation of (2) and the notion of a smooth frequency surface break down exactly at |ρ| = 1.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage, plus multi-week traps.

Galton’s “surface of frequency” in Natural Inheritance (1889) is the direct geometric ancestor of the bivariate normal distribution. It descends from Quetelet’s “social physics” and the concept of the “average man,” but Galton replaces the static average with a relational construct: the regression line, which he originally called “reversion toward mediocrity.” On [PAGE 222] the regression appears not as an algebraic command but as a property of conjugate diameters of frequency ellipses, with P as “the mean position of p”—the conditional mean anchoring each ellipse. Fig. 11 on [PAGE 101] presumably renders these ellipses and diameters visually, making OM and OP the two conditional-mean loci.

The lineage proceeds through Pearson’s mathematical formalization of correlation (1890s), Fisher’s conditional likelihood and maximum-likelihood regression (1915–1925), and the modern Gaussian graphical model, in which x and y are nodes joined by an edge of weight ρ and the ellipses are level sets of the joint density. Crucially, Galton’s derivation is purely relational: it describes where the conditional mean lies for an ensemble, not how any particular father–son pair moves across generations.

Multi-week trap: suppose a behavior or metric is observed weekly and an extreme value occurs in week 1. Because E[Y₂ | Y₁ = y₁] = μ + ρ(y₁ − μ) with |ρ| < 1, week 2 is statistically expected to be closer to μ even with zero intervention. Observers then commit the “regression trap” by attributing the week-2 decline to a treatment, a crisis response, or a coaching adjustment. The inverse trap is equally damaging: assuming that because regression is statistical, no real change can occur, and therefore ignoring a genuine level shift in μ between weeks. Both traps treat the conjugate diameter OP as a physical rail rather than a conditional expectation. Galton’s own data span generations, not weeks, but the same conditional structure produces pseudo-trends over any finite observation window when extremes are sampled. The protective principle is to compare the observed week-2 value against the conditional mean predicted from week 1 (the Galton line), not against the raw week-1 extreme, and to require that any claimed intervention effect exceed the width of the conditional ellipse, i.e. exceed PE_cond = 0.67449 σ√(1 − ρ²), before the statistical layer is vacated.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Model two agents, A and B, each maintaining a belief distribution over the other’s crisis-response intensity b ∈ ℝ. Let the joint belief be a Gaussian “surface of frequency” over (b_A, b_B) with correlation ρ, precisely the structure Galton analyzes: each agent’s conditional mean of the other’s behavior lies on a regression line that is a conjugate diameter of their shared belief ellipses. Agent A’s crisis-habit regression hypothesis is E[b_B,t+1 | b_B,t] = μ_B + ρ(b_B,t − μ_B): after an extreme crisis action, B is expected to regress toward μ_B.

Recursive theory of mind enters when B knows that A expects this regression. B can then deploy a counter-trap: deliberately sustain an elevated crisis habit b_B,t > μ_B precisely because A will discount B’s next move as mean-reverting. A, reasoning recursively, models B as modeling A’s discounting, and may choose not to discount. The fixed point of this recursion is not the Galton regression line but a second-order equilibrium in which the counter-trap signal δ_t = b_B,t − [μ_B + ρ(b_B,t − μ_B)] is sustained only while its strategic value exceeds its maintenance cost.

The clock is the finite horizon or discount factor γ ∈ (0,1). As the deadline approaches, the present value of maintaining the counter-trap decays like γ^{T−t}, while the reversion pressure ρ pulls b_B back toward μ_B. The crisis-habit regression therefore reasserts itself near the clock’s expiry unless the counter-trap is continuously refreshed. Formally, if b_t follows the AR(1) implied by Galton’s surface, b_{t+1} = μ + ρ(b_t − μ) + ε_{t+1}, then a counter-trap adds an exogenous control u_t: b_{t+1} = μ + ρ(b_t − μ) + u_t + ε_{t+1}. The optimal u_t under a finite clock satisfies a Bellman recursion; the veto condition is that the physical/biomechanical cost of u_t (see next section) exceeds the discounted strategic benefit, at which point u_t = 0 and the trajectory collapses onto the Galton regression line OM/OP. In Galton’s terms, P—the mean position—regains control once the agent can no longer pay to hold the ellipse off-center.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof.

The statistical layer furnished by [PAGE 222] yields conditional densities, not trajectories. A veto proof requires defining a hard admissibility predicate V(x, y) ∈ {0,1} that equals 1 when (x, y) violates a non-statistical constraint, and showing that the Gaussian surface must be multiplied by (1 − V) and renormalized wherever V binds.

Physical layer: measurement noise imposes a finite resolution ε > 0, so |ρ| cannot equal 1 in any physical sensor pair; the singularity at ρ → ±1 where conditional variance → 0 is vetoed. The breakdown regime is therefore not reachable empirically; as the empirical |ρ̂| approaches 1, the Gaussian kernel’s normalization C = [2π(1 − ρ̂²)]⁻¹/² inflates and the model overfits noise, forcing a regularization (e.g. shrinkage of ρ̂ toward 0) that is a physical-layer veto on the pure Galton surface.

Biomechanical layer: Galton’s canonical domain is human stature. Height is bounded by growth-plate closure, nutrition, and skeletal allometry; no conditional mean E[son | father] may prescribe a stature outside the viable biomechanical envelope [h_min, h_max]. If the regression line x = ρ y projects a child’s height beyond h_max for an exceptionally tall father, the Gaussian vertex on OP lies outside the admissible set and must be vetoed; the true conditional density becomes truncated and skewed, and the “probable error” of the section is no longer 0.67449√(1−ρ²) but smaller near the boundary.

Weather layer: if (x, y) represent, say, sea-level pressure and temperature, the joint surface must obey thermodynamic constraints (hydrostatic balance, Clausius–Clapeyron, conservation of energy). A statistical conjugate diameter cannot dictate a temperature–pressure pair that violates the ideal-gas law or the moist-adiabatic lapse rate. Where the Gaussian vertex on z OM or z OP projects into a physically impossible air-parcel state, the weather layer vetoes that point and the density mass is redistributed to the nearest admissible manifold.

Veto proof (summary): Let Z_G(x, y) be Galton’s unrenormalized Gaussian surface from (1) [PAGE 222]. Define the admissible surface Z_A = Z_G · (1 − V) / ∬ Z_G (1 − V) dx dy. The exact singularity/breakdown regime of the pure theory—|ρ| → 1, conditional PE → 0, ellipses collapsing to lines—is simultaneously the regime where V most frequently binds, because physical, biomechanical, and weather constraints all impose finite bounds and noise floors. Hence any prediction that relies on the unvetoed Gaussian tail beyond the admissible set is falsified a priori; the regression line is a conditional expectation over the statistical ensemble, and the veto layers determine which parts of that ensemble are physically realizable.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Scenario 1 — Bounded synthetic regressor. Construct a bivariate Gaussian (X, Y) with known ρ = 0.6 in PE units, then impose a hard physical bound |X| ≤ B. Counterfactual (no veto): conditional samples Y | X=x follow N(ρx, 1 − ρ²) with PE = 0.67449√(1 − 0.6²) ≈ 0.539 PE for every x. Veto: reject or resample any proposed (x, y) with |x| > B. Verifiable observable: the empirical conditional PE of Y given X near +B. Prediction: under the veto, the conditional distribution of Y given X ≈ B is truncated in X and the regression of Y on X flattens near the boundary; the slope of E[Y | X] estimated within |x| ∈ [B − δ, B] is significantly smaller than ρ = 0.6, and the conditional PE is strictly below 0.539 PE. Verification: Monte Carlo with N ≥ 10⁵ samples; reject the no-veto null if the boundary slope differs from 0.6 by more than 3 standard errors.

Let X_t be resting heart rate and Y_{t+1} next-morning heart rate in a cohort, with an extreme crisis value x* near the individual maximum HR_max (biomechanical bound). Prediction: the veto activates; observed E[Y | X ≈ HR_max] falls below the unvetoed regression line by a gap that grows with (x* − μ_X), and the post-crisis values cluster just below HR_max rather than scattering symmetrically around the regression vertex. Prediction: for extreme x where the unvetoed Gaussian vertex predicts a temperature anomaly below the physically admissible floor, the observed conditional mode shifts to the boundary (the freezing/energy constraint) rather than to the regression line; the conditional variance collapses asymmetrically and the ellipse of best fit is truncated. Verification: compare kernel-density conditional modes against the linear Galton prediction; a systematic boundary shift exceeding the estimated PE_cond constitutes a verified veto.
