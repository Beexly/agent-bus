This page from Knight's *Risk, Uncertainty and Profit* (p. 191) exposes the foundational lie of perfect competition: that "factors" are homogenous units sliding along a smooth, diminishing marginal productivity curve. Knight argues this is a logical device, not reality. The "factor" becomes a "pulp," an abstraction that erases heterogeneity. In sports, players are never physically interchangeable; they carry unique skill vectors, injury histories, and psychological profiles. Perfect competition assumes a static society where you can reorganize production "along natural lines." Sports is a dynamic, evolutionary ecosystem where every action changes the reaction (e.g., a star player's injury shifts defensive rotations). The curve itself is hypothetical, only valid in a limited range before becoming "pure fantasy." Applying it to roster construction or player valuation ignores the non-linear, emergent properties of team sport interactions. The combination clause ("if two or more such agents combine so as to compete as a unit") introduces cartel logic, which in sports translates to superteams forming through free agency or drafting, capturing the "total product" of multiple low-end units. However, this too is flawed because combining talent doesn't simply sum outputs due to friction, locker room dynamics, and tactical mismatch. The core gray-zone here is treating discrete, heterogeneous human capital as continuous, fungible inputs in a static equilibrium model.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let $A$ be the set of $N$ agents in a competitive group. Assume the marginal productivity curve is defined by $P(n)$, the productivity of the $n$-th unit when total quantity is $n$. Under perfect competition, each agent receives payment equal to the marginal product of the last (least important) unit employed, $w = P(N)$.
If $k$ agents form a coalition, their combined output under this static model would be the area under the curve from $0$ to $k$, i.e., $\int_0^k P(n) dn$. Since $P(n)$ is decreasing, this integral exceeds $k \cdot P(N)$, the sum they would earn individually if all remained at the margin. This mathematical artifact drives the incentive to combine.
However, the breakdown regime is immediate: $P(n)$ is ill-defined for large deviations from current employment levels because it requires "reorganizing production along natural lines"—an impossibility in a fluid market. The equation fails when heterogeneity prevents ordering agents on a single scalar dimension, or when strategic interdependence makes individual marginal product path-dependent. The model assumes $P(n)$ is time-invariant and independent of coalition structure, both violations of real-world sports economics where roster composition dictates tactical systems and thus individual value.

The lineage of this critique begins with Alfred Marshall's partial equilibrium supply and demand curves, which Knight inherits but radicalizes. By 1921, the neoclassical synthesis was beginning to treat factors as continuously divisible and perfectly mobile. Knight's intervention—emphasizing the hypothetical nature of the productivity curve—foreshadows Austrian and institutional critiques of equilibrium models. In sports analytics, this genealogical trap manifests in early Moneyball-style thinking: treating batting average, home runs, and stolen bases as commensurable, linear inputs into a win-expectancy function. The multi-week trap is assuming that past performance on the "curve" predicts future value independent of context. When teams overpay for established stars (the high-productivity end), they ignore Knight's warning that the curve is constructed from counterfactuals ("what would be... if...") and cannot capture the true value of adaptability and uncertainty navigation. The historical pattern is clear: every period of "perfect competition" illusion in sports ends with a collapse—salary cap rebellions, luxury taxes, or dynastic lockups—that forces a reset toward recognizing fundamental heterogeneity.
