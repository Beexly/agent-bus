<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

Hull’s own earlier postulates regarding habit strength (\( sEr \)) and drive reduction (\( Dr \)). The specific problem of stimulus patterning was a major battleground in the 1940s between methodological behaviorists and Gestalt psychologists. While Gestalt theorists argued for holistic perception fields, Hull attempted to derive pattern discrimination strictly from additive elemental processes plus interference terms.

However, a "multi-week trap" exists within this retrocausal narrative. Later research by Spence (1937) and Krechevsky & Harlow (1943) demonstrated that Hull's original equations underestimated the speed of discrimination learning under low-intensity stimulation. This led to the introduction of "Spence-Krechevsky modification," which posited that small stimulus differences lead to steeper generalization gradients than large ones—a nonlinearity not present in the 1943 text. Furthermore, the rise of Rescorla-Wagner (1972) associative models showed that attention is allocated dynamically based on prediction error, fundamentally altering how \( sIr \) is acquired. In the Hullian framework presented here, inhibition is treated as a passive, subtractive counterpart to excitation. Yet, modern neuroscience reveals that inhibitory interneurons (e.g., PV cells) can actively sculpt temporal windows for integration, meaning the "generalization gradient" is not a fixed property of the sensory input but a plastic, experience-dependent filter. Thus, viewing Hull's algebra through a modern lens exposes a trap: assuming static sensory encoding ignores the rettroactive influence of cognitive expectations on early sensory processing. The "afferent interaction effects" mentioned in the text are likely downstream consequences of top-down predictive coding mechanisms that Hull could not mathematically formalize.
