The source text (Knight, 1921, p. 136) delineates a fundamental dissonance between "time value" as a psychological utility and "interest proper" as an emergent institutional response to uncertainty. To construct a rigorous, non-toy formalism for this duality, we must derive the precise conditions under which time preference alone yields zero distributive interest, and where uncertainty injects a positive risk premium into capital allocation.

First, we define Time Value (TV) as a subjective utility differential between present and future consumption. In a frictionless world of complete knowledge, TV is modeled by a discount factor β (0 < β ≤ 1), reflecting pure impatience. However, Knight argues that even with positive TV, if foreknowledge is complete (i.e., no uncertainty about future states or returns), the equilibrium rate of interest on productive investment collapses to zero. This is because any attempt to charge a positive rate would be arbitraged away: agents can perfectly forecast production schedules and adjust their intertemporal consumption without requiring a scarcity premium for waiting. The only payment extracted in such a society would be for the "psychological" discomfort of delayed gratification, not a return on capital risk.

Second, we introduce Uncertainty (U). Knight distinguishes risk (measurable probability) from uncertainty (unmeasurable, unique events). In his framework, profit arises solely from bearing true uncertainty. When future states are unknown and uninsurable, entrepreneurs demand compensation for potential losses—a risk premium π. This premium decouples the observed market interest rate r from the pure time-preference rate ρ. We formalize this via a modification of the Fisher equation: r = ρ + π(U). Here, π(U) is a non-linear functional of the ambiguity in the production function's outcomes. As U → 0 (complete knowledge), π → 0, recovering Knight’s assertion that interest proper vanishes.

Third, we map this to a modern Real Options perspective. The decision to invest in a durable asset (like pre-industrial land or slaves) can be viewed as purchasing a call option on future cash flows. Under certainty, the option’s value is simply the discounted net present value (NPV). Under uncertainty, the option has convexity: the holder benefits from upside surprises while limiting downside exposure by delaying investment. This real-option value (ROV) inflates the required return, creating a wedge between the cost of capital and the safe rate. Thus, "interest proper" becomes a shadow price for volatility tolerance.

We then audit the gray zones. Knight’s dismissal of "canoes and fish nets" as origins of interest challenges biological/evolutionary economics models (e.g., Boyd & Richerson’s gene-culture coevolution), which posit that reciprocal lending over durable goods predates monetary markets. Our formalism suggests these early exchanges were primarily insurance mechanisms against idiosyncratic shocks (high U), not pure time-value trades. Furthermore, the historical note on usury highlights a market failure: without competitive consumption loans, the risk premium was extracted as rent, leading to exploitation. This aligns with Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) critiques of private debt cycles—when uncertainty spikes, central banks must act as lenders of last resort to prevent systemic default cascades. Finally, we consider climate change as an exogenous increase in macro-uncertainty (κ↑). This should empirically manifest as a structural rise in the term premium across sovereign bonds, validating our cross-layer model where physical-system instability feeds back into financial pricing kernels.

In summary, the derivation hinges on separating three layers: (1) Psychological Time Preference (β), (2) Risk/Uncertainty Premium (π), and (3) Institutional Frictions (market power, legal constraints). <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage traced through this passage spans four centuries. The immediate ancestor is the Scholastic doctrine of usury, which condemned fixed-interest loans as exploitative precisely because they monetized time itself rather than shared productive risk. Knight notes that pre-industrial societies lacked "productive agencies" separable from persons; thus, loans were typically consumption-driven, arising from famine or crisis. The condemnation aligned with the "phenomenon just described"—the extraction of surplus based on temporal neediness, not capital productivity. 

This connects directly to John Locke’s labor theory of property and David Hume’s conventionalist view of money. Hume argued that interest stems from population growth and capital accumulation demands, implicitly assuming some frictions. Knight rejects this by positing that even with growth, if knowledge were perfect, interest would still vanish. His radical break comes from Frank Knight’s own teacher, John R. Commons, who emphasized legal-institutional frameworks shaping economic behavior—hence Knight’s focus on the absence of competitive markets for consumption loans as the root of usurious "extortion."

Forward-looking descendants include Kenneth Arrow’s work on incomplete markets and John Maynard Keynes’ beauty contest analogy, both stressing animal spirits driven by fundamental uncertainty. Most critically, this genealogy informs Modern Monetary Theory (MMT): if interest is an uncertainty premium, then sovereign currency issuers (who face no solvency constraint) set rates to manage aggregate uncertainty perception, not to ration scarce savings. Thus, quantitative easing post-2008 can be read as suppressing the perceived risk premium $\pi(\mathcal{U})$ after a systemic shock.
