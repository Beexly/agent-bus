This anticipates Hull’s later concept of reactive inhibition (I_r), the opponent-process formalism of Solomon and Corbit, and modern stochastic-resonance accounts of perception-action coupling. The “normal law of chance” phrase places the model squarely in the early-to-mid 20th-century psychometric tradition of treating individual differences and moment-to-moment performance as normally distributed error around a true score.

The critical multi-week trap is stationarity. Chronic training, fatigue accumulation, circadian entrainment, and pharmacological tolerance will drift σ upward or downward. If σ drifts such that 6σ routinely exceeds the available sER of weaker habits, those habits are chronically suppressed below their baseline expression, producing a false appearance of habit extinction when in fact the inhibitory bound has swallowed the reaction potential. A second trap is common-mode induction: over days, disparate habits (e.g., lever pressing and runway running) come to share motivational state (hunger, thirst, arousal), inducing positive covariance in sOR that violates the explicit uncorrelated clause [PAGE 326]. Once correlated, the summed inhibitory pressure across the habit hierarchy can exceed what independent marginals predict, causing cascade suppression of behavior that the postulate cannot anticipate. A third trap is response generalization crystallization: Major Corollary predicts bidirectional generalization around the reinforced intensity [PAGE 326], but over weeks of differential reinforcement, the oscillation may selectively consolidate one flank of the gradient, converting stochastic generalization into a directional bias that the original postulate does not encode.
