If locomotion is gated by M_c, then experience before maturation is futile and experience after maturation may still be constrained by closing sensitive periods (myelination, ossification, sensory-map crystallization). For the human child, the clock runs in weeks and months: the stepping reflex appears at birth and disappears around two months; independent walking emerges near a year. A recursive analyst must therefore ask not only "is the capacity innate?" but "at what point on the clock does experience become effective, and after what point does the window narrow?" James's cut-off sentence precisely locates this temporal decision point: the prediction about the human child is a prediction about timing, not merely about origin.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer. Flight requires lift and thrust sufficient for body weight; wing loading, aspect ratio, and air density set the feasible envelope. Reynolds number \(Re = \rho v L / \nu\) determines whether the bird operates in a viscous- or inertial-dominated regime; small nestlings have low Re and different flight mechanics than adults. Spalding's birds were fully fledged at release, so their wing morphology was approximately adult-like, but the confinement period could still have altered feather microstructure or muscle fiber-type proportions through use-dependent mechanisms James did not measure. Veto status: physical capacity is necessary but not sufficient to prove the absence of learning.

Biomechanical layer. Human and avian locomotion both depend on central pattern generators in the spinal cord, proprioceptive feedback, vestibular integration, and descending cortical modulation. Maturation of these substrates—myelination, synapse pruning, ossification timing—follows a genetically constrained schedule that experience cannot accelerate beyond physiological limits. A veto experiment would immobilize an infant's limbs during the normal stepping-reflex window; if the reflex still emerges and disappears on schedule despite zero locomotor experience, the maturation-only model is supported. The James text offers no such human biomechanical data, only avian anecdote [PAGE 406].

Weather/environmental layer. Spalding's release day conditions are unrecorded [PAGE 406]. Calm air reduces the demand on flight stability; gusty or turbulent conditions would expose coordination deficits that calm weather masks. Muscle contractile kinetics are temperature-dependent (\(Q_{10} \approx 2\)–3), so a cool release day could depress performance independent of learning. This is a genuine veto on generalizing from a single release observation to a species-level claim.

Adjudication. The James–Spalding argument survives the physical and biomechanical vetoes at the level of gross coordination: precocial birds can produce coordinated flight without practice, consistent with prepackaged central-pattern-generator dynamics [PAGE 406]. It fails the weather and sample-size vetoes: n = 4 survivors, one mortality, unrecorded release conditions, and no systematic variation of wind or temperature mean the claim "birds do not learn to fly" is supported for obstacle avoidance in calm conditions but not established as a universal law across all flight parameters and weather regimes. The human extrapolation remains unvetoed but unproven by this page alone.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios. Each specifies an intervention, the prediction under the strong James–Spalding nativist claim, and the falsifying outcome that would veto it.

Veto Scenario 1 — Altricial confinement. Intervention: confine nestling pigeons (an altricial species) in boxes that prevent wing extension until the age at which controls fledge, then release and quantify flight-stability and obstacle-avoidance collision rates against age-matched unconfined siblings. Prediction under strong nativism: confined pigeons fly normally because flight is innate and maturation-gated, not learned. Verifiable veto: if confined pigeons show collision rates more than double controls (p < 0.05, two-sample test) or fail to achieve stable flight within the normal fledging window, experience is necessary and the universal "birds do not learn to fly" claim is vetoed for altricial species.

Veto Scenario 2 — Human neonatal immobilization. Intervention: for infants requiring medically indicated limb immobilization during the first six postnatal weeks, record the age of stepping-reflex disappearance and onset of independent walking, compared with a matched population without immobilization. Prediction under maturation-only (the human extension James invites [PAGE 406]): walking onset and reflex timing follow the standard normative schedule despite near-zero locomotor experience. Verifiable veto: if walking onset is delayed beyond the 95th percentile of the normative population and the delay correlates with immobilization duration, maturation is insufficient and experience contributes causally; the direct bird-to-human prediction is vetoed.

Veto Scenario 3 — Dark-reared sensory topography. Intervention: rear precocial chicks in darkness until after motor maturity, then expose them to light and test performance on the visual cliff and visually guided pecking. Prediction under James's claim that interpreting the topographical significance of sights is "all but fully developed at birth" [PAGE 406]: dark-reared chicks show normal depth perception and avoid the deep side immediately. Verifiable veto: if dark-reared chicks fail to avoid the visual cliff or show significantly delayed visually guided pecking despite normal motor development, then topographical visual interpretation requires patterned visual experience; James's nativist claim about perception is partially vetoed. All three scenarios use standard, replicable behavioral paradigms with binary pass/fail or continuous collision/depth-preference measures, enabling RLVR-style verification against the counterfactual outcome.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
