GSE/GSI adjudication of Lu et al. The first multi-hop step is definitional. The condition is not “attachment geometry alone”; the paper says: “Because relative intensity was standardized to attachment-specific 1RM, absolute loads differed among conditions” [PAGE 1]. The experimental load is therefore \(L_{i,a}=0.70\times 1RM_{i,a}\), measured in kg, and the condition bundle contains both handle geometry and attachment-specific absolute load. That single design fact blocks any clean causal attribution of between-condition sEMG contrasts to geometry.

The second step is signal construction. Raw sEMG is band-pass filtered 20–400 Hz, rectified, and converted to RMS in mV; RMS is then normalized to the peak 1-second MVIC value and expressed as %MVIC [PAGE 4–5]. The numerator is an electrical amplitude at an electrode site; it is not whole-muscle force. The paper explicitly warns: “Surface electromyography represents electrical signals recorded at standardized recording sites, not whole-muscle force” [PAGE 9]. Thus any inference from %MVIC to “muscle contribution,” “hypertrophy potential,” or “force” is a layer violation, not a stronger interpretation.

The third step is temporal. The paper anchors each repetition using a 20% right-latissimus-dorsi RMS threshold, then quantifies sEMG over nominal 2-second lifting and 2-second lowering windows. But the motion-capture and sEMG systems did not share a common time base: “Because the motion-capture and sEMG systems did not share a common time base, kinematic events were not used to assign individual sEMG samples to mechanical phases” [PAGE 5]. This prevents the middle-trapezius lowering-versus-lifting contrast from being read as proof of a mechanically defined lowering phase.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is a sequence of progressively finer constraints on lat-pulldown sEMG inference. Earlier work compared hand positions, grip width, forearm orientation, front-versus-back execution, and exercise variation [PAGE 1, PAGE 10]. Signorile et al. (2002), Lehman et al. (2004), Lusk et al. (2010), Andersen et al. (2014), Padovan et al. (2024), and Tanimoto et al. (2025) form the relevant ancestry: they established that pull-down variations can change local sEMG amplitude, but often with whole-repetition or between-exercise summaries. The 2026 study retroactively reframes that ancestry by asking whether attachment effects are interval-specific. Its introduction says: “Whole-repetition averages may obscure whether a between-condition difference occurs mainly during the lifting or controlled lowering portion of the repetition” [PAGE 1]. That is the genealogical hinge: the current study is not merely another attachment comparison; it is a phase-decomposition study with an explicit anti-overclaiming architecture.

The historical trap is that earlier findings can be overread backward. If prior work reports grip or handle differences, a coach may infer that a particular attachment “targets” a particular muscle. The present data weaken that inference for latissimus dorsi: no consistent between-condition difference was found, and the interaction attenuated in complete-case analysis [PAGE 7–8, PAGE 13]. The most visually salient reversal is that the wide V-shaped handle, often chosen as a “back” variation, produced the clearest biceps brachii elevation: “a wide V-shaped handle is often chosen as a back-training variation, yet one of the clearest sEMG patterns in the present study was greater elbow-flexor amplitude” [PAGE 8]. That is a retrocausal correction: the old coaching label is not supported by the new local-amplitude pattern.

Multi-week traps are substantial. First is the acute-to-chronic trap: the findings are acute sEMG responses and “should not be interpreted as direct evidence of long-term hypertrophy, strength, or sport-performance adaptations” [PAGE 9]. A four-week block cannot be inferred from one laboratory session. Second is the relative-load drift trap: because each condition uses 70% of its own 1RM, absolute loads differ; over weeks, if 1RM changes are not retested, the intended 70% relative intensity drifts differently by attachment. Third is the electrode-relocation trap: the same assessor used anatomical landmarks, but “exact between-session replication cannot be guaranteed because electrodes were removed after each session” [PAGE 4]. Fourth is the cadence-label trap: the 2-second/2-second metronome creates an illusion of mechanical precision, but the paper explicitly denies hardware synchronization [PAGE 5]. Fifth is the single-side trap: right-side recordings mean bilateral differences “could not be evaluated” [PAGE 9]. Sixth is the complete-case trap: complete-case models included only 6–8 participants for some muscles, and triceps brachii changed pattern [PAGE 13]. Any multi-week claim must therefore carry these six caveats, not just the primary p-values.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The practitioner’s crisis habit regression is predictable. Under time pressure, a coach defaults to the old cue “wide V for back” or “rope for rear delts.” The first cue is contradicted by the LD null and by the biceps brachii finding; the second is only partially supported because the significant posterior deltoid contrast is rope versus cambered bar, not rope versus every attachment [PAGE 7]. The paper’s own warning is direct: the wide V-shaped handle condition “produced the greatest biceps brachii sEMG amplitude, primarily in the lifting analytical interval” [PAGE 8]. A coach who regresses to “wide V is a back attachment” is not summarizing the source; they are overriding it.

The counter-trap is overcorrection. An athlete feels the rope more in the posterior shoulder and concludes “rope is best for posterior deltoid.” The data support a narrower claim: rope exceeded cambered bar by 8.0 %MVIC, 95% CI 4.1 to 11.9, p = 0.002 [PAGE 7], and the boundary-free estimate remained 7.15 %MVIC [3.15, 11.15], p = 0.003 [PAGE 16]. But the rope also had the lowest mean 1RM, 64.4 ± 7.1 kg [PAGE 2], and load adjustment raised the PD contrast to 13.27 %MVIC, indicating that absolute load is entangled with the condition [PAGE 16]. The counter-trap is to turn a two-condition contrast into a universal ranking.

The clock trap is different: because a metronome prescribed nominal 2-second lifting and 2-second lowering, the coach may mentally treat those windows as mechanical phases. The paper forecloses that: the systems “did not share a common time base,” and the windows were “cadence-defined analytical windows … rather than mechanically synchronized phase boundaries” [PAGE 5]. The middle trapezius lowering-minus-lifting effect, 6.2 %MVIC [3.3, 9.1], p < 0.001 [PAGE 7], is robust when the phase distinction is preserved, but it is not estimable in the boundary-free model [PAGE 16]. It also cannot be attributed precisely to the mechanical lowering phase because the systems were not temporally synchronized [PAGE 8–9]. A recursive theory-of-mind audit must also account for expectancy: the attachment was visually apparent, and “assessors were not blinded to attachment condition during technique screening” [PAGE 4]. Participants could not be blinded to a handle they were holding. The correct mental model is therefore: the athlete’s sensation, the coach’s cue, and the assessor’s screening are all condition-aware; the sEMG contrast is not a blind causal trial. The practical decision rule is to cue attachments by the narrow verified contrast, not by the broad muscle label: WVS for elbow-flexor amplitude in the lifting window; rope versus cambered bar for posterior deltoid; no attachment preference for LD; MT interval difference only with the cadence caveat.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto. To claim a pure attachment-geometry effect, the design must either fix absolute load across attachments or randomize geometry independently of load. It did neither: load was \(0.70\times 1RM_{i,a}\), and mean 1RMs differed from 64.4 to 76.4 kg [PAGE 2–3]. The paper states: “The results therefore describe differences among attachment-specific relative-load conditions and should not be attributed solely to attachment geometry” [PAGE 1]. Therefore any claim of the form “the handle alone caused the sEMG difference” is vetoed. The load-adjusted models preserve direction for the principal PD, BB, and MT contrasts, but the paper correctly says this “cannot isolate a pure attachment-geometry effect” [PAGE 9]. Physical-load proof is present, but it proves confounding, not isolation.

Biomechanical-layer veto. To claim a mechanically defined phase effect, the sEMG and kinematics need a common clock. The paper states: “Because the motion-capture and sEMG systems did not share a common time base, kinematic events were not used to assign individual sEMG samples to mechanical phases” [PAGE 5]. Therefore any claim that the MT difference is specifically the “lowering phase” in a mechanical sense is vetoed. A weaker claim—cadence-defined lowering window versus cadence-defined lifting window—is permitted. Likewise, to claim bilateral equivalence, both sides must be recorded; the paper recorded the right side only, and “potential bilateral differences in sEMG amplitude could not be evaluated” [PAGE 9]. The biomechanical mechanism of wrist alignment, distal freedom, and force transmission is plausible but not isolated; the paper itself says the rope mechanism “cannot be assigned to the rope geometry alone because absolute loads also differed among conditions” [PAGE 8].

Weather-layer veto. No weather, temperature, humidity, barometric, or outdoor-exposure variable appears in pages 1–16. The study is a laboratory crossover with standardized sessions. There is no weather-conditioned randomization and no environmental covariate. Therefore any claim that weather moderated the attachment or phase effect is non-identifiable and vetoed. This is a strict absence-of-evidence veto, not a negative result: the source cannot test weather, so it cannot support a weather claim.

Additional cross-layer vetoes. The LD targeting claim is vetoed: primary attachment p = 0.063, complete-case p = 0.277, boundary-free p = 0.194 [PAGE 13, PAGE 16]. The long-term hypertrophy claim is vetoed by the acute-sEMG design: “The findings describe acute sEMG responses and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of long-term hypertrophy, strength, or sport-performance adaptations” [PAGE 9]. The triceps “stable condition effect” claim is vetoed by the complete-case shift from p = 0.025 to an interaction p = 0.018 [PAGE 13]. The “whole-muscle force” claim is vetoed because sEMG is not force [PAGE 9]. The permitted evidentiary statement is narrow: in resistance-trained men, at 70% of attachment-specific 1RM, PD rope exceeded CB; BB WVS exceeded RP in the lifting interval robustly and exceeded SB/CB in the primary/early-window analysis; MT cadence-defined lowering exceeded lifting when the phase term was retained; LD showed no consistent attachment difference.
