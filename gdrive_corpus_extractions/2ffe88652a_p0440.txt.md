While Tolman’s text [PAGE 1] does not present formal equations, his arguments implicitly rely on principles from probability theory and statistics. We can formalize the core logic regarding the number of capacity factors and the resulting distribution of traits.

Let $T$ be the measured value of a cognitive trait (e.g., maze-running proficiency) for an individual. Let this trait be determined by $N$ independent component factors (from heredity and training), denoted $X_1, X_2, ..., X_N$. Each $X_i$ represents a "relatively minor unit" with mean $\mu_i$ and variance $\sigma_i^2$. We assume these units act additively and independently.

**Formal Statement:**
The total trait value is the sum of these components:
$$ T = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i X_i + \epsilon $$
where $w_i$ are weighting coefficients representing the contribution of each unit, and $\epsilon$ is residual error/environmental noise.

**Assumptions:**
1.  **Independence:** The random variables $X_i$ are statistically independent. This is a strong assumption that may not hold perfectly due to genetic linkage or correlated life experiences, but Tolman invokes it to explain distributional smoothness.
2.  **Finite Variance:** Each $X_i$ has a finite variance $\sigma_i^2 < \infty$.
3.  **Large N:** The number of contributing units $N$ is large. Tolman argues against a "small number" of factors, suggesting $N \gg 1$.

**Derivation / Law:**
According to the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), as $N \to \infty$, the distribution of the standardized sum $Z = \frac{T - E[T]}{\sqrt{Var(T)}}$ converges to a standard normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$, regardless of the underlying distributions of the individual $X_i$ (provided they meet the assumptions).

**Units:**
*   $T$: Measured trait value (e.g., time to solve maze in seconds, or an arbitrary psychometric score).
*   $X_i$: Contribution of the $i$-th minor unit (dimensionless or same units as $T$ if $w_i=1$).
*   $w_i$: Weighting factor (units ensure dimensional consistency).
*   $\epsilon$: Residual error (same units as $T$).

**Exact Singularity/Breakdown Regimes:**
1.  **Small N Regime:** If $N$ is small (e.g., $N=2$ or $3$), the CLT does not apply. The distribution of $T$ will reflect the shapes of the individual $X_i$ distributions. If some $X_i$ are heavy-tailed or skewed, $T$ will not be smooth/normal. This regime corresponds to Spearman's or early Tolman's "small number of independent affairs," which Tolman deems "highly improbable" because it fails to predict the observed smooth curves.
2.  **Dependence Regime:** If the $X_i$ are strongly correlated (e.g., due to pleiotropy in genetics or shared environmental exposures), the effective $N$ is reduced. The convergence to normality slows down, and deviations from smoothness may appear. Tolman’s argument implicitly assumes weak dependence among the "minor units."
3.  **Infinite Variance Regime:** If any $X_i$ has infinite variance (e.g., following a Cauchy distribution), the CLT breaks down entirely. The sum $T$ may converge to a stable distribution with heavy tails, not a normal curve. This would contradict the "smooth distribution curves" Tolman observes. Thus, the minor units must themselves be well-behaved with finite variance.

**Citation:**
This formalization is based on Tolman’s statement on [PAGE 1]: *"Only, if in the traits, as we actually measure them, there were a great number of mutually independent and relatively minor units acting jointly, should we expect to get the smooth distribution curves for such traits that we do."* This quote directly motivates the application of the Central Limit Theorem to reject low-dimensional factor models.
