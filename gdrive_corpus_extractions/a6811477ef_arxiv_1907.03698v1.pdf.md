This source is a 2019 computer-vision paper about frame-level ball localization, not a live physical measurement system. The audit chain must therefore move from image evidence to heatmap evidence to geometric evidence to evaluation evidence, and only then to any claim about ball state or player performance. The paper states the pipeline’s scale and scope early: “TrackNet takes images with the size of 640 × 360 to generate a detection heatmap from either a single frame or several consecutive frames to position the ball” [PAGE 1]. That sentence fixes both the spatial unit and the unit of inference: the network outputs a position in a resized image coordinate system, not a court-calibrated 3D trajectory. A multi-hop derivation of reliability must therefore ask whether each transformation preserves the ball-center hypothesis.

First hop: the source assumes “there is at most one ball on the court” [PAGE 2]. This is not a minor implementation detail; it is the invariant that permits the final geometric selector. The network produces a continuous heatmap, which is thresholded and then passed to a circle finder: “If exactly one circle is identiﬁed, the centroid of the circle is returned. In other cases, the heatmap is considered no ball detected” [PAGE 8]. Thus the final decision is not a probabilistic maximum over ball positions alone. It is a conjunction of heatmap peak, fixed threshold, Hough circle detection, and exact-one cardinality. If a second circle-like blob appears, the system rejects the frame rather than choosing one. That creates a specific breakdown regime under court markings, player equipment, logos, or afterimage fragments: more evidence can reduce detection by violating exact-one cardinality.

Second hop: the heatmap target is generated from a Gaussian around the labeled ball center. The paper states “σ2 = 10 is used in our implementation since the average ball radius is about 5 pixels, roughly corresponding to the region of G (x, y) ≥128” [PAGE 7]. But the experiments later state “The diameter of tennis images in the video ranges from 2 to 12 pixels and the mean diameter is around 5 pixels” [PAGE 9]. These scale statements are not mutually consistent: a mean radius of five pixels implies a mean diameter near ten pixels, while a mean diameter of five pixels implies a mean radius near 2.5 pixels. Under the printed Gaussian expression, the threshold-128 region has radius \(r_t=\sigma\sqrt{2\ln(255/128)}\approx 3.71\) pixels when \(\sigma^2=10\). If the true mean diameter is five pixels, the threshold boundary lies outside the average ball; if the true mean radius is five pixels, the threshold boundary lies inside it. This is a genuine gray zone in the operator stack: the fixed threshold and the fixed variance encode a scale prior that the text describes inconsistently. A rigorous audit should treat the Gaussian width and the 128 threshold as calibrated to an approximate, not exact, ball scale.

Third hop: temporal evidence. TrackNet with three consecutive frames is designed to learn trajectory patterns and can sometimes recover occluded balls. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines a heatmap-based localization operator rather than a direct coordinate regressor. The input scope is stated explicitly: “TrackNet takes images with the size of 640 × 360 to generate a detection heatmap from either a single frame or several consecutive frames to position the ball” [PAGE 1]. The single-ball assumption is also explicit: “our calculation and evaluation are based on the assumption that there is at most one ball on the court” [PAGE 2].

**1. Convolution output operator.** For an input data array of width \(W\), height \(H\), depth \(D\), kernel \(w \times h \times D\), padding \(p\), and stride \(s\), the paper gives
\[
W'=\frac{W+2p-w}{s}+1,\qquad
H'=\frac{H+2p-h}{s}+1.
\]
Units: \(W,H,w,h,p\) are pixels; \(s\) is a dimensionless stride. Assumption: \(s>0\) and the implementation floors non-integer outputs, although the printed equation does not show a floor operator. Breakdown regimes: \(s=0\) is singular; \(W+2p-w<0\) or \(H+2p-h<0\) yields no valid output; non-integer results require implementation-specific rounding. This operator is used in the first 13 layers, which “refer to the design of the first 13 layers of VGG-16” [PAGE 7].

**2. Gaussian heatmap operator.** The paper states that the ground truth heatmap is “an ampliﬁed 2D Gaussian distribution located at the center of the tennis ball” [PAGE 6–7] and gives
\[
G(x,y)=
\left[
\left(
\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}
e^{-\frac{(x-x_0)^2+(y-y_0)^2}{2\sigma^2}}
\right)
\left(2\pi\sigma^2\cdot 255\right)
\right].
\]
Units: \(x,y,x_0,y_0\) are pixel coordinates; \(\sigma^2\) is in pixels squared; \(G\) is an 8-bit grayscale intensity in digital numbers, nominally \(0\) to \(255\). Assumption: the Gaussian is centered on the labeled ball center and the second factor maps the distribution to the grayscale range. The paper says “σ2 = 10 is used in our implementation since the average ball radius is about 5 pixels, roughly corresponding to the region of G (x, y) ≥128” [PAGE 7]. The printed expression simplifies, up to bracket interpretation, to
\[
G(x,y)=255\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-x_0)^2+(y-y_0)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
\]
For threshold \(t\) with \(0<t<255\), the thresholded region has radius
\[
r_t=\sigma\sqrt{2\ln(255/t)}.
\]
With \(\sigma^2=10\) and \(t=128\), \(r_t\approx 3.71\) pixels. Breakdown regimes: \(\sigma=0\) makes the Gaussian singular; \(\sigma\to\infty\) makes the heatmap uniform; \(t\le 0\) thresholds every pixel; \(t>255\) thresholds none; and a fixed \(t=128\) is mismatched when ball diameter varies from 2 to 12 pixels [PAGE 9]. There is also an internal scale inconsistency: [PAGE 7] says “average ball radius is about 5 pixels,” while [PAGE 9] says “mean diameter is around 5 pixels.” An audit must not treat those as the same measurement.

**3. Softmax and argmax operators.** The network outputs continuous values \(L(i,j,k)\) over 256 grayscale bins. The source defines
\[
P(i,j,k)=\frac{e^{L(i,j,k)}}{\sum_{l=0}^{255}e^{L(i,j,l)}}.
\]
Units: \(P(i,j,k)\) is a dimensionless probability in \([0,1]\); \(L\) is an unnormalized logit. Then
\[
h(i,j)=\arg\max_k P(i,j,k)
\]
returns a grayscale index \(k\in\{0,\ldots,255\}\). Breakdown regimes: large logits can overflow, very negative logits can underflow, all-equal logits produce a uniform distribution with arbitrary tie-breaking, and argmax ties are not resolved by the equation itself. This operator quantizes the continuous heatmap into 256 levels before thresholding.

**4. Threshold and Hough operator.** The paper says the first step is “to pixel-wisely convert the heatmap into a black-white binary heatmap by the threshold t” and that “threshold t is set as 128” [PAGE 8]. The second step uses “the Hough Gradient Method [25] to ﬁnd the circle on the black-white binary detection heatmap” [PAGE 8]. The decision rule is exact:
\[
\text{detection}=
\begin{cases}
\text{centroid of circle}, & N_{\text{circles}}=1,\\
\text{no ball detected}, & N_{\text{circles}}=0\ \text{or}\ N_{\text{circles}}>1.
\end{cases}
\]
This is directly quoted: “If exactly one circle is identiﬁed, the centroid of the circle is returned. In other cases, the heatmap is considered no ball detected” [PAGE 8]. Breakdown regimes: non-circular objects, merged blobs, court markings, player equipment, afterimage streaks, and occlusions can make \(N_{\text{circles}}\ne 1\). For badminton, the geometric prior is especially weak because “Unlike tennis, badminton is not spherical, resulting in a larger size variation” [PAGE 10].

**5. Cross-entropy operator.** The target \(Q(i,j,k)\) is one-hot:
\[
Q(i,j,k)=
\begin{cases}
1, & G(i,j)=k,\\
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]
and the loss is
\[
H_Q(P)=-\sum_{i,j,k}Q(i,j,k)\log P(i,j,k).
\]
Units: if \(\log\) is natural, \(H_Q\) is in nats per pixel; if base-2, it is in bits per pixel. The source does not state the base, so the unit is convention-dependent. Assumption: \(G(i,j)\) is quantized to an integer \(k\); the paper does not specify the rounding operator, which is a gray zone because the Gaussian is continuous while \(Q\) is discrete.

**6. Evaluation operators.** The paper defines
\[
\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad
\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{TP}{VC_1+VC_2+VC_3},\qquad
F_1=\frac{2PR}{P+R}.
\]
Units: all are dimensionless proportions in \([0,1]\). The visibility classes are defined as “V C = 1 implies the ball can be easily identiﬁed. V C = 2 implies the ball is in the frame but can not be easily identiﬁed” [PAGE 4], and “V C = 3 implies the ball is occluded by other objects” [PAGE 4–5]. Position error is the Euclidean distance between prediction and ground truth; the paper sets the tennis specification at 5 pixels and the badminton specification at 7.5 pixels [PAGE 9–10]. A true positive requires detection within that radius. Breakdown regimes: changing the PE radius changes precision and recall; the fixed radius is not a physical error bound; and the denominator \(VC_1+VC_2+VC_3\) excludes \(VC_0\) frames, so the metric is conditional on the screened clip distribution.

The source’s own headline and cross-validated results must be kept separate. Same-test tennis is “99.7%, 97.3%, and 98.5%” [PAGE 1], while the enriched 10-fold result is “95.3%, 75.7%, and 84.3%” [PAGE 1]. Badminton transfer is “75.8%, 22.9%, and 35.2%” [PAGE 11], and badminton-trained performance is “85.0%, 57.7%, and 68.7%” [PAGE 10]. These are different operators under different evidence assumptions; they should not be pooled into one reliability claim.

In this system there are at least four minds: the player, the broadcast camera/director, the tracking model, and the analyst or coach. The player’s intent can be deceptive; a drop shot and a smash can begin with similar body language. The camera/director can zoom, pan, cut, or replay, changing the apparent trajectory. The model has a learned prior over trajectories. The analyst has a model of the model, and that recursive trust changes how the output is used. Crisis habit regression occurs when the ball is invisible or ambiguous: the model falls back on the trajectory prior learned from consecutive frames, and the human falls back on the last plausible heatmap. Counter-trap occurs when the player’s action or the broadcast cut violates that prior; the model may then produce a confident but wrong center, especially if a single circle-like blob survives thresholding. The clock is the 30 fps frame interval. At the paper’s recorded speeds—417 km/h for badminton and 253 km/h for tennis [PAGE 11]—the straight-line scene displacement per frame is about 3.86 m and 2.34 m, respectively, before considering camera projection. The “crisis” therefore unfolds in a handful of frames, not in human reaction time.

The correct recursive adjudication is to separate three questions. First, is the ball visible in the current frame? The visibility classes say \(VC=2\) means the ball “can not be easily identiﬁed” and \(VC=3\) means it is “occluded by other objects” [PAGE 4–5]. Second, is the heatmap peak supported by neighboring frames or only by a prior? The paper’s own occlusion sample is only “2 out of 7” [PAGE 10], so the prior is suggestive, not authoritative. Third, does the human analyst have independent biomechanical evidence—racquet face, player stride, contact sound, or a second camera? If not, the analyst should not convert a heatmap centroid into a contact-time or intent claim. Counter-trap resistance means treating a confident heatmap as one witness, not as the player’s intent. The clock rule is equally strict: if the ball’s projected displacement between frames exceeds the ball diameter or the PE tolerance, the frame is in a motion-blur regime, and the output should be marked low-confidence or rejected rather than smoothed into a false continuity. The model’s trajectory habit is useful for continuity, but it must not be allowed to overwrite the visibility state.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer vetoes any claim that pixel position is physical ball state. The paper reports pixel coordinates and a Euclidean positioning error with a 5-pixel tennis tolerance and 7.5-pixel badminton tolerance [PAGE 9–10]. It does not report camera intrinsics, court homography, lens distortion, or 3D reconstruction. Therefore a heatmap centroid cannot be converted into metres, metres per second, or contact force without an external calibration model. The physical speed values—253 km/h for tennis and 417 km/h for badminton [PAGE 11]—are context for the difficulty of tracking, not measurements produced by TrackNet. Veto proof: if a proposed conclusion requires a physical trajectory, speed, or bounce point, the source lacks the calibration layer and the conclusion is unsupported.

The biomechanical layer vetoes contact-time and occlusion claims unless they are independently grounded. The paper labels “hit” and “bouncing” trajectory patterns [PAGE 5], but the network output is a heatmap, not a racquet-ball contact detector. When the ball is occluded by the player, the label itself may be estimated from neighboring frames: the paper says the ball position can be estimated from neighboring frames for occlusion [PAGE 5]. That is label imputation, not direct observation. For badminton, the geometric veto is stronger: “Unlike tennis, badminton is not spherical, resulting in a larger size variation” [PAGE 10], while the final geometry uses a circle finder [PAGE 8]. A circular Hough prior is mismatched to a shuttlecock’s shape and to its 3-to-24-pixel diameter range [PAGE 10]. Veto proof: if a claim depends on shuttlecock center or spin from a circular centroid, the geometry layer does not support it.

The weather layer is the weakest and should be vetoed unless separately measured. The paper includes “9 videos recorded at different tennis courts, including grass court, red clay court, hard court etc.” [PAGE 5], which addresses surface variety, not weather. There is no wind, rain, sun angle, shadow, temperature, or humidity covariate. A missed detection under outdoor glare or wind-blown ball flight cannot be attributed to weather from this source alone. Veto proof: weather claims require synchronized meteorological or illumination data and a model that includes those covariates; neither is present. The adjudication order should be geometric veto first—does the object match the circle and scale assumptions?—then physical veto—is there calibration for the claimed unit?—then biomechanical veto—is contact directly observed or imputed?—then weather veto—is there an environmental measurement? If any layer fails, the tracking output may still be a useful image-space cue, but it cannot support the stronger claim.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
