My multi-hop derivation begins with the preface’s central claim that football and war both require intelligence under uncertainty: “There is more intelligence in one football game” than in “a whole war” [PAGE 13]. That statement establishes the book’s causal chain: uncertainty must be reduced by coaching systems, signals, drills, and diagnosis. The first derived operator is therefore an information-control problem. A team must know what the opponent intends before the opponent can execute it, and must conceal its own intention until execution is already in motion.

The second hop is physiological. Cavanaugh treats the squad as a thermal and mechanical system whose fatigue state is measurable by weight. He says that “careful weighing of each player on accurate scales before and after every practice will provide coach and trainer with the surest index to physical and mental condition in the squad” [PAGE 52]. The rule that “at least three-fourths of the weight stripped off during a day's practice should be regained overnight” [PAGE 53] is not a poetic maxim; it is a recovery constraint. It implies that practice load must not exceed the athlete’s overnight restoration capacity. If the loss is large and the regain is incomplete, the next day’s mechanical output is compromised. This links directly to Cavanaugh’s warning that “thousands of players have become overtrained, without the knowledge of their overseers” [PAGE 55]. The missing variable in many old programs, he argues, is water: “if there is neither water nor fat in the system to supply energy, valuable flesh and muscle must suffer” [PAGE 54]. Thus the physiological chain is: practice intensity, weight loss, fluid replacement, recovery ratio, next-day force production.

The third hop is neuromuscular. Cavanaugh’s grass drill is designed to reduce the delay between command and body repositioning. He describes commands such as “Attention. Front. Right. Left. Back. Go. Faster. Slower. Halt. Rest” and insists that all movements be made “with the least possible motion, exertion or wasted effort, but with the greatest possible speed” [PAGE 29]. The drill’s purpose is “immediate response to the word of command” [PAGE 29]. This yields a simple control-theoretic operator: reduce dead time between perception and motor output. The drill’s value is not merely conditioning; it is mental-physical co-ordination under an unnatural body position, which Cavanaugh says is “difficult at first to follow and instantly execute the commands” [PAGE 32]. The same principle appears in football: the player must “develop the utmost in muscular response to brain command” [PAGE 25].

The fourth hop is tactical geometry. Cavanaugh’s line defense favors a low, immediate charge rather than a waiting stance. He writes that “the time to stop a play is when it starts” [PAGE 85] and that the crouched defense can “match the jump of the attack with a practically simultaneous charge” [PAGE 87]. His theory of offense similarly says: “The essence of the offense is to get the jump on the defensive line” [PAGE 148]. The derived geometric object is a race to a point: the offensive back must reach the line before defensive help arrives, while the defensive lineman must reach the play before the interference forms. Cavanaugh’s end-play chapter makes this explicit: the end should “strike at its heart as quickly as possible, before it has a chance to form and get under way with real power and speed” [PAGE 128]. The gray zone is whether to charge the ball carrier or the interference. Cavanaugh resolves it with a distance condition: if the runner is close to the interference, the end should disrupt the interference; if the runner is far, the end should charge the probable runner path [PAGE 129-130].

The fifth hop is signal theory. Cavanaugh’s “only starting signal” is designed to eliminate delayed response. He rejects methods that make players wait for a repeated number because that creates “an unnecessary, nervous tension” and a charge that is “not a charge as a unit” [PAGE 188]. His preferred method sets a known count after a break, so that “the charge, the snap and the quarterback's bark all going together” [PAGE 189]. This is a synchronization operator: the snap time must be known to all players with minimal information loss, while the opponent’s inference probability remains low. The source warns that if a coach has “one punt signal only,” a spy may reconstruct the whole system [PAGE 213]. Thus signal design must balance internal certainty against external leakage.

The sixth hop is kicking time. Cavanaugh states that a ball kicked “one and four-fifths seconds after the center moves his hands to pass in the ordinary kick formation is considered perfectly safe,” while “a punt delayed for two and one-fifth seconds should be blocked” [PAGE 248]. This is a hard temporal veto: the kicking operation must be completed inside a safety window. The chain is snap, pass, handling, drop, contact. Any delay increases block probability. The same temporal logic applies to the surprise kick: the kicker must be in a natural position and must not telegraph the kick [PAGE 227-229].

The final audit is the gray zone between aggression and safety. Cavanaugh repeatedly rejects excessive protection because it creates false confidence and fatigue. The overall reasoning is therefore not “more force is better,” but “the smallest sufficient control variable that produces reliable execution under uncertainty is best.” That is the book’s hidden formal structure: reduce delay, reduce uncertainty, reduce excess mass, and make the body respond to command before the opponent can diagnose the play.

1. Recovery-load constraint from body weight  
Cavanaugh’s weighing system gives a measurable recovery operator. Let body mass be measured in kilograms:

\[
W_{\text{pre}},\quad W_{\text{post}},\quad W_{\text{next}}
\]

where  
- \(W_{\text{pre}}\) = mass before practice,  
- \(W_{\text{post}}\) = mass after practice,  
- \(W_{\text{next}}\) = mass the next morning.

Define acute practice loss:

\[
L = W_{\text{pre}} - W_{\text{post}}
\]

and overnight regain:

\[
R = W_{\text{next}} - W_{\text{post}}
\]

Cavanaugh’s rule is:

\[
\frac{R}{L} \geq \frac{3}{4}
\]

except for intentional reduction of excess flesh. The source says: “At least three-fourths of the weight stripped off during a day's practice should be regained overnight” [PAGE 53]. Units: \(L\) and \(R\) are kilograms; the ratio is dimensionless. Breakdown: if \(L = 2.0\ \mathrm{kg}\), then \(R \geq 1.5\ \mathrm{kg}\). If \(R/L < 0.75\), the athlete is entering the next session with unresolved fluid and tissue deficit. Cavanaugh warns that if the man is “losing too much weight on exercise and not getting enough of it back, something is wrong” [PAGE 53-54].

2. Hydration operator  
Cavanaugh rejects the old abstinence doctrine and treats the athlete like a heat engine requiring water. Let practice fluid intake be:

\[
V_{\text{practice}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} v_i
\]

where \(v_i\) is the volume served at interval \(i\), in liters, and \(n\) is the number of intervals. He recommends small amounts at least thrice during a workout [PAGE 37]. The source says: “During a hard practice the average man can absorb a pint of this refreshment if given to him at three or four intervals” [PAGE 54-55]. Using SI conversion:

\[
1\ \mathrm{pint} \approx 0.473\ \mathrm{L}
\]

Thus:

\[
V_{\text{practice}} \lesssim 0.473\ \mathrm{L}
\]

for the practice period, divided into small doses. For the remainder of the day, Cavanaugh writes that “a quart and a pint additional of water taken during the remainder of the day, equalling six ordinary glasses, is not too much” [PAGE 37]. In SI units:

\[
V_{\text{after}} = 1\ \mathrm{qt} + 1\ \mathrm{pt}
\approx 0.946\ \mathrm{L} + 0.473\ \mathrm{L}
= 1.419\ \mathrm{L}
\]

Total daily target, excluding unusual cases:

\[
V_{\text{day}} = V_{\text{practice}} + V_{\text{after}}
\lesssim 1.89\ \mathrm{L}
\]

Breakdown: this is not a universal prescription; it is Cavanaugh’s coaching threshold for ordinary training. The source also warns: “Too much water, like too much of anything, is harmful” [PAGE 37]. The operator is therefore bounded: insufficient water causes tissue loss and overtraining; excessive water is harmful.

3. Grass-drill response operator  
The grass drill measures and trains the latency between command and movement. Let:

\[
t_{\text{resp}} = t_{\text{motor}} - t_{\text{command}}
\]

where \(t_{\text{resp}}\) is response latency in seconds. If the body must rotate or rise through distance \(d\) in meters, average response speed is:

\[
v_{\text{resp}} = \frac{d}{t_{\text{resp}}}
\]

with units \(\mathrm{m/s}\). Cavanaugh says the drill “demands and teaches immediate response to the word of command” [PAGE 29]. The commands are explicitly listed: “Attention. Front. Right. Left. Back. Go. Faster. Slower. Halt. Rest” [PAGE 29]. He also states that the drill develops “greater speed and accuracy of thought in the man, and the ability to think speedily and accurately whatever the physical dilemma” [PAGE 51]. Breakdown: the drill is not merely calisthenics; it is a trained reduction of \(t_{\text{resp}}\) under awkward starting positions.

4. Tackling target-point operator  
Cavanaugh’s tackling doctrine says the tackler must aim beyond the runner’s visible position:

\[
x_{\text{target}} = x_{\text{runner}} + \delta
\]

where \(\delta\) is the forward correction distance. The source gives:

\[
\delta \in [1\ \mathrm{ft},\ 2\ \mathrm{ft}]
\]

or in SI:

\[
\delta \in [0.305\ \mathrm{m},\ 0.610\ \mathrm{m}]
\]

The quote is: “the runner must be reached a foot to two feet beyond where he is actually seen to be” [PAGE 38]. He also requires the tackler to “watch the knees and lower legs rather than the head” [PAGE 8] and to turn the head to avoid punishment [PAGE 38-39]. Breakdown: if \(\delta = 0\), the tackler meets the runner’s current position rather than the projected position; if \(\delta\) is too large, the tackler misses. The correct value is a coachable biomechanical interval.

5. Starting-signal synchronization and information leakage  
Let the complete signal sequence be:

\[
S = (s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_m)
\]

where each \(s_i\) is a spoken number. The play number \(p\) may be formed by addition:

\[
p = a + b
\]

with the second number restricted to avoid mental overload. Cavanaugh says: “never add or subtract a larger number than five” [PAGE 194]. The starting count \(c\) is derived from a key:

\[
c = g(s_j)
\]

where \(c\) is the number of the post-break count on which the snap occurs. If the cadence interval is \(\Delta t\) seconds per number, then:

\[
t_{\text{snap}} = t_{\text{break}} + c\Delta t
\]

The desired condition is simultaneity:

\[
t_{\text{snap}} \approx t_{\text{charge}}
\]

Cavanaugh says: “the charge, the snap and the quarterback's bark all going together” [PAGE 189]. The internal information set is low entropy for the offense but high uncertainty for the defense. If the opponent observes \(N\) signals and has per-observation inference probability \(q\), the probability of eventual detection is:

\[
D_N = 1 - (1-q)^N
\]

The source warns that a single punt signal can become “the key to the entire signal system” if the opponent records several signals and finds the formula [PAGE 213-214]. Therefore multiple punt signals are required. Units: \(D_N\) is probability; \(q\) is probability per observation; \(N\) is count.

6. Punt temporal safety window  
Let:

\[
t_k = t_{\text{snap}} + t_{\text{pass}} + t_{\text{adjust}} + t_{\text{swing}}
\]

where \(t_k\) is the time from snap to ball contact, in seconds. Cavanaugh gives explicit thresholds:

\[
t_k \leq 1.8\ \mathrm{s} \quad \text{safe}
\]

and

\[
t_k \geq 2.2\ \mathrm{s} \quad \text{blockable}
\]

The source says: “a ball kicked one and four-fifths seconds after the center moves his hands to pass in the ordinary kick formation is considered perfectly safe,” whereas “a punt delayed for two and one-fifth seconds should be blocked” [PAGE 248]. Breakdown: if pass distance increases, \(t_{\text{pass}}\) increases; if the center’s hands telegraph the play, the defense can charge earlier; if the kicker turns too long, \(t_{\text{adjust}}\) increases.

7. Defensive charge intercept geometry  
Let the ball carrier’s path be:

\[
\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{r}_0 + \mathbf{v}_r t
\]

and the defender’s path be:

\[
\mathbf{e}(t) = \mathbf{e}_0 + \mathbf{v}_e t
\]

where positions are in meters, velocities in \(\mathrm{m/s}\), and time in seconds. Under constant-velocity approximation, intercept time is:

\[
t^* = \frac{\|\mathbf{r}_0 - \mathbf{e}_0\|}{\|\mathbf{v}_e - \mathbf{v}_r\|}
\]

provided the denominator is nonzero and the geometry permits interception. Cavanaugh’s end must “charge for a point where he can meet the opposing backfield” and must not waste energy on angular delay [PAGE 125-126]. He also says the end must “strike at its heart as quickly as possible, before it has a chance to form” [PAGE 128]. Breakdown: if the end hesitates, \(t^*\) increases, allowing interference to form. If the end charges too wide, the path length increases and the intercept fails.

8. Defensive-line jump operator  
Let the offensive snap time be \(t_s\), and let the defender’s movement initiation be \(t_d\). The defensive advantage is:

\[
\Delta t = t_d - t_s
\]

Negative or near-zero \(\Delta t\) means the defense charges with the snap rather than after it. Cavanaugh says the crouched defense can “match the jump of the attack with a practically simultaneous charge” [PAGE 87]. The standing defense, by contrast, “permits the attack its chance to get to the line of scrimmage” [PAGE 84]. The operator is therefore:

\[
\Delta t_{\text{crouched}} < \Delta t_{\text{standing}}
\]

with the caveat that the crouch must not blind the defender to the play. Cavanaugh resolves this by requiring the defender to “look up through their own eyebrows as they advance” [PAGE 87].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Cavanaugh’s 1919 book sits at a hinge point in American football. The preface places the text between the First World War and the postwar reconstruction of college athletics. He writes from France, hearing “the clanking and grinding of artillery” and reflecting that football is “very like warfare” only if intelligence is present [PAGE 13]. This military framing is not incidental. Cavanaugh was a Dartmouth coach, and the grass drill’s later military interest is explicitly noted: army officers saw its value, but rain and mud abroad made it impractical because “the soldier has only one suit of clothes” [PAGE 51]. The genealogy therefore runs from prewar college coaching to wartime conditioning and back to postwar football standardization.

One lineage is the shift from brute force to coordinated intelligence. Cavanaugh complains that modern coaching has “too much confusion among us, too much bewilderment” and calls for “a consistent theory of the whole art of football” [PAGE 15]. This is a scientific-management lineage: the coach must define assignments, measure weight, regulate water, schedule practice, and encode plays in signals. The book’s recurring target is improvisation without system. He criticizes the charging machine and bucking strap as “abhorrent” and “inferior” because they lack “individuality, no come-back” and fail to preserve snap and speed [PAGE 35-36]. The tackling dummy survives only as “a necessary evil” because live tackling in feature practice causes more injuries than game-like practice [PAGE 37]. This is an early injury-management lineage: specificity of practice is not merely tactical but physiological.

A second lineage is equipment reform. Cavanaugh’s chapter “The Warrior's Armor” argues against excessive protective devices: “Whatever protection is used should be particularly chosen with a view to strength and lightness” [PAGE 30]. He requires headgear: “no football man should ever go on the field for a scrimmage or a game without a headgear” [PAGE 30]. He also rejects high-cut shoes because they produce a false sense of bracing: “The result was an extraordinary number of sprained ankles” [PAGE 31-32]. Instead, he cuts shoes down and forces the ankle “to do its work and protect itself” [PAGE 32]. This is a biomechanical lineage: external bracing can suppress the natural motor control that protects the joint. The same logic appears in his preference for light hip pads, kidney pads, thigh pads, and a wet sponge knee pad [PAGE 31]. Protection must reduce injury without adding fatigue.

A third lineage is the evolution of line defense. Cavanaugh identifies three defensive styles: standing line, all men down, and a mixed correct method [PAGE 84]. He rejects the standing defense because it allows the attack to reach the line before contact: “The time to stop a play is when it starts” [PAGE 85]. Yet the all-crouched defense is also insufficient because defenders may lose sight of the backfield [PAGE 86]. The correct method is an “extreme crouch against regular formations” with higher positions against shifting formations [PAGE 86]. This lineage anticipates later defensive hybrids: low leverage for line battles, vision for diagnosis.

A fourth lineage is signal cryptography. Cavanaugh’s “only starting signal” is designed to solve a recurring problem: teams wait for a repeated number and therefore start late. He says the waiting method creates “a charge as soon thereafter as each man can accomplish it” [PAGE 188]. His solution is a known count after a break, with the snap occurring on a predetermined number in an even rhythm [PAGE 208-210]. The genealogy runs from simple cadence signals to disguised arithmetic keys, changing from addition to subtraction, then to digit extraction [PAGE 194-195]. The multi-week trap is obvious: if the opponent observes enough signals, the system becomes inferable. Cavanaugh explicitly warns that one punt signal can reveal the whole system [PAGE 213-214].

The multi-week traps in the book are practical coaching errors that compound over time. The first is overscrimmage. Cavanaugh says the bulk of scrimmage should be completed early so injuries can mend, and later teams should go into games “without the sore spots” rather than with extra knowledge plus soreness [PAGE 41]. The second is excessive water restriction. The old theory “that it is fatal to take liquids into the system during hard exercise” produces overtraining and failure [PAGE 36-37]. The third is false bracing through high shoes or locked legs. Locked legs among center and guards reduce efficiency and make charging difficult [PAGE 117-118]. The fourth is telegraphed intention: centers who use different passing methods for kicks and short passes give away the play [PAGE 218-219]. The fifth is single-signal dependence, which allows opponent deduction [PAGE 213-214]. These traps are genealogical because they persist unless the coach audits them over multiple weeks.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Cavanaugh’s coaching system is a recursive theory of mind: the coach must model what the opponent believes about what the coach intends. The clearest example is the quarterback under center on kick formations. Cavanaugh says many teams keep the quarterback under center for deception, but the deception “generally fails” because the quarterback cannot keep his hands in the same position; he holds them farther apart and away from the ball [PAGE 119-120]. The quarterback’s body betrays his uncertainty. The coach must therefore model the quarterback’s fear, the defense’s observation, and the second-order inference that the defense makes from that fear.

The center is another recursive node. Cavanaugh calls the center “veritably the hub of the wheel” [PAGE 105] and warns that the center’s passing style can reveal intention. If the center uses one method for short passes and another for punt passes, the defense can infer the play immediately [PAGE 200-201]. The center must therefore randomize or camouflage passing style: “when the spiral is used at all, many of the center's short passes to his quarterback should be made on the spiral plan” [PAGE 90]. This is a counter-trap: the offense hides the signal inside a routine, but the routine must remain executable.

The defensive quarterback is modeled as a high-judgment agent. Cavanaugh says he “must stop line bucks, outside tackle plays and end runs” and is “directly responsible for practically all long gains” [PAGE 100]. The primary rule is to keep eyes on center, then quarterback, then ends [PAGE 100]. The recursive loop is: offense hides intention in formation and cadence; defense watches center and quarterback for pre-snap leakage; offense must then invent a second-order deception, such as a fake pass, delayed buck, or check play. Cavanaugh says check plays are needed to prevent defenders from taking chances by early reinforcement of a danger spot [PAGE 142]. The check play is a belief-management tool: it teaches the defense that committing early is unsafe.

Crisis habit regression occurs when teams revert under pressure. Cavanaugh observes that a standing-defense team, when unable to stop the attack, suddenly assumes a lower position: “until finally the line is down where it belongs, on its hands, on the ground” [PAGE 86]. This is regression to a more instinctive posture. Another regression is zone play. Cavanaugh calls zone play an over-systematized habit driven by fear of criticism [PAGE 267-268]. Coaches instruct quarterbacks conservatively because they fear blame if unconventional tactics fail [PAGE 268]. The result is predictable play: “a style of play in the attacking team's own territory so conservative as to allow an experienced player on defense to predict almost without failure what the opposing quarterback will elect to do” [PAGE 268]. The crisis is not only physical fatigue; it is reputational fear. The coach’s recursive model must account for the opponent’s expectation of conservatism.

The counter-trap is to violate the opponent’s expected model at the moment when violation is physically safe. Cavanaugh’s example is a team backed near its own goal line. Zone doctrine says kick. Cavanaugh argues that a well-chosen running play or safe forward pass may be better because it avoids repeated dangerous snaps and long punts from deep territory [PAGE 271-276]. He says: “The time to score is in the first minute of the game, if possible; if not then, in the next minute” [PAGE 269]. This is not recklessness; it is a model-based attack on the defense’s expected conservative behavior. The opponent expects a kick; the offense chooses a play that exploits the defense’s readiness to stop a kick or a predictable line attack.

The clock enters as a third recursive variable. There are three clocks: the pre-snap clock, the play clock, and the season clock. The pre-snap clock is governed by the starting signal. Cavanaugh’s cadence eliminates waiting and makes the snap simultaneous with the charge [PAGE 189]. The play clock is governed by geometry and fatigue. The end must reach the interference before it forms [PAGE 128]. The tackler must target a point beyond the runner [PAGE 38]. The season clock is governed by recovery. Weight regain, water, sleep, and rest determine whether the team can execute late-season plays [PAGE 52-56]. Cavanaugh says the coach must avoid using up a player in a preliminary game if he is needed for a championship contest [PAGE 66]. The recursive model must therefore optimize not only the next play but the next week.

Sequence plays illustrate the fragility of recursive control. Cavanaugh says a sequence can sweep opponents from their feet if surprise is present, but it also “disorganizes most teams using them” [PAGE 175]. The opponent wakes up: “a good team against which even a two-play sequence has been attempted becomes at once the liveliest and most wide-awake aggregation imaginable” [PAGE 176]. The coach must model the opponent’s arousal state. If the opponent is already alert, the sequence loses value. If the opponent is passive, the sequence may create disorder. The optimal policy is conditional, not fixed.

The captain and quarterback form a hierarchy of recursive judgment. Cavanaugh says the captain is “G. H. Q.” and should not constantly interfere with the quarterback, who is the “general” [PAGE 117]. The captain should intervene only when the quarterback is seriously wrong. Cavanaugh’s best captain challenged the quarterback’s judgment only six times in a season and was right each time [PAGE 117]. This is a model of disciplined intervention: too much intervention destroys initiative; too little allows error. The coach must model both the quarterback’s confidence and the team’s trust in the captain.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto test asks whether a tactic survives physical, biomechanical, and weather constraints. In Cavanaugh’s system, a tactic is vetoed when the body, the equipment, or the environment makes the tactic less efficient than an available alternative. The veto is not moral; it is operational.

First veto: mud vetoes reliance on the spiral snap. Cavanaugh says centers should learn the spiral and regular passes, but “the spiral cannot, however, be relied upon exclusively, because of its impracticability on muddy days” [PAGE 23]. Later he says: “As a mud-covered ball is sometimes inevitable, centers must also be able to use the slower pass” [PAGE 89]. The formal condition is:

\[
\text{Use spiral only if } P(\text{clean ball}) \approx 1
\]

or, more precisely:

\[
\text{If ball is mud-covered, prefer regular pass.}
\]

Breakdown: mud increases grip uncertainty and changes the ball’s interaction with the center’s hands. Cavanaugh notes that mud “retards charging and blocking rather more than it retards the speed of the passing for kicks” [PAGE 89], but for short snaps from center, reliability matters more than marginal speed. The veto proof is direct: the source explicitly makes the spiral impracticable in mud.

Second veto: rain and mud veto the grass drill for soldiers, and excessively for football players. Cavanaugh says the grass drill should be used “every day, except in excessively rainy or muddy weather” [PAGE 51]. The military adoption failed because of “the almost continual rain and mud encountered abroad” and the soldier’s single suit of clothes [PAGE 51]. The veto inequality is:

\[
\text{If ground is excessively wet or muddy, grass drill value decreases because clothing, skin abrasion, and thermal loss rise.}
\]

Breakdown: the drill requires repeated prostrate movement. In dry conditions, the risk is low; Cavanaugh says he never saw an injury from it in six years [PAGE 49-50]. In wet mud, the cost rises and the benefit falls.

Third veto: excessive padding is vetoed by fatigue. Cavanaugh says: “A man can be injured by first becoming exhausted through carrying too many pounds of pads and braces” [PAGE 30]. The formal condition is:

\[
\text{Protection is valid only if added mass does not reduce work capacity below the threshold needed for safe execution.}
\]

Let \(m_p\) be added protective mass and \(W\) be useful work capacity. A veto occurs if:

\[
\frac{\partial W}{\partial m_p} < 0
\]

beyond the point where protection reduces injury more than fatigue increases it. Cavanaugh’s preference for “strength and lightness” [PAGE 30] is the source’s own optimization rule.

Fourth veto: high-cut shoes are biomechanically vetoed because they suppress ankle control. Cavanaugh says tight high shoes created “a decided sensation of protection” but produced “an extraordinary number of sprained ankles” [PAGE 31-32]. When the ankle works freely, it protects itself: “Cutting the shoe down and lacing it tightly over the instep, giving no brace whatever to the ankle, forced this joint to do its work and protect itself” [PAGE 32]. He adds: “I cannot recall three cases of sprained ankle in fifteen years of coaching” [PAGE 32]. This is a long-horizon veto proof: the tactic with apparent protection had worse outcomes than the tactic requiring active joint control.

Fifth veto: water abstinence is physiologically vetoed. Cavanaugh says the active body “should be treated like a steam engine” and supplied with water [PAGE 36]. If water and fat are absent, “valuable flesh and muscle must suffer” [PAGE 36]. The veto condition is:

\[
\text{If } V_{\text{intake}} < V_{\text{required}},
\]

then tissue loss, fatigue, and overtraining rise. The source says many players have become overtrained because water was withheld [PAGE 37]. The old gargle-and-spit custom is called “a horrible fallacy” [PAGE 38]. This is a cross-layer veto because the tactic fails at the physiological layer even if it appears psychologically tough.

Sixth veto: headgear is required because the cost of cuts and head injuries is high. Cavanaugh says headgear “neither makes a coward out of him to wear one nor a braver man to go without” [PAGE 30]. It avoids “many cuts requiring stitches, and other serious head injuries” [PAGE 30]. The veto proof is direct: the tactic of playing without headgear increases injury exposure without increasing courage.

Seventh veto: telegraphed intention is cognitively vetoed. If the center’s hand position reveals a punt pass, the defense can “exclude immediately” line attack and adjust [PAGE 218-219]. The source says one promising team “spoiled its season by having two methods of passing from center” [PAGE 219]. The formal veto is:

\[
\text{If } P(\text{opponent infers play} \mid \text{center behavior}) \text{ is high, tactic is vetoed.}
\]

The remedy is mixed passing styles, multiple signals, and concealed intention [PAGE 90, 213-214].

The final cross-layer proof is the scrimmage line itself. Cavanaugh tells the team: “Hate and abhor the scrimmage line! It is a restraining mark” [PAGE 291]. The line is a physical constraint; the body must leave it instantly. On defense, the line must be “on the ground, charging as one man with the snap of the ball” [PAGE 292]. Any tactic that keeps the player behind the line after the snap is vetoed because it surrenders time. The line is the boundary between legal stillness and active play. Cavanaugh’s entire system is built to minimize time spent under that constraint.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>

Scenario 1: Signal-codex leak veto  
Counterfactual setup: a coach uses one punt signal formula for the entire season and uses the same arithmetic style for other signals. An opponent records several punt signals and infers the rule.  
Verifiable test: collect the first five punt signals observed by the opponent and test whether a single addition, subtraction, or digit rule maps them to the actual punts. If the mapping succeeds, the signal system is compromised.  
Source basis: Cavanaugh says the punt signal can become “the key to the entire signal system” and that a spy may record four, five, or six punt signals and compare them [PAGE 213-214].  
Veto rule:

\[
\text{If } D_N \geq \tau,
\]

where \(D_N = 1 - (1-q)^N\) is the opponent’s detection probability after \(N\) observations and \(\tau\) is a coaching tolerance threshold, the single-code system is vetoed.  
Prediction: after the opponent infers the code, fake punts, delayed punts, and surprise kicks from that formation will fail at a higher rate. The coach should adopt multiple punt signals [PAGE 214].  
Verifiable prediction: in the second half, the team using a single punt signal will show a measurable decline in successful deception plays compared with the first half, unless the signal set is changed.

Scenario 2: Punt timing veto  
Counterfactual setup: a center’s pass, catch, drop, and swing require more than 2.2 seconds from snap to contact.  
Verifiable test: measure \(t_k\) with video frames or a stopwatch. Count blocked kicks, hurried kicks, and poor kicks as a function of \(t_k\).  
Source basis: Cavanaugh states that a kick made within 1.8 seconds is safe, while a punt delayed to 2.2 seconds “should be blocked” [PAGE 248]. He also says there is no excuse for a blocked kick when the pass is good, assignments are completed, and the kicker is fast [PAGE 243-244].  
Veto rule:

\[
t_k > 2.2\ \mathrm{s}
\]

vetoes the punt as executed under pressure.  
Prediction: if the center passes slowly, the kicker adjusts slowly, or the line fails to hold the rush, block probability rises sharply after the 2.2-second threshold. The corrective actions are a faster spiral pass where conditions permit [PAGE 89], a deeper kicker position [PAGE 217], and tighter line protection on kick formations [PAGE 119].  
Verifiable prediction: teams that reduce \(t_k\) below 1.8 seconds will have fewer blocked kicks and better field-position outcomes than teams that routinely exceed 2.2 seconds.

Scenario 3: Mud-spiral veto  
Counterfactual setup: the field is muddy, the ball is wet or mud-covered, and the center attempts to use the spiral pass exclusively because it is faster in dry conditions.  
Verifiable test: compare snap success rates for spiral versus regular passes under muddy conditions. Record fumbles, bad snaps, and punter adjustments.  
Source basis: Cavanaugh says the spiral “cannot... be relied upon exclusively, because of its impracticability on muddy days” [PAGE 23], and that centers must also use the slower pass because “a mud-covered ball is sometimes inevitable” [PAGE 89].  
Veto rule:

\[
\text{If surface condition is muddy and ball grip is degraded, veto exclusive spiral snapping.}
\]

Formal condition:

\[
P(\text{successful spiral} \mid \text{mud}) < P(\text{successful regular pass} \mid \text{mud})
\]

then the regular pass is preferred.  
Prediction: in mud, a team that insists on spiral-only snapping will have more mishandled snaps, more punter delays, and more blocked or aborted kicks. The counter-trap is to mix passing styles so the center can choose the regular pass when grip is poor [PAGE 89-90].  
Verifiable prediction: after switching from spiral-only to conditional regular snapping in mud, snap-to-kick time variance will decrease and blocked-kick events will decline.

Scenario 4: End hesitation veto  
Counterfactual setup: a defensive end stops charging and waits to see the play develop.  
Verifiable test: measure the end’s first three steps after the snap and the time until interference forms. Compare gains on plays where the end hesitates versus plays where the end charges immediately.  
Source basis: Cavanaugh says “the great secret of defense is fast charging ends” [PAGE 126] and that the end must “strike at its heart as quickly as possible, before it has a chance to form” [PAGE 128]. He also says the mistake is to “discontinue the forward charge” because each stride increases the runner’s uncertainty and delays the interference [PAGE 131].  
Veto rule:

\[
\text{If the end hesitates, the play is not yet stopped.}
\]

Using the intercept equation:

\[
t^* = \frac{\|\mathbf{r}_0 - \mathbf{e}_0\|}{\|\mathbf{v}_e - \mathbf{v}_r\|}
\]

hesitation increases the initial distance or reduces effective velocity, increasing \(t^*\). If \(t^*\) exceeds the time required for interference to form, the tactic is vetoed.  
Prediction: hesitation will produce larger gains around the end and more yards after contact. Immediate charging will force the runner to change direction or slow down, allowing secondary defenders to arrive.  
Verifiable prediction: ends who charge within the first step after the snap will reduce opponent’s wide-play efficiency compared with ends who wait for diagnosis.

Scenario 5: Recovery-load veto  
Counterfactual setup: a player loses 3 kg in practice but regains only 1 kg overnight.  
Verifiable test: weigh the player before practice, after practice, and the next morning. Compute \(R/L\).  
Source basis: Cavanaugh requires at least three-fourths of practice weight loss to be regained overnight [PAGE 53]. If not, “something is wrong” [PAGE 54].  
Veto rule:

\[
\frac{R}{L} < \frac{3}{4}
\]

vetoes the next day’s full-load participation unless corrective rest, food, and water are provided.  
Prediction: the player will show reduced snap, reduced charge, poorer diagnosis, and higher injury risk. The coach should reduce practice load, increase fluid replacement, and monitor weight [PAGE 52-55].  
Verifiable prediction: players kept below the recovery threshold will show declining performance in grass drills, tackling, and signal response over the following days.
