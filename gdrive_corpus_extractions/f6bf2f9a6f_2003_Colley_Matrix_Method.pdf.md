The derivation begins with Laplace’s rule of succession, not with raw winning percentage. Let team \(i\) have \(n_{w,i}\) wins, \(n_{\ell,i}\) losses, and \(n_{tot,i}=n_{w,i}+n_{\ell,i}\) games. Colley writes \(r_i=(1+n_{w,i})/(2+n_{tot,i})\) [PAGE 5]. The integral derivation is the Bayesian posterior mean under a uniform prior on \([0,1]\): if \(\hat r\) is the unknown rating, the likelihood for \(n_w\) wins and \(n_\ell\) losses is proportional to \(\hat r^{n_w}(1-\hat r)^{n_\ell}\), so
\[
r_i=\frac{\int_0^1 \hat r^{n_{w,i}+1}(1-\hat r)^{n_{\ell,i}}\,d\hat r}{\int_0^1 \hat r^{n_{w,i}}(1-\hat r)^{n_{\ell,i}}\,d\hat r}
=\frac{1+n_{w,i}}{2+n_{w,i}+n_{\ell,i}}.
\]
This is dimensionless and probability-like. It resolves the gray-zone cases that raw winning percentage cannot: before any games, all teams have \(r=1/2\), and a 1-0 team versus an 0-1 team yields \(2/3\) versus \(1/3\), not \(1\) versus \(0\) [PAGE 5]. The “craps table” analogy [PAGE 5] is not decorative; it supplies the uniform prior and the beta-function normalization.

The second move is strength of schedule. Colley rewrites wins as \(n_{w,i}=(n_{w,i}-n_{\ell,i})/2+n_{tot,i}/2\), then replaces the random-opponent value \(1/2\) with the actual opponent ratings:
\[
n^{eff}_{w,i}=\frac{n_{w,i}-n_{\ell,i}}{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{tot,i}} r_{ij}
\]
[PAGE 7]. The fixed-point condition is therefore
\[
(2+n_{tot,i})r_i-\sum_j n_{j,i}r_j=1+\frac{n_{w,i}-n_{\ell,i}}{2},
\]
where \(n_{j,i}\) is the number of times team \(i\) played team \(j\). In matrix form, \(C\vec r=\vec b\), with \(c_{ii}=2+n_{tot,i}\), \(c_{ij}=-n_{j,i}\), and \(b_i=1+(n_{w,i}-n_{\ell,i})/2\) [PAGE 10]. The paper calls \(C\) the Colley Matrix and solves it by Cholesky decomposition and back-substitution [PAGE 10].

The conservation and positivity audits are central. Colley decomposes \(C=2I+\sum_k G_k\), where each game operator has \(+1\) on two diagonal entries and \(-1\) on the corresponding off-diagonal entries [PAGE 14]. Then \(\sum_i(C\vec r)_i=2\sum_i r_i\), while \(\sum_i b_i=N\), so the average rating is exactly \(1/2\) [PAGE 14]. Positive definiteness follows because
\[
\vec v^T C\vec v=2\sum_i v_i^2+\sum_k (v_i-v_j)^2,
\]
which is strictly positive for nonzero \(\vec v\) [PAGE 15]. The singularity audit is equally exact: if the \(+2\) is removed from \(c_{ii}\), every row and column sums to zero, so the matrix is singular [PAGE 15]. The iterative scheme converges because the denominator is \(n+2\), not \(n\); without the \(+2\), the round-robin error factor would be \(n/n=1\) and convergence would fail [PAGE 11].

The gray-zone audit is the schedule graph. Non-I-A games are excluded because the method “relies on some interconnectedness between opponents” [PAGE 7], and “padding the schedule with I-AA teams contributes absolutely nothing to a team’s rating” [PAGE 7]. Early-season rankings are prior-dominated: no games means all ratings are \(1/2\) [PAGE 5], and the paper explicitly has no pre-season poll [PAGE 3]. The comparison to press polls is not ground truth; Colley admits “we do not actually know the ‘truth’” [PAGE 15] and uses a mean absolute ratio \(\eta\), typically about \(1.25\) [PAGE 16]. The method is reproducible and bias-free relative to its declared inputs, but it cannot adjudicate physical context, weather, or biomechanics except insofar as those alter wins, losses, or the schedule graph. That boundary is the main audit point.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines a sequence of formal operators. All ratings are dimensionless probability-like quantities on \([0,1]\); all counts \(n_{w,i}\), \(n_{\ell,i}\), \(n_{tot,i}\), and \(n_{j,i}\) are dimensionless game counts; the matrix entries and right-hand side are dimensionless. No SI units enter because the method uses only win-loss counts.

1. Laplace rating operator:
\[
r_i=\frac{1+n_{w,i}}{2+n_{w,i}+n_{\ell,i}}.
\]
Assumption: a uniform Bayesian prior on the unknown rating, with likelihood \(\hat r^{n_w}(1-\hat r)^{n_\ell}\). The paper states: “All teams at the beginning of the season, when no games have been played, have an equal rating of 1/2” [PAGE 5]. Breakdown regimes: zero games gives \(r_i=1/2\); one win gives \(2/3\); one loss gives \(1/3\) [PAGE 5]. Removing the \(+1\) and \(+2\) recovers raw winning percentage, which is undefined before play and produces the \(1\) versus \(0\) extremal nuisance that the paper explicitly rejects [PAGE 4].

2. Strength-of-schedule effective wins:
\[
n^{eff}_{w,i}=\frac{n_{w,i}-n_{\ell,i}}{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{tot,i}} r_{ij}.
\]
The second term is “the adjustment for strength of schedule” [PAGE 7]. Assumption: opponent ratings are the same simultaneous unknowns being solved for. Breakdown regime: if all opponents are treated as random \(1/2\) teams, the sum collapses to \(n_{tot,i}/2\) and one returns to the Laplace statistic; if the schedule graph is sparse, the prior \(1/2\) pulls more strongly.

3. Colley Matrix equation:
\[
C\vec r=\vec b,
\]
with
\[
c_{ii}=2+n_{tot,i},\qquad c_{ij}=-n_{j,i},\qquad b_i=1+\frac{n_{w,i}-n_{\ell,i}}{2}.
\]
The paper states: “The matrix C is defined as the Colley Matrix. Solving equations (17)–(19) is the method for rating the teams” [PAGE 10]. Assumption: the game multigraph is encoded exactly by \(n_{j,i}\). Breakdown regime: the \(+2\) is essential; without it, the matrix is singular [PAGE 15].

4. Conservation operator:
\[
C=2I+\sum_k G_k,
\]
where each game contributes \(G^k_{ii}=G^k_{jj}=1\) and \(G^k_{ij}=G^k_{ji}=-1\) [PAGE 14]. Therefore
\[
\sum_i (C\vec r)_i=2\sum_i r_i,\qquad \sum_i b_i=N,
\]
so
\[
\frac{1}{N}\sum_i r_i=\frac12.
\]
The paper states: “the average value of r is exactly one-half” [PAGE 14]. This is a conserved prior mass, not an ad hoc normalization.

5. Positive-definiteness operator:
\[
\vec v^T C\vec v=2\sum_i v_i^2+\sum_k (v_i-v_j)^2.
\]
Since the first term is positive for nonzero \(\vec v\) and the second is nonnegative, \(C\) is positive definite [PAGE 15]. This is why Cholesky decomposition is stable and legal [PAGE 10].

6. Singularity operator for straight winning percentage:
\[
c_{ii}=n_{tot,i},\qquad c_{ij}=-n_{j,i}.
\]
Then every row and column has zero sum. The paper states: “if one removes the addend of 2 from cii, the total of the cij’s for any row or any column is zero... hence the matrix is singular” [PAGE 15]. Breakdown regime: raw winning percentage cannot define a unique matrix ranking; the iterative scheme also fails because the convergence factor becomes \(n/n=1\) [PAGE 11].

7. Poll-comparison statistic:
\[
\eta=\exp\left(\frac{1}{25}\sum_{i=1}^{25}\left|\log j_C(team_i)-\log i\right|\right).
\]
This is a dimensionless mean absolute ratio. The paper reports values “typically about 1.25” [PAGE 16]. Assumption: the top-25 press ranking is a comparison baseline, not truth. Breakdown regime: the statistic is descriptive; it does not validate causality or remove poll correlation.

The declared assumptions are: Div. I-A games only; wins and losses only; no margin; no home/away adjustment; no conference, tradition, or regional prior; no pre-season poll [PAGE 1, PAGE 3, PAGE 4]. The exact breakdown regimes are: no games yields the uniform prior; non-I-A games are ignored [PAGE 7]; removal of the Laplace constants yields a singular matrix [PAGE 15]; and iterative convergence depends on the \(+2\) denominator [PAGE 11].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is explicit: Laplace’s rule of succession, introduced to Colley by his thesis advisor J. Richard Gott, III [PAGE 4], and formalized through the craps-table posterior-mean problem [PAGE 5]. That lineage matters because the method is not a generic “win percentage plus schedule” heuristic. It is a Bayesian estimator with a uniform prior, then a simultaneous linear system. The historical setting is the Bowl Championship Series era: the paper describes the NCAA’s 1998 BCS arrangement [PAGE 1] and the 2001 pressure on computer rankers to “eliminate score dependence altogether” [PAGE 3]. Colley’s response is to remove score margin entirely and solve for ratings that conserve average \(1/2\) [PAGE 1, PAGE 14].

The retrocausal feature is structural. A team’s final rating is not computed week by week as a frozen sum. It is the simultaneous solution \(C\vec r=\vec b\). When an opponent’s rating rises or falls in later weeks, the meaning of an earlier win changes retroactively through the sum \(\sum_j r_{ij}\). The paper’s two-team example shows this mechanically: the winner’s initial \(2/3\) drops to \(11/18\), then rises to \(17/27\), then converges to \(5/8\), while the loser converges to \(3/8\) [PAGE 8, PAGE 9]. The final values are not the first-week values; they are the fixed point of all games at once.

Multi-week traps follow from that structure. First, early-season ratings are prior-dominated because no games means all \(1/2\) [PAGE 5], and the method has “no pre-season poll” [PAGE 3]. Second, schedule padding is a trap with no payoff: non-I-A games “contribute absolutely nothing to a team’s rating” [PAGE 7]. Third, a team can carry a strong record while its opponent graph is weak; the matrix adjusts through opponent ratings, but it cannot invent connectivity where none exists. The paper warns that there is “barely enough interconnectedness among the I-A teams themselves” [PAGE 7]. Fourth, human polls have a recency clock: the paper notes press voters are “more concerned about recent games than earlier ones” [PAGE 17], whereas Colley has no recency term. Fifth, convergence is itself a clock: the round-robin case is slowest, with errors shrinking by about \(n/(n+2)\), and the 2000 season required 60 iterations before median correction fell below \(10^{-7}\) [PAGE 11]. The multi-week trap is therefore not a single bad game; it is mistaking an intermediate iterate, or an early sparse graph, for the final simultaneous solution.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer separates three failure modes: crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock.

Crisis habit regression is the human-poll failure mode. Under sparse or conflicting evidence, rankers regress to habit: tradition, conference reputation, recent style, and margin impressions. Colley explicitly contrasts this with a method that has “no bias toward conference, tradition, history, or prognostication” [PAGE 17]. The paper also notes that press polls are “artificially correlated simply because the coaches are well aware of the AP poll, and the press writers are well aware of the Coaches’ poll” [PAGE 15]. That is a recursive belief loop: each poll models the other poll, and both model public opinion. The Colley operator has no such strategic belief state; it solves a fixed point from counts.

Counter-trap is the corrective discipline. The counter-trap is not to “fix” a single surprising result by adding margin, home/away, weather, or recency terms. The source’s counter-trap is exactly the opposite: “By focusing on wins and losses only” [PAGE 4], it eliminates “any other ad hoc adjustments, such as home/away tweaks” [PAGE 4]. This prevents overreaction to one game, but it also means the method cannot explain why a game looked the way it did. It only records that the game happened.

Clock has two meanings. The first is the season clock: before games, all ratings are \(1/2\) [PAGE 5]; as the schedule graph fills in, opponent ratings become informative. The second is the numerical clock: iterative convergence is an alternating, shrinking correction [PAGE 9], and the paper reports about 48 iterations in a typical sparse schedule and 60 in the 2000 season [PAGE 11]. A crisis habit regression would treat the first week’s rating as final; a counter-trap would wait for the simultaneous solution; the clock tells you when the fixed point is numerically stable. The recursive mind of the pollster asks “what will others think?”; the Colley matrix asks “what rating vector simultaneously satisfies every game count?” Those are different epistemic objects.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical, biomechanical, and weather veto question can be adjudicated by parameter dependence. Let the schedule be a game multigraph \(\mathcal G\), and let the Colley operators be \(C(\mathcal G)\) and \(\vec b(\mathcal G)\). The source equations depend only on \(n_{w,i}\), \(n_{\ell,i}\), \(n_{tot,i}\), and \(n_{j,i}\) [PAGE 10]. Therefore, for any cross-layer variable \(X\)—weather, surface condition, travel, biomechanical state, injury status, or officiating context—the partial derivative is
\[
\frac{\partial r_i}{\partial X}=0
\]
unless \(X\) changes a game outcome, participation, or cancellation that changes the counts in \(\mathcal G\). If \(X\) changes a win or loss, it enters only through \(n_{w,i}\) and \(n_{\ell,i}\). If \(X\) cancels a game, it enters through \(n_{tot,i}\) and the graph. If \(X\) affects a non-I-A game, it has no entry at all, because those games are ignored [PAGE 7].

This gives a clean veto rule. A claim that weather or biomechanics “causes” a Colley rating must exhibit a path through \(C\) or \(\vec b\). If the same win-loss multigraph is held fixed and weather changes, the Colley rating vector is invariant. If a biomechanical injury changes who won, the path is real but indirect: it changes \(n_w\) and \(n_\ell\), not an independent biomechanical coefficient. If weather causes a cancellation, the path is through \(n_{tot}\). If weather affects only style, margin, or score, the path is vetoed by the source’s own input restriction: “margin of victory does not matter” [PAGE 1]. The method also explicitly removes “home/away tweaks” [PAGE 4], so home-field weather interactions have no separate operator.

The proof is therefore an invariance proof, not a narrative. For fixed \(\mathcal G\), \(C\) and \(\vec b\) are fixed; because \(C\) is positive definite [PAGE 15], the solution \(\vec r=C^{-1}\vec b\) is unique. Any cross-layer variable that leaves \(\mathcal G\) unchanged cannot change \(\vec r\). A cross-layer variable can veto an interpretation of a single performance, but it cannot veto the ranking equation unless it alters the graph. The residual gray zone is confounding: if weather systematically changes outcomes and the schedule is unbalanced, the ranking can reflect that confounding through wins and losses. The source does not remove that confounding; it only refuses to add an ad hoc weather term.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Counterfactual: remove the Laplace constants and solve with straight winning percentage, setting \(c_{ii}=n_{tot,i}\) and \(c_{ij}=-n_{j,i}\). Prediction: every row and column sums to zero, so \(C\) is singular; there is no unique rating vector; Cholesky decomposition fails; the average-rating conservation proof no longer applies; the iterative scheme fails because the error factor becomes \(n/n=1\). This is verifiable by computing row sums and determinant on any nontrivial schedule. Veto: any claim that a raw-winning-percentage matrix yields unique Colley-type ratings. Source support: “if one removes the addend of 2 from cii, the total of the cij’s for any row or any column is zero... hence the matrix is singular” [PAGE 15], and without the \(+2\) “the convergence would not occur” [PAGE 11].

2. Counterfactual: add a non-I-A win to a team’s input record. Prediction: the source-consistent rating is invariant because non-I-A games are excluded; “padding the schedule with I-AA teams contributes absolutely nothing to a team’s rating” [PAGE 7]. If one instead inserts that game into \(n_w\), \(n_{tot}\), and the matrix, the rating vector can change, but the variant is no longer the declared Colley method. This is verifiable by duplicating a non-I-A game and checking that the declared method’s \(C\) and \(\vec b\) are unchanged. Veto: any claim that a non-I-A padding game improves a Colley rating.

3. Counterfactual: add a score-margin, home/away, weather, or biomechanical term to \(\vec b\) or \(C\). Prediction: the resulting system no longer solves the Colley equations; it breaks the source’s bias-free and reproducibility claims; and unless the added term is globally balanced, it can destroy the exact average \(1/2\) conservation. This is verifiable by holding the win-loss multigraph fixed while changing margin, venue, weather, or biomechanical annotation: the source rating vector must be identical, while the altered variant will generally differ. Veto: any claim that a difference in such a variant is attributable to the Colley Matrix method. Source support: “margin of victory does not matter” [PAGE 1] and the method “eliminates any other ad hoc adjustments, such as home/away tweaks” [PAGE 4].
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
