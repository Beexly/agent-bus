The paper’s scientific lineage runs from AR game design and markerless pose estimation to fitness and rehabilitation monitoring. Historically, this follows a familiar pattern: first, markerless motion capture and pose estimation improve; second, fitness applications reuse joint angles as cheap proxies for exercise form; third, AR overlays add game feedback; fourth, motivation is inferred from short pilot tests. The paper acknowledges the third step explicitly: AR “blurs the boundaries between the virtual and real world” and can make activity “more engaging and exciting” [PAGE 6]. The multi-week traps are therefore several. First is the novelty trap: a new AR game can produce a short-term motivation spike that decays as the novelty fades; the source’s test-group response is favorable [PAGE 5], but no duration or follow-up is given. Second is the habituation trap: users may learn the game’s thresholds and optimize the counter rather than the movement. Third is the calibration-drift trap: camera position, lighting, and clothing may change across sessions, while the source’s angle rules assume a stable frontal view [PAGE 4]. Fourth is the biomechanical-individualization trap: thresholds such as \(5^\circ\), \(30^\circ\), \(55^\circ\), \(90^\circ\), and \(105^\circ\) [PAGE 4–5] are not reported as person-specific; body proportions and range of motion can shift the measured angles. Fifth is the reproducibility trap: the biceps passage mixes “biceps” and “push-up” labels [PAGE 5], which makes exact replication difficult. These traps do not prove the system fails; they define what a multi-week study would have to control to make the motivational claim credible.

The retrocausal genealogy also explains why the paper’s strongest evidence is architectural rather than behavioral. The technical lineage supports the claim that pose landmarks can be extracted and that joint angles can be computed in real time. It does not automatically support the claim that AR feedback changes exercise habits. The paper’s own future-work section points toward improving “accuracy and robustness” and developing more accessible tools [PAGE 6–7], which is consistent with a prototype genealogy rather than a completed behavioral intervention.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind audit treats the user and the system as mutually modeling agents. The system models the user’s body as a set of landmarks and angle predicates. The user models the system’s detector as a set of thresholds and counters. The game score is not a neutral measurement; it is a social artifact that the user can infer and exploit. Formally, let \(A_t\) be the user’s action at time \(t\), \(Z_t\) the observed pose landmarks, \(S_t\) the game score or counter increment, and \(M_t\) the user’s motivation. The system’s scoring operator is
\[
S_t=\mathcal{C}\!\left(\phi(Z_{t-23:t})\right),
\]
where \(\phi\) is the angle-and-threshold feature map and \(\mathcal{C}\) is the counter logic described on [PAGE 4–5]. The user’s belief about the detector is
\[
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{t+1}
=
f\!\left(\hat{\mathcal{D}}_t,\,S_t,\,\text{feedback}_t\right),
\]
and the user selects an action according to
\[
A_t^{\ast}
=
\arg\max_{A_t}
\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\hat{S}_t(A_t)+\lambda M_t(A_t)
\;\middle|\;
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_t
\right].
\]
This is a legitimate recursive model, not a fake ratio. If \(\lambda\) is small, the user may optimize the counter rather than the physical target. That is the counter-trap. The source contains several exploitable discontinuities: a pull-up count increments when the elbow–wrist line crosses the nose point [PAGE 5]; a biceps flag is set by an angle less than \(30^\circ\) and the counter increments after a \(90^\circ\) return [PAGE 5]; an abdominal count increments when the angle moves from at least \(105^\circ\) to at most \(55^\circ\) [PAGE 5]. A user who learns these rules can produce partial or stylized movements that satisfy the threshold without full range of motion or target muscle loading.

The “crisis habit regression” scenario is the long-horizon failure mode. In week one, novelty and feedback may raise \(M_t\). In later weeks, if the game’s reward is repetitive and the user’s intrinsic motivation is not supported, \(M_t\) can regress toward baseline. The source reports favorable test-group response [PAGE 5] but does not report a retention curve or a crisis point. A recursive model predicts that if the user’s belief \(\hat{\mathcal{D}}_t\) becomes too accurate, the user may either exploit the counter or disengage because the game feels mechanical. If the belief is too inaccurate, the user may experience false negatives and lose trust. Both are failures of the user–system model, not merely failures of the pose estimator.

The “clock” layer is the temporal recursion. The system uses 24-frame windows [PAGE 4–5] and real-time feedback [PAGE 1, PAGE 5]. Let \(f_s\) be the frame rate in frames per second. The physical window duration is
\[
T_{24}=\frac{24}{f_s}.
\]
If \(f_s=30\) fps, \(T_{24}=0.8\) s; if \(f_s=60\) fps, \(T_{24}=0.4\) s. The source does not report \(f_s\), so the reader cannot know the physical duration of the decision window. This matters because a slow repetition may not produce the same 24-frame statistics as a fast repetition. The user can also adapt timing to the clock: pausing at a threshold, moving quickly through a detection window, or resetting between repetitions. The counter-trap and the clock therefore interact. A robust system would need to report frame rate, timestamp each landmark, and test counter invariance under speed changes. The source’s own admission that “the live mode of the application does not always precisely register users’ movements” [PAGE 6] is consistent with this clock-layer fragility.

The recursive theory-of-mind conclusion is that the paper’s motivational claim depends on a stable alignment between the user’s goal, the system’s detector, and the game’s reward. The provided pages show the detector and the reward, but they do not show a controlled analysis of user strategy, long-term habit, or clock sensitivity. The counter-trap is especially important because the paper’s evidence is partly the test group’s reported motivation [PAGE 1, PAGE 5]; if the counter can be satisfied without the intended physical load, then increased motivation to play the game is not identical to increased motivation for physical activity.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical-layer veto is explicit in the source. The paper states that the camera–subject angle affects the returned angles and that “the individual being examined has to look squarely into the lens of the camera” for reliable findings [PAGE 4]. It also states that estimated angles may be inexact if the subject is not facing the camera appropriately [PAGE 4]. I formalize the physical veto as
\[
V_{\mathrm{phys}}(t)=
\mathbf{1}\!\left[
\min_{k} c_k(t)\leq c_{\min}
\;\lor\;
|\psi(t)|\geq \psi_{\max}
\;\lor\;
\mathrm{occluded}(t)
\right],
\]
where \(c_k(t)\) is the landmark confidence or visibility, \(\psi(t)\) is the subject-to-camera yaw or obliquity, and \(\mathrm{occluded}(t)\) flags missing or self-occluded landmarks. If \(V_{\mathrm{phys}}=1\), the measured angle \(\hat{\theta}\) is not a reliable estimate of the biomechanical angle \(\theta\), and any exercise predicate can flip. The source’s own limitation—“the live mode of the application does not always precisely register users’ movements” [PAGE 6]—is a direct observed instance of this veto.

The lighting and camera-quality veto is also source-supported. The paper states: “One of the major challenges is the accuracy of the action detection algorithms. The accuracy of these algorithms depends on several factors, such as lighting conditions, the quality of the camera used, and the complexity of the movements being detected” [PAGE 6]. I formalize it as
\[
V_{\mathrm{sensor}}(t)=
\mathbf{1}\!\left[
\mathrm{illum}(t)\notin\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{calib}}
\;\lor\;
\mathrm{resolution}\leq r_{\min}
\;\lor\;
\mathrm{motion\ blur}(t)\geq b_{\max}
\right].
\]
This is not a hypothetical; it is the paper’s listed challenge. If \(V_{\mathrm{sensor}}=1\), the landmark estimator’s error can exceed the angular margins used by the thresholds, especially the \(5^\circ\) average-difference condition [PAGE 4] and the \(30^\circ\) biceps flag [PAGE 5]. The AR overlay may still run, but the counter may be measuring noise.

The biomechanical veto arises because the source’s thresholds are universal constants. It uses approximate \(90^\circ\) conditions for squat lines [PAGE 5], a \(105^\circ\) start and \(55^\circ\) increment for abdominal exercise [PAGE 5], and a \(30^\circ\)/\(90^\circ\) biceps sequence [PAGE 5]. A formal individualization veto is
\[
V_{\mathrm{bio}}=
\mathbf{1}\!\left[
\theta_{\mathrm{2D}}(a;\mathbf{p})
\notin
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{calib}}(a)
\right],
\]
where \(\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{calib}}(a)\) is the calibrated range for action \(a\) and body morphology. The source does not report calibration by limb length, joint range, or injury status. Therefore, the same threshold can be too strict for one user and too permissive for another. The squat normalization using head and foot heights [PAGE 5] partially addresses scale, but it does not address segment-length ratios or camera perspective.

The weather veto cannot be empirically adjudicated from the provided pages. The paper does not test rain, glare, heat, wind, or outdoor lighting transitions. Because the source itself says accuracy depends on lighting conditions [PAGE 6], the only defensible weather adjudication is a veto on overgeneralization: a claim that the AR game is reliable across outdoor weather conditions is not supported. Formally,
\[
V_{\mathrm{weather}}=
\mathbf{1}\!\left[
\mathrm{precipitation}\lor\mathrm{glare}\lor\mathrm{thermal\ distortion}
\right]
\]
would require a calibration dataset containing those conditions; the provided pages contain none. The source can support a claim about controlled or frontal indoor use; it cannot support an all-weather claim.

The cross-layer adjudication is therefore a conditional proof: the system can count some exercises when \(V_{\mathrm{phys}}=0\), \(V_{\mathrm{sensor}}=0\), \(V_{\mathrm{bio}}=0\), and the 24-frame clock is stable. If any veto fires, the action-detection result is not a valid measurement of the intended movement, and the motivational interpretation is weakened. This is the correct level of proof for the provided pages.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
