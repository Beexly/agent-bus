The model’s scientific lineage is a retrocausal chain of paired-comparison ideas. The source identifies the Bradley-Terry model as “the first detailed paired comparison model” [PAGE 4], then traces Elo-style ranking, Glickman-Stern state-space extensions, and later dynamic Bradley-Terry work. The present paper retrofits that lineage to a different outcome: instead of point differential, it uses market-implied win probabilities, which makes cross-league comparison possible because probabilities are dimensionless and bounded.

The historical traps are multi-week and multi-season. First, Noll-Scully inherits season length: “Noll-Scully increases, on average, with the number of games played” [PAGE 3], so a 16-game NFL season and a 162-game MLB season are not directly comparable by win-percentage dispersion. Second, unbalanced schedules mean “one team’s won-loss record may not be comparable to another team’s due to differences in the respective strengths of their opponents” [PAGE 3]. Third, home advantages differ by league and city, so win-loss comparisons confound team quality with venue [PAGE 4]. Fourth, market probabilities are efficient enough to be useful but not metaphysically exact; the source validates them with fit checks and then treats them as “true” [PAGE 8]. Fifth, cumulative state-space fits can leak future weeks into past estimates; the source contrasts cumulative and sequential fits [PAGE 11] and warns that using cumulative estimates to predict past games is unfair [PAGE 22]. Sixth, rounded prices create a discrete artifact: posterior predictive distributions are smoother than observed data because prices cluster on rounded values [PAGE 13]. Seventh, postseason structure is a later-designed layer that can reverse regular-season parity: the source notes that “MLB would have to switch to a 16-team tournament in which each round was approximately 75-game series” to approach the NBA’s lack of parity [PAGE 28]. The genealogy therefore runs from paired comparisons to dynamic state-space models to market-probability unification to tournament-format counterfactuals; each step adds a new identification assumption rather than merely adding data.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears in the model as autoregressive shrinkage: when a team is far from league average, the next-season and next-week transitions pull it back toward zero. The source reports that between-season reversion is largest in the NHL and that “team strengths in each of the leagues tend to revert towards the league average of zero” [PAGE 19]. In a crisis, the habituated statistical response is therefore regression to the mean, not persistence of the crisis. The counter-trap is that opponents, fans, and league designers anticipate that regression. If a rival expects a bad team to improve by draft position, it may overvalue the draft; if a league expects playoff randomness, it may lengthen series to reward regular-season strength. The source’s tanking discussion is a counter-trap example: bad NBA teams’ strength estimates “lie further from 0 than the estimates for good teams,” which “possibly reveals the tendency for teams in this league to ‘tank’” [PAGE 16]. The clock is the season and tournament horizon. The NFL’s short season magnifies single-game variance; MLB’s long season dilutes it; NBA and NHL series length changes the probability that the best seed survives. The source captures this trade-off: “a tournament in which the higher seeds always win is potentially less interesting, but a tournament in which seeds don’t matter might compromise the competitiveness of late-season games” [PAGE 28]. Recursive theory of mind therefore predicts three behaviors: crisis teams regress toward league average unless incentives reward losing; opponents who know this may counter-adjust too far; and commissioners choose clocks—season length, playoff field size, series length—to steer the tension between parity and meritocracy.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical/biomechanical/weather veto proof proceeds by omitted-variable algebra. Suppose the true logit is
\[
\operatorname{logit}(p)=\theta_i-\theta_j+\alpha_{q0}+\alpha_{q,i^\star}+\beta C_{game}+\varepsilon_{game},
\]
but the fitted model omits \(C\). If \(C\) is constant within a home city, then the fitted \(\hat\alpha_{q,i^\star}\) estimates \(\alpha_{q,i^\star}+\beta C_{i^\star}\), so altitude, climate, or stadium physics are perfectly collinear with the city intercept. The source’s Denver result is exactly this gray zone: “teams playing at home in Denver have the largest home advantages in MLB, the NBA, and the NFL, and the 7th-highest in the NHL” [PAGE 20], but the model has no altitude covariate. Without cross-city variation or external physical measurements, the altitude effect is not identified. If \(C\) varies game-to-game but is independent of team and venue, it inflates \(\sigma^2_{q,game}\) without necessarily biasing \(\theta\); if it correlates with home/away or with team scheduling, it biases both \(\theta\) and \(\alpha\). Biomechanical vetoes enter through player availability: injuries, rest, and in-season trades are time-varying, team-specific, and correlated with \(\theta\) evolution. The source admits that “our model cannot precisely gauge team strength when star players who could play are rested in favor of inferior players” [PAGE 18]. MLB pitching rotations are another biomechanical/skill-layer veto: the source attributes extra MLB game-level error to “the league’s pitching match-ups, in which teams rotate through a handful of starting pitchers of varying calibers” [PAGE 18]. Weather is not in the equations at all; the source only offers an anecdotal NFL day/night conjecture [PAGE 20]. Therefore the cross-layer adjudication is: the statistical layer may estimate a home intercept, but the physical layer vetoes any causal claim about altitude, weather, fatigue, or injury unless the physical variable is measured in SI units, enters the design matrix with independent variation, and is checked for collinearity with city/team/time. Otherwise the physical effect is absorbed into \(\alpha\), \(\sigma_{game}\), or \(\gamma\), and the posterior is descriptive, not adjudicative.
