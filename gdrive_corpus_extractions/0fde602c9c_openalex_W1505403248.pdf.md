<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Crisis habit regression: As channel degradation increases, the decoder regresses from the computationally expensive triple-estimation strategy (2.33), which jointly identifies the transmitted word index j, adjacent word index k, and slip s [PAGE 24], to the joint/double strategy (2.34) that estimates only slip and the dominant word [PAGE 24–25]. Corollary 2.1 [PAGE 27] formalizes this regression: performance degrades but correction ability is retained under a stricter distance bound. The same regression appears in Theorem 3.3 versus Theorem 3.4 [PAGE 48–50], where the iterative shell-search decoder of 3.3 is replaced by a lower-complexity remainder-term decoder in 3.4.

Counter-trap: Adversarial error patterns exploit decoder assumptions. On [PAGE 24], the report notes that distinct slip values can produce colliding syndromes because error vectors and adjacent-word fragments contribute to the syndrome; ambiguity is resolved only by selecting the minimum-weight additive error pattern [PAGE 24]. For Reed-Solomon codes, the [PAGE 38] footnote provides a concrete counter-trap: an (63,9) code over GF(64) with slip T=7 and adjacent zero vectors yields zero weight, violating the assumed detection boundary when K does not divide N. Thus a slip pattern aligned with code periodicity can masquerade as a valid codeword.

Clock: Word synchronization must be acquired in sequence—carrier, then symbol, then word [PAGE 9]. Timing jitter or clock inaccuracy at any level propagates to word synchronization [PAGE 9]. Once lost, the receiver must re-establish synchronization, with word synchronization last [PAGE 9]. The decoder’s reframing loop [PAGE 22–23] is the clock-recovery mechanism: it shifts the received vector and recomputes distance until the error metric drops below the design threshold e. If the physical clock cannot hold lower synchronization levels, the code’s distance guarantees are void because the error is no longer a bounded substitution within a fixed frame [PAGE 11].

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof.

Physical layer veto: [PAGE 9–10] attributes loss of word synchronization to timing inaccuracies, jitter in clocking circuitry, and receiver start-up sequence constraints. Fading and multipath are cited as physical channel phenomena causing insertion or deletion of symbols, net effect equivalent to persistent misframing or slip [PAGE 10]. These veto the foundational assumption on [PAGE 11] that the channel introduces only substitution errors. If the physical layer produces insertions/deletions outside the substitution model, the Hamming-distance protection framework does not apply.

Biomechanical/hardware veto: Cyclic codes are practically implemented with linear feedback shift registers [PAGE 9]. The iterative reframing decoder required by Theorem 2.3 and Theorem 3.3 may have prohibitively large processing time [PAGE 23]. Hardware real-time constraints therefore veto the full-iteration strategy; the reduced-complexity syndrome decoder of Corollary 2.1 [PAGE 27] or the fixed-pattern subset decoder of Theorem 3.5 [PAGE 62] becomes mandatory under latency limits.

Weather/environmental veto: Atmospheric fading and multipath [PAGE 10] are the environmental mechanisms that induce burst symbol loss and slip. Because the theory assumes a substitution-error channel [PAGE 11], deep fades causing erasures or long insertion/deletion bursts fall outside the proven protection envelope. The code’s distance structure is invalidated when the error process is not memoryless substitution within a frame.

Veto proof summary: The code guarantees hold only if (i) carrier/symbol synchronization are already locked [PAGE 9], (ii) the channel introduces substitution errors only [PAGE 11], and (iii) decoder storage permits the reframing iterations [PAGE 23]. Any physical-layer violation of these preconditions voids the algebraic distance bounds regardless of code construction.
