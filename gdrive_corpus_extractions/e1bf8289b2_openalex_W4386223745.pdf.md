<THINK_TRACE>
The drive source is a 2023 Scientific Reports paper by Baumgartner, Paassen, and Klatt on extracting scene geometry from track-and-field broadcasts to test monocular 3D human pose estimation. The central multi-hop argument is not “can a neural network estimate a 3D skeleton?” but “does the 3D skeleton that a network lifts from a 2D skeleton remain consistent with the true camera and scene geometry when projected back into the image?” The paper formalizes this using the projection chain: actual 3D skeleton (a), image (b), 2D skeleton (c), estimated 3D skeleton (d), actual geometry (e), and implied geometry (f). Starting from a 2D pose (c), a monocular 3D HPE method produces (d), which necessarily implies some unknown geometry (f). The authors recover the true geometry (e) from lane markings and a vanishing point, then compare \(\pi_e(d)\) with (c). A nonzero discrepancy proves that (d) is not merely a global translation, scale, or rotation of (a); it is inconsistent with the actual pose and/or the actual scene geometry. The paper’s own summary is explicit: “we show that current state-of-the-art 3D human pose estimation methods are not (yet) accurate enough to be used in kinematics research” [PAGE 1].

The derivation has several gray zones that must be audited. First, the recovered geometry is a pinhole-camera approximation, not the physical multi-lens broadcast camera. The authors acknowledge this and validate against an Xsens motion-capture suit: the predicted camera location is “within 5.5% of the correct camera position” with average offsets of 1.75 m, 2.67 m, and 0.72 m in x/y/z, at an average athlete distance of 37.56 m [PAGE 8]. That is a real physical-layer uncertainty, but it is bounded. Second, the error metric is a 2D reprojection error, not direct 3D MPJPE, because no ground-truth 3D exists for the broadcast data. The authors convert pixel error to approximate millimetres using the known lane width and athlete scale, and they approximate 3D knee error by perturbing the predicted knee and measuring the induced 2D change [PAGE 7]. This approximation is validated in the 50-frame Xsens study: the 2D HPE projection RMSE is \(7.56 \pm 3.75\) px, equal to \(50.42 \pm 28.51\) mm, and the knee-angle errors are \(8.39 \pm 4.41^\circ\) left and \(7.94 \pm 5.84^\circ\) right [PAGE 8]. These numbers are consistent with the approximated table error, so the approximation is not arbitrary.

Third, the result is a threshold veto, not a claim that every frame is useless. The literature cited by the paper requires “high angular accuracy (below 3° error)” for running kinematics [PAGE 3], and reports 2–3° treadmill/outdoor knee-angle differences and 2–4° warm-up/transition differences [PAGE 3]. Against that bio-mechanical requirement, the best approximated 3D knee error in Table 1 is \(8.45 \pm 13.19^\circ\) for MeTRAbs plus rotation knowledge [PAGE 7–8]. The paper concludes: “This margin of error is larger than the levels of change for significant differences in running kinematics and implied running economy ... rendering the current state-of-the-art methods infeasible for the collection of data towards kinematic investigations” [PAGE 8]. The gray-zone audit must therefore distinguish “insufficient for small-angle running kinematics” from “insufficient for every possible pose task.” The authors themselves limit the claim to kinematic research and to the straight sections of track-and-field stadiums [PAGE 9].

The experimental design also contains a useful adversarial control. If lens distortion or camera-location error were the true cause of the reprojection discrepancy, then optimizing those parameters should remove much of the error. The authors report that adversarial experiments improve reprojection error by only “0.36±0.48 pixels (lens distortion) and 0.66±0.48 pixels (camera location)” [PAGE 9]. That is small relative to the observed errors and supports the stronger inference: the monocular 3D HPE method’s implied geometry is inconsistent with the scene, not merely offset by a correctable calibration nuisance. The remaining uncertainty is the pinhole assumption and the manual selection of geometry, but the ground-truth validation bounds both. Thus the source supports a veto on using current monocular 3D HPE from broadcast footage for sub-3° running kinematics, while leaving open future work that injects scene geometry directly into the estimator.

\mathbf{v}_0 \sim K R \mathbf{d}_{\parallel},

where \(\mathbf{d}_{\parallel}\) is the unit world direction of the parallel lane markings and \(\sim\) denotes equality up to homogeneous scale; \(\mathbf{v}_0\) is in homogeneous pixel coordinates. \[
E_{\mathrm{reproj}}
=
\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}
\left\|
\pi_{K,R,t}\!\left(\hat{\mathbf{X}}_j\right)
-
\mathbf{x}_{j}
\right\|_2,
\]
where \(\hat{\mathbf{X}}_j\) is the estimated 3D joint in metres, \(\mathbf{x}_j\) is the corresponding 2D joint in pixels, \(\pi_{K,R,t}\) is the projection operator above, and \(J=17\) major joints are used [PAGE 7]. The paper’s scientific lineage runs from classical line detection to modern 2D/3D human pose estimation and sports-field registration. The Hough transform is cited as the classical line-segment detector [PAGE 10, ref. 38], and the authors use a “default OpenCV implementation” for line extraction [PAGE 6]. Human3.6M is the marker-based 3D pose benchmark that supplies the training convention for 3D HPE and the 17-joint definition [PAGE 2, PAGE 7, ref. 16]. The 2D HPE backbone is a ResNet-50 trained on MPII [PAGE 6, refs. 12, 21]. MeTRAbs is the state-of-the-art monocular 3D HPE method trained additionally on external data and designed for “3D HPE in the wild” [PAGE 7, ref. 21]. Sports camera calibration has a separate lineage: court/field-template matching, keypoint detection, homography estimation, and broadcast-video calibration [PAGE 3, refs. 24–27]. The paper’s contribution is retrocausal in the sense that it recovers the scene geometry that earlier monocular 3D HPE methods implicitly discarded, then uses that recovered geometry to audit those methods after the fact.

The multi-week research traps are clear. Trap one: collect more broadcast frames. More frames increase sample size but do not remove systematic geometry inconsistency; the paper’s 355 frames over 16 athletes and five venues already show the error, and the authors explicitly say current methods “do not generalize towards this data” [PAGE 2]. Trap two: add external training data. MeTRAbs is trained on additional data and still has \(13.38\pm31.15^\circ\) approximate 3D knee error, improving only to \(8.45\pm13.19^\circ\) with injected rotation knowledge [PAGE 7]. Trap three: assume lens distortion explains the gap. The adversarial experiments improve reprojection by only \(0.36\pm0.48\) px for lens distortion and \(0.66\pm0.48\) px for camera location [PAGE 9], so distortion is not a sufficient explanation. Trap four: rely on 2D annotation alone. The paper states that 2D HPE is easy to annotate, but 3D requires “additional information beyond a 2D pixel location” [PAGE 9]. Trap five: extend template/homography methods directly from ball sports. In this track scenario the visible lines are parallel and co-linear, so “a homography cannot be computed” [PAGE 3]. Trap six: assume the method generalizes to curves or all stadium viewpoints. The authors limit it to the straight sections and propose future work using higher-level vision or neural radiance fields [PAGE 9, ref. 45].

The historical lesson is that laboratory 3D motion capture and in-the-wild 2D pose annotation solved different problems. Human3.6M provides metric 3D from markers in a lab [PAGE 2], while broadcast footage provides only a 2D projection with unknown camera motion and zoom. The paper’s recovered geometry is a bridge, but it is a narrow bridge: it requires standardized lane markings, a straight home stretch, a fixed broadcast camera, and manual or semi-automatic geometry selection. The retrocausal audit therefore does not say “3D HPE is useless”; it says the field’s evaluation habit—reporting 2D/3D joint errors on lab-like benchmarks—does not transfer to sub-3° running kinematics without explicit scene geometry.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer concerns how authors, reviewers, and downstream practitioners anticipate objections. The authors know a reviewer may respond: “Your geometry is approximate, so your error is a calibration artifact.” They pre-empt this with the Xsens validation: camera location within 5.5%, 2D HPE projection RMSE \(7.56\pm3.75\) px, and knee errors \(8.39\pm4.41^\circ\) and \(7.94\pm5.84^\circ\) [PAGE 8]. They also know a reviewer may respond: “Just improve the model with more data.” They pre-empt this by testing MeTRAbs with external training data and by injecting movement and rotation knowledge, showing the best approximate 3D knee error remains \(8.45\pm13.19^\circ\) [PAGE 7–8]. They further know a reviewer may blame lens distortion, so they run 200 adversarial optimizations and report improvements of only \(0.36\pm0.48\) px and \(0.66\pm0.48\) px [PAGE 9].

The “crisis habit regression” is the tendency, when a validation fails, to retreat to familiar habits: collect more data, smooth the time series, add a larger backbone, or report average pixel error without checking scene consistency. The source blocks that regression by making scene geometry the test operator. The counter-trap is to inject geometry into the estimator rather than use geometry only as a post hoc correction; the authors argue that a track-specific monocular 3D HPE method “should, therefore, incorporate this 3D scene information, instead of just using it to correct its output” [PAGE 8]. The “clock” is the finite annotation and validation budget: 355 frames, 16 athletes, five venues, and a 50-frame ground-truth study [PAGE 6–8]. The authors use a 0.5° azimuth sampling sweep and a slider-based interface so a domain expert can adjust geometry quickly [PAGE 9]. This is a clock-aware compromise: exhaustive geometry search is expensive, so they build a dense discrete set and interpolate.

The recursive prediction is that practitioners will split into two camps. Camp one treats the paper as a general indictment of monocular 3D HPE; that overreads the source. Camp two treats the paper as a calibration paper and continues collecting kinematic data from broadcasts unchanged; that underreads the source. The source’s own stance is narrower and stronger: current methods are not accurate enough for kinematic research in this domain, but the geometry-extraction method can support future annotation and domain-specific model design [PAGE 8–9]. The correct counter-trap is not “reject all video” but “make geometry a first-class variable in training, evaluation, and annotation.”
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer veto is bounded but real. The method assumes a pinhole camera, no digital zoom, fixed camera location, horizontal main vanishing points, and perfect 2D HPE [PAGE 5]. Real broadcast cameras have multiple lenses and distortion; the authors state that their predicted camera location is offset by an average of 5.5% and that the pinhole model ignores “more minute details of lens distortions” [PAGE 9]. However, the ground-truth study shows the geometry approximation is close enough to detect the pose error: the camera offsets are 1.75 m / 2.67 m / 0.72 m in x/y/z, and the 2D RMSE is 7.56 px [PAGE 8]. The adversarial optimizations improve reprojection by less than 1 px [PAGE 9], so the physical layer does not veto the paper’s conclusion; it bounds the uncertainty.

The biomechanical layer veto is decisive. The literature requires angular accuracy below 3° for running kinematics, with 2–3° and 2–4° effects reported as meaningful [PAGE 3]. The paper’s best approximated 3D knee error is \(8.45\pm13.19^\circ\) [PAGE 8], and even the ground-truth validation gives overall 3D angle error \(11.00\pm5.93^\circ\) [PAGE 8]. This exceeds the biomechanical tolerance. The source’s conclusion is therefore a biomechanical veto: “rendering the current state-of-the-art methods infeasible for the collection of data towards kinematic investigations” [PAGE 8]. It is not merely that the pixels are noisy; the angular error is larger than the physiological differences the research aims to detect.

The weather/environmental layer has no affirmative evidence in the source. The paper does not report rain, wind, temperature, lighting, or surface conditions as variables, and it does not model weather effects on Hough line detection or camera calibration. A weather-based veto therefore cannot be adjudicated from this source; any claim that weather caused the observed errors would be unsupported. The only environmental statement is that the analyzed shots use tele-zoom cameras and that lens distortion is assumed small for that footage [PAGE 9]. Cross-layer adjudication therefore yields: physical uncertainty is bounded and non-explanatory; biomechanical error is above threshold and vetoes kinematic use; weather is null-evidence and cannot be used either to rescue or to condemn the method. The correct proof obligation is a joint one—geometry must be correct enough and angular error must be below the biomechanical threshold—and the source shows the second condition fails even when the first is approximately satisfied.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Pass condition: if the upper bound remains above \(3^\circ\), the kinematic-use veto stands.

Scenario 2: Lens-distortion and camera-location adversarial optimization. Intervention: optimize lens distortion and camera location within the reported 5.5% radius to minimize reprojection error, using the same 200-optimization protocol. Verification: measure the change in reprojection error and the resulting approximate 3D knee error. Prediction: reprojection improves by only \(0.36\pm0.48\) px for lens distortion and \(0.66\pm0.48\) px for camera location, and the approximate 3D knee error remains far above \(3^\circ\) [PAGE 9]. Veto condition: if adversarial optimization reduces the 3D knee error below \(3^\circ\) on a held-out athlete set, the paper’s claim that model limitations are “not severe enough to explain the observed effect” is false for that set [PAGE 9]. Pass condition: if the improvement is sub-pixel and the angular error remains above threshold, the geometry-inconsistency explanation is verified.

Scenario 3: Straight-path and rotation-knowledge injection. Intervention: enforce the known domain constraint that athletes run in a straight line down the home stretch and rotate the estimated 3D skeleton to face the finishing line using the recovered camera-relative orientation. These three scenarios are verifiable because each specifies an intervention, a measured unit (pixels, millimetres, or degrees), and a pre-registered threshold tied to the source’s own biomechanical criterion [PAGE 3, PAGE 8].
