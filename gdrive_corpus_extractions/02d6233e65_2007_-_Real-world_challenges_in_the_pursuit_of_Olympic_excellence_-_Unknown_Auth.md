First, the vertical jump identity. From [PAGE 12], the slide names “Loss Height,” “Takeoff Height,” “Flight Height,” “Reach Height,” and “Jump and Reach Height” under “Deterministic model of vertical jumping” [PAGE 12]. Let \(H_t\) be touch height above a fixed reference in metres, \(H_{to}\) takeoff height in metres, \(H_f\) flight height in metres, \(H_r\) reach height in metres, and \(H_\ell\) loss height in metres. \begin{equation}
H_t = H_{to} + H_f + H_r - H_\ell .
\end{equation}

The normalized share for component \(k\) is

\begin{equation}
p_k = 100\,\frac{H_k}{H_t},
\end{equation}

where \(p_k\) is dimensionless percent and \(H_k\) is in metres. This immediately gives a singularity: if \(H_t \to 0\), the percentages are undefined. \begin{equation}

\end{equation}

where \(v_{z0}\) is vertical takeoff velocity in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(g\) is in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), and \(D_{\mathrm{air}} \geq 0\) is the air-resistance loss in metres. \end{equation}

\beta = \Sigma^{-1}\Sigma_{XY},
\end{equation}

with units of \(Y\) per unit of \(X\). The exact singularity is \(\det \Sigma = 0\); near-singularity gives unstable coefficients even when \(\det \Sigma \neq 0\). The model breaks down under nonlinearity, range restriction, selection effects, or omitted confounders.
