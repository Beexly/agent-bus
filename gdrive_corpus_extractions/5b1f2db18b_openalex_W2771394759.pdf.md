The source is a retrospective single-centre study of professional male footballers in the Polish Premier League, with 340 isokinetic test reports from both limbs of 66 players and 12 hamstring injuries in 11 players. The first multi-hop audit is therefore to separate the unit of analysis from the unit of inference. The paper’s denominators are limb-tests nested inside players, seasons, and repeated testing occasions, while the clinical event is a player-level hamstring strain within 12 months. That nesting makes naive independence assumptions fragile: 340 limb-level observations do not equal 340 independent injury-prone athletes. The paper itself reports “Eleven players (16.67%) suffered hamstring injuries during the analysed period” and “One player sustained hamstring strain injury on both legs, thus the total number of injuries was 12” [PAGE 9]. This immediately creates a clustering and rare-event problem for any threshold comparison.

The second hop is reconstruction of the contingency tables. The actual-positive count is 12 and the actual-negative count is 328, so prevalence is \(12/340=0.03529\) or 3.53%. At cut-off 0.47 the table gives \(TP=2, FP=18, FN=10, TN=310\), giving sensitivity \(16.7\%\) and specificity \(94.5\%\). At 0.60 the table gives \(TP=6, FP=174, FN=6, TN=154\), giving sensitivity \(50.0\%\) and specificity \(46.9\%\). At 0.658 the table gives \(TP=11, FP=249, FN=1, TN=79\), giving sensitivity \(91.7\%\) and specificity \(24.1\%\) [PAGE 10]. These numbers show a pure threshold trade-off, not a monotonic gain in diagnostic truth. Moving from 0.47 to 0.658 raises true positives by 9 but raises false positives by 231. That is why the paper’s main finding is defensible: “application of different cut-off values for Hcon/Qcon significantly affects the sensitivity and specificity of isokinetic test used as a tool for muscle injury detection” [PAGE 11]. But the clinical interpretation must not jump from “threshold changes sensitivity and specificity” to “0.658 is the better injury predictor.”

The third hop is the ROC and chance baseline. The paper states that the ROC curve “was basically a 45-degree line running from the intersection of the axes, indicating that Hcon/Qcon ratio was no better than chance alone for predicting hamstring injury (area under curve 0.537)” [PAGE 10]. An AUC of 0.537 is extremely close to 0.5. Picking 0.658 because it maximizes sensitivity on this sample is therefore a high-variance, low-discrimination choice. It produces a high-sensitivity rule only by labelling 260 of 340 limb-tests as positive, of which 249 are false positives. The paper itself warns: “applying 0.658 value results in almost 75% of incorrectly identified players as test positive (false positive), which makes the usefulness of isokinetic evaluation in hamstring injury prediction very questionable” [PAGE 11]. That is the core gray-zone audit: the threshold is statistically visible but clinically unstable.

The fourth hop is a numerical audit. Using the paper’s displayed \(Se\) and \(Sp\), the standard likelihood-ratio identities imply \(LR^+ = Se/(1-Sp)\) and \(LR^- = (1-Se)/Sp\). At 0.47, \(LR^- = (1-0.167)/0.945 \approx 0.881\), yet Table 3 reports 1.67 [PAGE 10]. That is an internal inconsistency worth flagging. The displayed sensitivity interval at 0.47 is also written as “(2.1–4.8)” in the table [PAGE 10], whereas an exact binomial interval for 2/12 is far wider, roughly 2.1% to 48.4%. These are not reasons to dismiss the paper’s central caution; they are reasons to treat exact numeric precision as provisional. The paper also says McNemar’s chi-square with Yates’s correction was used [PAGE 9], while Table 4 reports \(t\) statistics [PAGE 11]. The method-statistic mismatch is another audit flag, not proof of error.

The final hop is causal adjudication. It also says “During the analysed period a low number of hamstring strain injuries occurred, and results from this study should be verified on a bigger sample with longer hamstring injury observation to identify significant interrelations” [PAGE 14]. Because exposure time is absent—“Since we have no data regarding the exposure time, we have not calculated the injuries as number injuries / 1000h of training/competitions” [PAGE 14]—no rate-normalized causal claim can be rescued from the table. The correct verdict is: the paper convincingly vetoes single-normative-cut-off certainty, but it does not establish a transportable injury-prediction threshold.

Let \(i\) index a limb-test occasion. Define actual injury within the 12-month follow-up as \(Y_i \in \{0,1\}\), where \(Y_i=1\) means a hamstring strain confirmed by ultrasound after the isokinetic evaluation, consistent with the paper’s injury definition and follow-up statement [PAGE 8]. Define the conventional concentric ratio at \(60^\circ/\mathrm{s}\) as
\[
r_i=\frac{\tau_{H,\mathrm{con},i}(60^\circ/\mathrm{s})}{\tau_{Q,\mathrm{con},i}(60^\circ/\mathrm{s})},
\]
where \(\tau\) is peak torque in \(\mathrm{N\cdot m}\). The ratio \(r_i\) is dimensionless: \(\mathrm{N\cdot m}/\mathrm{N\cdot m}=1\). The angular velocity \(60^\circ/\mathrm{s}\) equals \(\pi/3\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\approx 1.047\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\). The source says “Three repetitions of knee concentric flexion and concentric extension movement were performed through a knee range of motion (ROM) of 0˚ (flexed) to 90˚ (full extension) at the 60˚ s-1 angular velocity” [PAGE 7].

For a cut-off \(c\in\{0.47,0.60,0.658\}\), define the binary test
\[
T_i^c=\mathbf{1}\{r_i \lt c\}.
\]
The paper’s 2×2 table therefore uses \(TP_c=\sum_i Y_iT_i^c\), \(FP_c=\sum_i(1-Y_i)T_i^c\), \(FN_c=\sum_iY_i(1-T_i^c)\), and \(TN_c=\sum_i(1-Y_i)(1-T_i^c)\) [PAGE 10]. The diagnostic operators are
\[
Se_c=\Pr(T^c=1\mid Y=1)=\frac{TP_c}{TP_c+FN_c},
\]
\[
Sp_c=\Pr(T^c=0\mid Y=0)=\frac{TN_c}{TN_c+FP_c},
\]
\[
PPV_c=\Pr(Y=1\mid T^c=1)=\frac{TP_c}{TP_c+FP_c},
\]
\[
NPV_c=\Pr(Y=0\mid T^c=0)=\frac{TN_c}{TN_c+FN_c},
\]
\[
Acc_c=\frac{TP_c+TN_c}{TP_c+FP_c+FN_c+TN_c},
\]
\[
LR^+_c=\frac{Se_c}{1-Sp_c},\qquad LR^-_c=\frac{1-Se_c}{Sp_c}.
\]
All sensitivity, specificity, predictive-value, and accuracy quantities are probabilities in \([0,1]\); they are reported as percentages on [PAGE 10]. The likelihood ratios are dimensionless probability ratios. If one wants an information summary, the empirical mutual information is
\[
I(Y;T^c)=\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}}\sum_{t\in\{0,1\}}
\Pr(Y=y,T^c=t)\log_2\frac{\Pr(Y=y,T^c=t)}{\Pr(Y=y)\Pr(T^c=t)}
\]
in bits; multiplying by \(\ln 2\) converts to nats. This quantity is not reported by the paper and would require clustering-aware estimation.

The paper’s ROC operators are the Youden index and the upper-left distance:
\[
J_c=Se_c+Sp_c-1,
\]
\[
d_c=\sqrt{(1-Se_c)^2+(1-Sp_c)^2}.
\]
The source says ROC analysis identified 0.658, but also that the curve “was basically a 45-degree line running from the intersection of the axes, indicating that Hcon/Qcon ratio was no better than chance alone for predicting hamstring injury (area under curve 0.537)” [PAGE 10]. Thus the empirical AUC operator is \(0.537\), only \(0.037\) above the chance value \(0.5\). That is the central breakdown regime: the ranking signal is near-null, so threshold selection is dominated by sample noise and prevalence.

Exact singularity and breakdown regimes:
1. If \(TP_c+FN_c=0\), then \(Se_c\) is undefined because there are no actual injured cases. If \(TN_c+FP_c=0\), then \(Sp_c\) is undefined. If \(TP_c+FP_c=0\), then \(PPV_c\) is undefined; if \(TN_c+FN_c=0\), then \(NPV_c\) is undefined.
2. If \(Sp_c=1\), then \(LR^+_c\) diverges; if \(Sp_c=0\), then \(LR^-_c\) diverges. If \(Se_c=1\), then \(LR^-_c=0\). These are boundary regimes, not stable clinical estimates.
3. If prevalence \(p=\Pr(Y=1)\to 0\), then \(PPV_c\) becomes unstable even when \(Se_c\) and \(Sp_c\) are fixed; the paper’s \(p=12/340=0.03529\) is low enough that false positives dominate the positive-label group. At 0.658, \(PPV=4.3\%\) despite \(Se=91.7\%\) [PAGE 10].
4. If \(c\) is below the minimum observed ratio, all tests become positive: \(FP+TP=N\), \(Sp=0\), and the rule is clinically useless. If \(c\) exceeds the maximum observed ratio, all tests become negative: \(TP=0\), \(Se=0\). The observed thresholds already approach this boundary: at 0.47 only 20 of 340 tests are positive; at 0.658, 260 of 340 are positive [PAGE 10].
5. If the AUC is near 0.5, any “optimal” cut-off selected by Youden or upper-left distance has high variance. The paper’s own statement that “no useful decision threshold could be identified in this study” [PAGE 10] is the correct operator-level conclusion.

Audit note: Table 3 reports \(LR^-=1.67\) at 0.47, but the displayed \(Se=16.7\%\) and \(Sp=94.5\%\) give \((1-Se)/Sp\approx0.881\) [PAGE 10]. The displayed sensitivity interval “(2.1–4.8)” at 0.47 also appears truncated relative to an exact binomial interval for 2/12. These numerical flags require source-data verification before any fine-grained precision claim.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tracing the cut-offs backward exposes a lineage problem. The paper states: “It was Klein and Allman in 1969 who first proposed a concentric hamstring-to-quadriceps ratio of 0.60, as reported by several authors [15–17]” [PAGE 3]. That 0.60 then travelled into football screening through later studies, including Dauty et al., even though the paper says the earlier supporting studies were “performed on non-soccer populations” [PAGE 3]. The 0.47 lineage is even more fragile. The paper says “Tracing its history backwards we learn that Dauty et al. [9]decided to use conventional ratio fixed at 0.47 on 136 footballers in reference to the study of Croisier et al. [7] published in 2008” [PAGE 3], and then that the 2002 Croisier study observed “only 26 athletes with a history of hamstring muscle injury and recurrent strains and discomfort” and “only 14 of them were soccer players” [PAGE 4]. The deepest ancestor is a 1999 reliability study: “In 1999 Croisier et al. performed a reliability study on the group of sedentary people and physical education students [20]” [PAGE 4], and “Unfortunately, there was no follow-up registration of muscle injury applied in this study [20]” [PAGE 4]. The paper’s verdict is unambiguous: “it seems questionable to use ‘the normative’ value of Hcon/Qcon which was adapted from non-footballers with no muscle strain follow up to verify the risk of hamstring muscle injury in professional soccer players based on isokinetic assessment” [PAGE 4].

This genealogy creates multi-week and multi-season traps. First, the test is performed in preseasons and mid-season windows: the paper says evaluation was “usually performed twice a year, during the preseason period from January to February, and from June to July” [PAGE 7]. A 12-month injury window after each test can overlap with another test window, so the same biological event can be temporally adjacent to more than one screening occasion. Second, the Polish league has a winter break, and the paper explicitly warns that “Since there are season structure differences between Poland (likewise few other leagues in Central Europe, e.g. Bulgaria, Czech Republic, Slovakia) and other European countries like England, Italy, France, Spain or Qatar, the results from our study should be applied to the European leagues with caution” [PAGE 13]. Third, players transfer or retire: the paper excluded records where “the period of observation of 6 players was too short (less than 12 months) due to a transfer to another team or career termination” [PAGE 9]. That is a selection trap: the healthiest or most stable players may be overrepresented in the analysed denominator. Fourth, the study includes only ultrasound-confirmed injuries, so “It is possible that some muscle strain might have been missed” [PAGE 14]. The historical lesson is not that 0.47 or 0.60 is numerically wrong in every population; it is that a cut-off with non-football ancestry and no injury follow-up cannot be treated as a universal football law.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis habit regression here is the repeated fallback to one “normative” number when a clinician, coach, or player wants a yes/no answer under uncertainty. The paper names the habit directly: “it might not be an appropriate approach to use one ‘normative’ value as a cut-off (e.g. 0.47 or 0.60, or any other calculated from ROC analysis) to qualify a football player to a group with higher risk of knee injury” [PAGE 12]. Under time pressure, the mind prefers a single threshold because it converts a continuous, noisy biomechanical signal into a binary label. That regression is understandable but unsafe: the source shows that the same test yields \(Se=16.7\%\) at 0.47 and \(Se=91.7\%\) at 0.658, while specificity falls from \(94.5\%\) to \(24.1\%\) [PAGE 10]. The label “at risk” therefore depends more on the chosen number than on a stable biological property.

The counter-trap is the opposite overcorrection: seeing that 0.658 catches 11 of 12 injuries, a decision-maker may conclude that 0.658 is the “safe” cut-off. But the paper’s own warning is that “applying 0.658 value results in almost 75% of incorrectly identified players as test positive (false positive)” [PAGE 11]. The recursive problem is that the clinician knows the coach wants sensitivity, the coach knows the clinician can generate a label, and the player knows that the label may affect training or selection. A high-sensitivity rule can thus create a cascade of false alarms, extra rest, altered training, and anxiety. A low-sensitivity rule such as 0.47 creates the opposite cascade: most injured players are labelled not-at-risk, producing false reassurance. Neither cascade is resolved by pretending the cut-off is objective.

The clock is the 12-month follow-up and the twice-yearly testing calendar. The paper counts injuries “within the 12-month period after the isokinetic evaluation” [PAGE 8], and the testing schedule is January–February and June–July [PAGE 7]. A player’s injury may occur weeks or months after the test, after changes in load, fixture congestion, travel, or recovery. The recursive theory-of-mind failure is to treat the test as a timeless snapshot rather than a time-stamped measurement. The paper’s proposed countermeasure is a range: “Extending the threshold in the current study by about 10% (from 0.6 –to 0.658) resulted in five more cases of true positive (increasing sensitivity from 50% to 91.7%, Table 3)” [PAGE 12]. But even that range must be read against the clock and the chance-level AUC, not as a new universal law.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical and biomechanical veto: the isokinetic ratio is a single layer of a multi-layer injury system. The paper describes football actions as “intense high-speed actions, quick force production with multiple directional changes during accelerating, decelerating, jumping or kicking” and says “These specific athletic movements induce stress and require the prime mover hamstrings to work in position of extreme stretching” [PAGE 2]. A concentric \(60^\circ/\mathrm{s}\) peak-torque ratio cannot exhaust that physical layer. The testing protocol itself is highly controlled: warm-up on a Monark cycle ergometer at 50–100 W, dynamic stretches, Biodex System 3 Pro, gravity correction, three sub-maximal trials, one maximal set, 30-s rest after the third sub-maximal trial, and a 3-min break when switching legs [PAGE 7]. Tests were also “conducted in the same order for each player” and “before 12 am to exclude inter-day variability” [PAGE 8]. These controls reduce measurement noise, but they also mean the ratio is a laboratory snapshot, not a match-play load measure.

Biomechanical veto: natural asymmetry is not a pathology. The paper states that “football players tend to be asymmetrical in lower leg strength mainly attributed to preferred sidedness in executing most of the unilateral football skills [45]” [PAGE 12]. It also reports that in Rahnama et al., “28 of 41 players (68%) were found to have at least one musculoskeletal abnormality which consisted of a contralateral strength imbalance of greater than 10%” [PAGE 12]. Therefore a single normative cut-off can misclassify normal football-specific asymmetry as risk. The paper’s own alternative is a range, not a dichotomous rule: assign the predicted-uninjured group when results are within a “normal” range and predicted-injured when values are lower or higher than extreme values [PAGE 13]. That is a biomechanical veto against any claim that “below 0.47” or “below 0.658” is a stand-alone causal lesion.

Weather and environmental veto: the source contains no ambient temperature, humidity, surface, rainfall, travel, or fixture-congestion variables. It is a single-centre Polish Premier League study with a winter break, and the authors caution about transport to leagues with different season structure [PAGE 13]. Therefore any claim that the Hcon/Qcon threshold predicts injury across climates or weather regimes is vetoed by absence of evidence. The correct cross-layer statement is conditional: the threshold may be an associative marker within this retrospective, ultrasound-confirmed, single-team dataset, but it cannot adjudicate weather-sensitive or load-sensitive causation. The paper’s limitation section reinforces the veto: retrospective design gives “evidence only of an association between the factor and risk of injury” [PAGE 14], and exposure time was unavailable [PAGE 14]. The physical, biomechanical, and weather layers therefore veto any strong causal or universal screening claim.
