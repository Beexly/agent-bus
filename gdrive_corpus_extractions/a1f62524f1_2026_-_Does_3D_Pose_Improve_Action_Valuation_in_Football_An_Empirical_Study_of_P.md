The paper’s inference chain is: 3D pose \(P_t\) is mapped to a representation \(u_t\), fused with the 92-dimensional position–velocity state \(x_t\), encoded by a GRU, and read out as action values \(Q_\theta(s_t,a_t)\); validity is then tested by mean squared error against the realised return \(G_t\). Each link is a possible veto point. The first multi-hop step is representational: raw joints preserve 87 dimensions, torso preserves 6, torso+head preserves 14, and ST-GCN compresses a \(5\times29\times3\) skeleton window to 128 dimensions. The second step is optimisation: the prior model minimises a SARSA TD loss plus an action-prediction loss, \(L=L_{TD}+\lambda_{AS}L_{AS}\), with \(\gamma=1\). The third step is reward horizon: the reward is zero except at the sequence end, where it is \(+1\), \(-1\), or the location-based EPV. The fourth step is evaluation: observed-action MSE, skill \(1-\mathrm{MSE}/\mathrm{Var}(G)\), bootstrap intervals over matches, and same-sign agreement across three seeds.

The central gray-zone result is that pose carries information about imminent actions but not, in this protocol, robustly about terminal attack value. The abstract states: “Across 50 on-ball and off-ball comparisons, pose robustly improved return prediction in only three, all using torso and head orientation, while worsening it in twelve, mostly with ST-GCN.” [PAGE 1]. That is a narrow claim: three of fifty comparisons, not a general rejection of pose. The same abstract reports the stronger training-objective result: “Training choices had greater effects: removing the action-prediction loss raised on-ball skill of position-only values from 0.003 to 0.040 and their correlation with expected goals from 0.23 to 0.60; the improvement in return prediction persisted at matched training length.” [PAGE 1]. The inference is therefore not “pose is useless” but “the prior learning setting is a larger bottleneck than the pose representation.”

A gray-zone audit must separate information from exploitability. The imminent-action probe shows that raw pose raises macro AUC from 0.914 to 0.934 for the on-ball action 0.2 s ahead, and the passer’s execution pose raises pass-success AUC from 0.844 to 0.856 [PAGE 1]. That supports nonzero mutual information between pose and near-future action or pass execution. It does not establish that the same information survives a sparse terminal return and a TD objective. The action-prediction loss uses the values themselves as action scores, so it can make the values encode action identity while degrading their distance to \(G_t\). The probe result is consistent: after six epochs the baseline values predicted action with macro AUC 0.853, but after training to early stopping only 0.735 [PAGE 9]. Thus the value vector can become a better action classifier and a worse return estimator at the same time.

Another gray zone is the word “realised.” \(G_t\) is an observed goal or conceded goal only for sequences ending in one; otherwise it is the location-based EPV at the end of the attack, “not an observed number of goals” [PAGE 3]. MSE against this mixture is not MSE against a pure observed outcome. The paper is careful: “An evaluation is valid when the value of the observed action agrees with what actually happened: \(Q(s_t,a_t)\) should be close to \(G_t\), and the value of a shot should increase with the probability that the shot is scored.” [PAGE 3]. That criterion validates observed-action values only, not counterfactual actions, rankings, or causal player contribution.

The robustness rule is also stricter than a naive bootstrap. The paper requires the interval to exclude zero and “the same sign for all three seeds” [PAGE 7]. This matters because the baseline off-ball MSE varied across seeds: 3.365, 3.408, and 3.755 × \(10^{-2}\), and raw/torso differences changed sign across seeds [PAGE 7]. A match-only bootstrap would have declared four off-ball effects under terminal TD with \(\lambda_{AS}=0.01\), but the seed-sign rule removes them. Conversely, the paper did not apply a multiple-comparison correction, and it explicitly notes that such a correction “could remove some of the three robust improvements of the action values but not add new ones” [PAGE 11]. The correct conclusion is asymmetric: the three improvements are fragile; the twelve deteriorations and the null results are the stronger pattern.

Training-length confounding is a major trap. Without the action loss, all terminal and package TD runs stopped after \(1+5=6\) epochs because the early-stopping minimum improvement exceeded the validation-loss decrease; the paper states: “These curves therefore do not show that the values had converged, and the results of TD without the action loss are those after six epochs.” [PAGE 5]. Yet the matched-length retraining still showed the action loss lowered validity: “The action loss therefore lowered the validity of the values both at matched length and at the length chosen by early stopping.” [PAGE 9]. This blocks the simple objection that \(\lambda_{AS}=0\) only looked better because it trained less.

The qualitative examples are illustrative, not inferential. The pose condition was chosen by the best on-ball test metric, torso+head under terminal TD with \(\lambda_{AS}=0.01\), and cases were selected by the smallest or largest \(\Delta_t\) [PAGE 10]. The paper itself says these cases “illustrate how pose changes the values; they are not evidence of validity” [PAGE 7]. A failure case includes a head below the hips in all frames, “a fallen or misestimated pose” [PAGE 8], which shows that pose-estimation failure can veto any biomechanical interpretation.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The state is \(x_t\in\mathbb{R}^{92}\), containing positions and velocities of the ball and 22 players in pitch coordinates divided by \(52.5\,\mathrm{m}\), with the attacking team directed toward positive \(x\) [PAGE 4]. The pose tensor is \(P_t\in\mathbb{R}^{5\times29\times3}\): 29 joints in the five 25 Hz frames of a 5 Hz step, expressed relative to the mid-hip joint and divided by \(2.44\,\mathrm{m}\) [PAGE 4]. The representation \(u_t\) is one of: none, \(0\); raw averaged joints, \(\mathbb{R}^{87}\); torso orientation, \(\mathbb{R}^{6}\); torso+head orientation, \(\mathbb{R}^{14}\); ST-GCN embedding, \(\mathbb{R}^{128}\); ST-GCN+torso, \(\mathbb{R}^{134}\) [PAGE 4]. The fused state is
\[
z_t=[x_t;u_t].
\]
The network is
\[
h_t=\mathrm{GRU}_\theta\!\left(\mathrm{ReLU}(Wz_t),h_{t-1}\right),\qquad q_t=f_\theta(h_t),
\]
with 64 hidden units and 16 outputs: 15 actions plus a padding class [PAGE 4]. The SARSA target and losses are
\[
y_t=r_t+\gamma m_{t+1}Q(s_{t+1},a_{t+1}),
\]
\[
L_{TD}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_t\left(y_t-Q(s_t,a_t)\right)^2,
\]
\[
L_{AS}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_t\log\mathrm{softmax}(\tilde q_t)_{a_t},
\]
\[
L=L_{TD}+\lambda_{AS}L_{AS},
\]
where \(m_{t+1}=1\) inside a sequence and \(0\) at its end, \(\gamma=1\), and \(\tilde q_t\) sets unavailable actions to \(-99{,}999\) [PAGE 4]. The Monte Carlo reference replaces \(L_{TD}\) with
\[
L_{MC}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_t\left(G_t-Q(s_t,a_t)\right)^2
\]
[PAGE 6]. The primary metric is
\[
\mathrm{MSE}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_t\left(Q(s_t,a_t)-G_t\right)^2,
\]
with skill
\[
\mathrm{skill}=1-\frac{\mathrm{MSE}}{\mathrm{Var}(G)}.
\]
The reported \(\Delta\) is the MSE of the seed-averaged values minus that of the baseline on the same steps, in units of \(10^{-4}\) squared goal-probability-equivalent; \(\mathrm{Var}(G)=3.507\times10^{-2}\) on-ball and \(3.319\times10^{-2}\) off-ball [PAGE 7]. \(Q\), \(G\), and xG are dimensionless goal-probability-equivalent quantities; MSE is squared goal-probability-equivalent; log loss is in nats when natural log is used; AUC, MCC, and skill are dimensionless; time horizons are seconds, with 5 Hz \(=0.2\,\mathrm{s}\) and 25 Hz \(=0.04\,\mathrm{s}\) [PAGE 4].

The ST-GCN block equations are
\[
H=\mathrm{ReLU}\!\left(\mathrm{BN}(W_sX\hat A)\right),
\]
\[
Y=\mathrm{ReLU}\!\left(\mathrm{BN}(\mathrm{Conv}_{3\times1}(H))\right)+R(X),
\]
with \(\hat A=D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}\), two blocks mapping \(3\to64\to128\), temporal kernel 3, and mean pooling over five frames and 29 joints [PAGE 12]. The encoder has 79,360 parameters; total model parameters rise from 31,952 for the baseline to 119,504 for ST-GCN [PAGE 13].

Assumptions and breakdown regimes. First, the skill metric is undefined or explosive as \(\mathrm{Var}(G)\to0\); here the variance is fixed and nonzero, so the near-zero skill values are interpretable as near-constant prediction. Second, if \(\lambda_{AS}\) dominates the TD gradient, the values become action logits rather than return estimates; the paper reports action-loss gradients 0.43 to 0.96 times the TD gradient under terminal TD with \(\lambda_{AS}=0.01\), versus 0.07 to 0.22 under MC regression [PAGE 9]. Third, package TD over padded length \(t=0,\ldots,T_{\max}-2\) can omit the final reward of the longest sequence and bootstrap shorter sequences from padded steps [PAGE 5]; package TD with \(\lambda_{AS}=0.01\) “did not learn” and all values moved toward large negative values [PAGE 7]. Fourth, the SOFmax mask is safe only if at least one available action remains; the defensive action is never masked, so the mask does not eliminate all logits. Fifth, missing joints are set to the mid-hip and missing frames and padded steps are zero, without a mask; batch normalisation computes statistics over all steps of a batch including padded zeros [PAGE 12]. This is a breakdown regime: the embedding of the same posture can depend on batch length and zero-input share. Sixth, bootstrap intervals over 12 test matches treat seed-averaged values as fixed and do not include seed variation; the paper therefore adds the same-sign-three-seeds rule [PAGE 7]. Seventh, pass-success matching retained only 1,845 of 6,332 training and 390 of 969 test sequences containing a pass [PAGE 8], so selection effects bound the pass AUC claim. Eighth, the xG model is an auxiliary reference, not ground truth: its test AUC was 0.75 with log loss 0.274 against 0.311 for a constant [PAGE 6].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from event-based action valuation, especially VAEP, and expected possession value, through deep reinforcement learning for player evaluation in ice hockey and football, to the multi-agent deep reinforcement learning model of Nakahara et al. that values on- and off-ball actions [PAGE 2]. The present paper is retrocausal in the sense that it reopens an earlier assumption: the prior model’s negative or null correlations with season goals, expert ratings, OBSO, and C-OBSO were read as “a different view of player contribution” [PAGE 2]. By replacing player-level correlations with step-level MSE against realised returns, the 2026 study shows that the prior setting’s values were “barely better than a constant prediction on on-ball steps (skill 0.003)” and worse off-ball (skill \(-0.057\)) [PAGE 7]. That retroactively reframes earlier null correlations: they may reflect weak observed-action validity, not merely a different valuation philosophy.

A second lineage is skeleton-based action recognition. ST-GCN treats joints as vertices and bones as edges, with spatial graph convolutions and temporal convolutions [PAGE 2]. The paper imports ST-GCN but strips its classification objective: the encoder is trained “only through the action-value objective” and is not pretrained [PAGE 3]. This is a genealogical inversion: a representation designed for labelled movement recognition is asked to serve a sparse terminal reward. The result is consistent with a mismatch: ST-GCN accounts for nine of the twelve robust deteriorations and lowers shot-value correlation with xG under three settings [PAGE 7]. The historical lesson is that representation capacity is not a substitute for objective alignment.

Multi-week traps. Week 1, the feature trap: add pose and expect value improvement because pose predicts imminent action. The paper blocks this with 3 improvements out of 50 comparisons and 12 robust worsenings [PAGE 7]. Week 2, the embedding trap: treat a learned ST-GCN embedding as automatically better than handcrafted orientation. Instead, ST-GCN+torso is robustly worse on on-ball steps under terminal TD with \(\lambda_{AS}=0.01\) (\(+2.69\times10^{-4}\)) and ST-GCN is worse under \(\lambda_{AS}=0\) [PAGE 7]. Week 3, the objective trap: optimise the action-prediction loss because it improves action classification. The probe shows the reverse for value validity: long training with \(\lambda_{AS}=0.01\) yields baseline action-probe AUC 0.735, while six epochs yields 0.853; at matched length, every pose condition is below the baseline on action-probe AUC [PAGE 9]. Week 4, the evaluation trap: select on TD loss or total validation loss. The paper states that TD loss “does not measure the distance to the true values” and that early stopping on total loss includes the action loss [PAGE 5]. The correct evaluation is observed-action MSE, an external shot reference, multiple seeds, and matched training length [PAGE 11]. A remaining trap is external-indicator worship: xG and OBSO are not ground truth for action values; xG is used only for shots, and off-ball values have no external indicator in this study [PAGE 11].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when a negative result triggers a return to the habit that more inputs or more capacity must help. Here the habit is: add 3D pose, then add ST-GCN, then trust the learned embedding. The paper’s own diagnosis is different: “Pose carried information about the next action and about the outcome of a pass at its execution (Section VI-B), but not in a form that improved the action values.” [PAGE 9]. The regression is to confuse “information exists” with “the value learner can use it under a sparse terminal reward.” A coach or analyst under pressure may likewise see a player’s body orientation and infer readiness, but the action-value model must propagate that orientation through several later decisions by both teams to a terminal goal or EPV. The model’s belief about the future is not the player’s belief, and the target player’s pose is only one agent’s body, not the other 21 players’ intentions.

The counter-trap is to separate tasks and clocks. The imminent-outcome predictor uses the same features without value learning and shows that raw joints improve the 0.2 s action prediction (macro AUC 0.914 to 0.934) and that execution pose improves pass-success prediction (AUC 0.844 to 0.856) [PAGE 1]. That is a short clock. The action-value target is a long clock: an attack may last several seconds and several actions, and the reward is terminal and sparse. The recursive theory-of-mind point is that a value model must represent nested expectations: what the target player will do, what teammates and opponents will do, and how the referee and game state constrain them. The paper’s state contains positions and velocities plus the target’s pose only; it has “no player identifier” and no joint policy or explicit coordination [PAGE 3]. It can value observed actions but cannot validate unobserved actions or causal contribution [PAGE 3]. The counter-trap is therefore not to ask pose to prove too much. Use pose for short-horizon anticipation and execution classification; use value error, seed agreement, and matched training length for long-horizon valuation.

The clock also appears in training. Under terminal TD with \(\lambda_{AS}=0.01\), runs lasted 47 to 134 epochs because early stopping monitored total validation loss; without the action loss, all TD runs stopped after six epochs [PAGE 5]. A researcher who reads only the six-epoch result may overcredit \(\lambda_{AS}=0\); a researcher who reads only the long-run result may overcredit pose because the baseline declined during training. The paper’s matched-length retraining is the counter-trap: it shows the action loss lowered validity at both lengths [PAGE 9]. The remaining clock problem is that six epochs do not prove convergence; the paper explicitly says a further slow decrease “cannot be excluded” [PAGE 5]. Thus the correct mental model is a race among three clocks: the 0.2 s anticipation clock, the multi-second attack clock, and the 6-versus-47-to-134-epoch optimisation clock. Pose wins the first, loses or ties the second, and is confounded by the third.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto. The pose input is a 29-joint, 25 Hz skeleton window of the target player only. A missing joint is placed at the mid-hip; a missing frame and a padded step are zeros; there is no mask [PAGE 12]. Therefore an apparent posture with joints at the hip and an absent pose are not distinguishable by the ST-GCN encoder. Batch normalisation computes statistics over all steps in a batch, including padded zeros, while evaluation uses running statistics [PAGE 12]. If a claimed pose effect depends on the ST-GCN conditions, the physical preprocessing provides an alternative explanation: the embedding may encode batch composition and missingness rather than biomechanics. The paper’s own limitation section says the encoder input has no mask and that batch normalisation can make “the embedding of the same posture depend on the presence of missing or padded steps rather than on the posture itself” [PAGE 11]. This vetoes any strong claim that ST-GCN’s deterioration is intrinsic to skeleton geometry.

Biomechanical veto. The handcrafted features are torso orientation and head orientation, not full-body joint angles, center-of-mass, foot planting, or muscle force. Torso orientation is the unit vector perpendicular to the left-to-right shoulder axis pointing forward; head orientation is perpendicular to the eye–ear axis with sign chosen by neck-to-nose or torso [PAGE 4]. These are horizontal orientation summaries averaged over frames. They cannot establish balance, weight transfer, limb loading, or kick mechanics. Raw joints contain more posture, but they are averaged over valid frames and do not include the surrounding players’ poses [PAGE 4]. The qualitative failure case with the head below the hips in all frames is explicitly “a fallen or misestimated pose” [PAGE 8]; any biomechanical inference from that step is invalid. The pass-success analysis uses the passer’s pose at the pass step, and the paper warns that this pose “can include the kick itself” [PAGE 5]. Therefore the AUC 0.844 to 0.856 result is an execution/posture association, not proof that pre-pass posture anticipates success. A pre-pass-only replication is required before any anticipatory coaching claim.

Weather veto. The dataset description contains event, Hawk-Eye tracking, and 3D pose data from UEFA Champions League 2022/23; it does not report weather, pitch moisture, wind, temperature, or surface state [PAGE 6]. There is no weather operator in the state \(x_t\) or pose \(u_t\), and no weather-stratified evaluation. Any claim that wind, rain, or pitch condition explains pose-value coupling is unfalsifiable in this study. The correct adjudication is a veto: weather cannot be invoked as a mechanism, and weather cannot be used to rescue a null pose effect. If a future study adds weather, it must show that the pose coefficient or MSE difference survives conditioning on weather and pitch variables; otherwise the physical layer remains unidentified.

Cross-layer veto proof. A pose-value claim is admissible only if (i) the required joints are present or the missingness is explicitly modelled, (ii) the representation is not confounded by padded-step batch statistics, (iii) the evaluation uses observed-action MSE against \(G_t\) with seed-sign robustness, and (iv) the temporal window matches the claimed anticipation horizon. The paper meets (iii) partially and (iv) only for the 0.2 s probe; it explicitly does not meet (i) and (ii) for ST-GCN, and it does not meet the pre-pass requirement for pass success. Therefore the strongest admissible claim is the paper’s own: “Within this training protocol, pose informed imminent actions but rarely improved observed-action values.” [PAGE 1]. The data are from one competition and season, with a 12-match test set [PAGE 11], so cross-layer generalization is also vetoed until replicated.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

V1. Matched-length, multi-seed objective veto. Veto condition: if terminal TD with \(\lambda_{AS}=0\) is retrained for the same 47–134 epoch regime as \(\lambda_{AS}=0.01\), and torso+head yields a robust on-ball and off-ball MSE reduction with the same sign for all seeds under both objectives, then the claim that pose “rarely” improves observed-action values is vetoed. Prediction from the reported data: torso+head on-ball under terminal TD with \(\lambda_{AS}=0\) has \(\Delta=-1.08\times10^{-4}\) with interval \([-2.60,+0.59]\), which includes zero, and under both MC settings its seeds disagree in sign [PAGE 7]. Predicted outcome: the apparent torso+head gain will not survive the matched-length, all-seed robustness test; the baseline’s six-epoch skill of 0.040 may drift, and the pose effect will remain non-robust. Verifiable metrics: \(\Delta\)MSE, 95% match-bootstrap interval, and three-seed sign agreement.

V2. Missing-pose and batch-normalisation ablation veto. Veto condition: if masking missing joints, excluding padded steps from batch-normalisation statistics, and using per-sample normalisation removes the robust ST-GCN deteriorations, then the claim that ST-GCN embeddings are intrinsically harmful is vetoed. Prediction: the nine of twelve robust deteriorations attributed to ST-GCN [PAGE 7] will shrink or become non-robust, because the current encoder cannot distinguish absent pose from a posture at the mid-hip and its batch statistics depend on zero-padded steps [PAGE 12]. Predicted outcome: ST-GCN+torso may cease to be robustly worse than baseline, but it will still not produce a robust value improvement; the shot-value correlation with xG should remain below the baseline interval. Verifiable metrics: count of robust \(\Delta\)MSE comparisons, \(\rho(Q,\mathrm{xG})\) with bootstrap interval, and parameter-matched ablations at 119,504 versus 31,952 parameters [PAGE 13].

V3. Temporal-window causal veto. Veto condition: if the passer’s pose from a strictly pre-pass window, excluding the kick and the pass step, produces the same pass-success AUC gain as the execution pose, then the “execution posture” interpretation is vetoed and a genuine anticipatory claim becomes admissible. Prediction: the execution-pose AUC gain \(+0.012\) \([+0.007,+0.018]\) from 0.844 to 0.856 [PAGE 8] will not replicate at the same magnitude before the kick, because the current window “can include the kick itself” [PAGE 5]. Predicted outcome: pre-pass pose will show a smaller or zero gain, while the 0.2 s on-ball action probe will retain its gain (raw joints macro AUC 0.914 to 0.934) [PAGE 1]. Verifiable metrics: pass-success AUC and log loss on matched pass events, with a pre-registered pre-pass window, 2,000 match-bootstrap resamples, and five model seeds. If all three veto conditions fail to trigger, the paper’s conclusion stands; if any one triggers, the corresponding claim must be narrowed to the specific representation, preprocessing, or temporal window that produced it.
