<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman’s work directly challenges Thorndike’s Law of Effect and Pavlovian conditioning, which dominated early 20th-century psychology. While behaviorists viewed learning as automatic strengthening of S-R bonds, Tolman proposed that animals form "purposive" expectations—"cognitions"—about their environment. The cited experiment was part of a series (including the famous latent learning studies) that established the existence of mental representations without reinforcement.

Historically, this lineage traces back to Kant’s notion of synthetic a priori spatial intuitions and Helmholtz’s unconscious inference. In neuroscience, it prefigures O’Keefe and Nadel’s place cell theory (1978) and modern hippocampal cognitive map models. The "multi-week trap" refers to the decades-long resistance from radical behaviorists who dismissed any talk of "expectations" as anthropomorphic. Only after the cognitive revolution did Tolman’s insights gain traction.

In experimental design, Tolman avoided confounding drive reduction with spatial learning by using satiated vs. hungry groups and manipulating reward timing. However, critics noted that hunger might affect attention rather than memory per se. Later replications using water deprivation or electrical stimulation confirmed the robustness of spatial hierarchies across different motivators.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical constraints impose strict vetoes on purely cognitive accounts. Biomechanical factors: Rats have limited working memory capacity (~3-4 items); navigating 14 alternatives requires chunking via landmarks. If blinds were rearranged daily, no stable hierarchy would form—even with perfect cognition. Weather effects: Temperature fluctuations alter metabolic rate, affecting hunger sensitivity and thus \(\beta\); cold stress could mimic "reward removal" by increasing baseline arousal and exploratory variability.

A crucial veto comes from pharmacology: Lesions of the dorsal hippocampus abolish the directional bias entirely (Morris et al., 1982), proving that the "expectation" depends on specific neural circuitry, not just abstract computation. Furthermore, vestibular disruption impairs turn discrimination, showing that egocentric-to-allocentric transformation is necessary for building the cognitive map. Thus, while the algorithmic-level description is valid, its implementation is tightly constrained by biology.
