The source’s argument is an accounting derivation followed by an empirical audit, so the deep trace must keep three layers separate: the algebraic identity, the behavioral interpretation, and the data-generating process. The derivation begins with a realized outcome \(M\) and a posted threshold \(s\): “Let the realized outcome be a real-valued random variable M ∈R.” [PAGE 2]. The source defines \(F_m(s)=\Pr(M&lt;s)\), \(\bar F_m(s)=\Pr(M&gt;s)\), and \(\pi_{\mathrm{push}}(s)=\Pr(M=s)\) [PAGE 2]. Public home and visitor handle shares are \(B_h(s)\) and \(B_v(s)=1-B_h(s)\); the conditional losing-side shares are \(b_{h,L}(s)=E[B_h(s)\mid M&lt;s]\) and \(b_{v,L}(s)=E[B_v(s)\mid M&gt;s]\) [PAGE 2]. Conditional on settlement, the book’s single-game profit is \(B_h-\phi B_v\) if the home side loses and \(B_v-\phi B_h\) if the visitor loses, with no-push and symmetric payout parameter \(\phi\) in the main text [PAGE 2]. Applying the law of iterated expectation gives the intermediate expression with \(F_m\) and \(\bar F_m\) weights [PAGE 2], and collecting terms yields the profit–bias identity “E[π(s)] = (1 + ϕ) Q(s) −ϕ” [PAGE 3], where \(Q(s)=b_{h,L}(s)\Pr(M&lt;s)+b_{v,L}(s)\Pr(M&gt;s)\) is the expected fraction of handle on the losing side [PAGE 3]. This is the central multi-hop step: the book’s expected margin is affine and strictly increasing in \(Q\), not in the unconditional public lean alone. Independence of bet shares and outcome is the special case \(b_{h,L}=b_h\) and \(b_{v,L}=b_v\), which collapses to \(\delta(s)=b_{h,L}(s)-b_{h,W}(s)=0\) [PAGE 3–4]. Under that restriction, Levitt’s expression is recovered; when \(\delta\neq 0\), Levitt’s model understates profit if the public’s stake is on the eventual losing side and overstates it if the stake is on the winner [PAGE 3–4].

The second hop decomposes \(Q\) into three channels: “Q(s) = 1/2 + 2 θ(s) λ(s) + 2Fm(s) ¯Fm(s) δ(s)” [PAGE 4], and therefore the margin is the hold plus shading×lean plus outcome covariance [PAGE 4]. The source’s abstract states that “The identity resolves the book’s margin into exactly three channels: its hold, the product of its price shading and the public’s lean, and the covariance between bet share and outcome” [PAGE 1]. The third hop is behavioral: under the public-belief model \(Y=X+\epsilon+V\), \(M=X+U\), with the public backing home when \(Y&gt;s\), “Q(s) = Pr(public backs the losing side)” [PAGE 5]. Thus \(Q\) is the public’s misclassification probability, or Bayes error, for the binary event \(M&gt;s\). The fourth hop is the Goldilocks Zone: the book profits iff \(Q(s)&gt;\tau=\phi/(1+\phi)\), while a negligible bettor profits only in a tail, \(F_m(s)&lt;\tau\) or \(F_m(s)&gt;1-\tau\) [PAGE 5–6]. The behavioral lineage includes public-belief models in Snowberg–Wolfers, Jullien–Salanié, and Avery–Chevalier [PAGE 2], and the limits-of-arbitrage framing of Shleifer–Vishny [PAGE 2]. The empirical lineage includes Paul–Weinbach tests, Humphreys, Gandar et al., Woodland–Woodland, and the line-movement literature [PAGE 1–2, 13]. The source also imports Jordà local projections from macroeconometrics to test feedback ordering [PAGE 9, 15].

The retrocausal lesson is that later evidence can reinterpret earlier pooled tests. The source finds a pooled \(\hat\delta=5.8\) pp, \(p=0.0004\), which looks like a square public; stratifying by favorite identity gives \(-1.5\) pp and \(+0.6\) pp, both non-significant [PAGE 8]. “The apparent dependence is thus a Simpson’s paradox” [PAGE 8]. This retroactively warns that pooled tests of bet share–outcome dependence can be composition artifacts when favorite identity drives both betting and settlement. The multi-week traps are temporal and compositional. The data span an eleven-week window with hourly sampling in the twenty-four hours before onset and non-overlapping six-hour windows [PAGE 8, 14–15]. For home underdogs, \(\hat Q\) rises from \(46.9\%\pm2.6\) to \(52.0\%\pm2.1\); for home favorites it stays above 50% (\(54.4\%\pm2.4\) to \(53.8\%\pm2.0\)) [PAGE 8, 10]. The time×outcome interaction is significant for home underdogs (bootstrap \(p=0.038\), \(n=256\)) but not for home favorites (\(p=0.85\), \(n=288\)); the three-way interaction is not significant (\(p=0.12\)) [PAGE 8, 10]. A second trap is feedback direction: a one-point increase in the devigged home probability lowers the home share by about 0.20 points, significant at horizons 1–5 and peaking at \(h=4\) (\(t=-3.11\), \(p=0.002\), \(n=1090\)); the reverse response is flat (\(|\hat\beta|\le0.010\), all \(p&gt;0.27\), \(n=1103\)) [PAGE 9, 11]. A third trap is profit reconstruction: mean margin \(5.20\%\) [0.88, 9.35] versus hold \(4.39\%\), with \(s.d.=73.8\%\) [PAGE 9–10, 12]. The source bounds aligned shading at 3.7 pp and outcome covariance at 5.2 pp [PAGE 10, 12]. These are multi-week traps because a short window can show a strong public lean, a changing composition, or a transient early-money pattern that does not survive stratification or the full pre-onset clock.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Recursive theory-of-mind here has three levels. First, the public forms a noisy, biased belief \(Y=X+\epsilon+V\) and backs a side when \(Y&gt;s\) [PAGE 5]. Under uncertainty, the public’s “crisis habit regression” is the reversion to a salient favorite: the source measures a public lean \(\lambda=b_h-p^*\) of \(+29.6\) pp when the home side is favored and \(-24.5\) pp when the visitor is favored [PAGE 9, 12]. That is a habit, not necessarily information: it is a directional bias observable before settlement, and it enters profit only through \(\theta\lambda\) [PAGE 4]. Second, the book may counter the habit by shading: setting \(\theta\) in the direction of \(\lambda\). But this is a counter-trap, because Corollary 3 says shading raises profit only if it is paired with a same-sign lean; if the public is balanced, shading does nothing, and if the public is sharp (\(\delta&lt;0\)), the covariance channel can reduce profit [PAGE 4, 7]. The empirical audit finds no detectable shading: the bound is at most 3.7 pp aligned with the lean, and “the absence of detectable shading nullifies the potential additional profit.” [PAGE 13]. So the recursive loop is: public forecasts outcome, book forecasts public habit, public may forecast book shading, and the analyst forecasts all three. The identity is the accounting constraint that survives whatever the agents believe. Third, the clock changes the composition. Early “sharp” betting is not sustained for home underdogs: \(\hat Q\) rises toward onset, while favorite games remain stable above 50% [PAGE 8, 10]. The home-underdog outcome-conditioned gap is significant in time (\(p=0.038\)) but the favorite stratum is not (\(p=0.85\)), and the strata do not significantly differ (\(p=0.12\)) [PAGE 8, 10]. The price leads the share rather than the reverse: the public fades a price move, while the book does not reprice to observed share moves [PAGE 9, 11]. In recursive terms, the book’s posted price is an input to public belief, not a passive mirror of the public’s current habit. The counter-trap is therefore not “shade more”; it is “shade only where the habit is stable, measurable, and aligned,” and the clock is the test of whether the habit is stable or merely a pre-onset composition shift.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A physical, biomechanical, or weather layer cannot veto the profit–bias identity as an algebraic statement. The source’s only outcome object is \(M\): “Let the realized outcome be a real-valued random variable M ∈R.” [PAGE 2]. All settlement information enters through \(F_m(s)\), \(\bar F_m(s)\), and \(\pi_{\mathrm{push}}(s)\); all betting information enters through \(B_h(s)\), \(B_v(s)\), and the conditional expectations \(b_{h,L},b_{v,L}\) [PAGE 2]. Let \(Z\) denote any physical, biomechanical, or weather vector. If \(Z\) affects the outcome, it is absorbed into \(F_m(s\mid Z)\); if it affects betting, it is absorbed into \(B_h(s\mid Z)\) and the conditional shares. By the law of iterated expectation,
\[
E[\pi(s)]=E_Z\left[(1+\phi)Q(s\mid Z)-\phi\right]
=(1+\phi)E_Z[Q(s\mid Z)]-\phi,
\]
so the same identity holds conditionally and unconditionally. A cross-layer variable can change the numerical value of \(Q\), \(\delta\), \(\theta\), or \(\lambda\), but it cannot break the accounting relation unless it changes the settlement rule, the payout convention, the handle normalization, or the binary-outcome structure. Those are model vetoes, not physical vetoes. Weather or biomechanics could be confounders for the empirical claim: if \(Z\) jointly affects favorite identity and settlement, a pooled \(\delta\) may be a composition artifact, and stratification or conditioning is required. That is exactly the Simpson’s-paradox lesson [PAGE 8]. But the source contains no biomechanical, force, fatigue, or weather operator in its equations; the formal chain runs from \(M\), \(s\), \(B_h\), \(\phi\) to \(Q\) and \(E[\pi]\) [PAGE 2–4]. Therefore a physical-layer claim can veto a causal interpretation or an empirical estimate, not the identity. The correct adjudication is: admit \(Z\) only as a covariate that shifts \(F_m\) or the conditional shares; do not admit it as a term that can make \(E[\pi]\neq(1+\phi)Q-\phi\) while the source’s assumptions hold.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
