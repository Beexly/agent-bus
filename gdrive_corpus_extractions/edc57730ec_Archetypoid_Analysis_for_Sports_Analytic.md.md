<THINK_TRACE>
The source is a methodology paper, not a contest-pricing document. Its central multi-hop claim is: raw sports performance observations \(X\) are represented by convex mixtures of archetypes; archetypes may be unobserved convex combinations; archetypoids restrict the mixture weights \(\beta\) to observed cases; therefore the output is a set of real extreme athletes or teams plus dimensionless composition weights \(\alpha\), which can be sorted into rankings. The first hop is the observation matrix \(X\in\mathbb R^{n\times m}\). The second hop is the AA objective, which minimizes reconstruction error under simplex constraints. The third hop is the ADA restriction, which changes \(\beta_{jl}\) from a continuous simplex weight to a binary selector. The fourth hop is interpretation: \(\alpha_{ij}\) says how much archetypoid \(j\) contributes to case \(i\). The fifth hop is ranking, which is valid only under strong conditions, especially \(k=2\) with a real “worst-best” direction or \(k>2\) with several archetypoid-specific rankings.

The paper states the core idea plainly: “In archetypoid analysis each datum is expressed as a mixture of actual observations (archetypoids).” [PAGE 1]. This matters because a real extreme case is easier for coaches, scouts, and fans to interpret than a synthetic convex combination. The AA objective is described as minimizing “the residual sum of squares (RSS)” [PAGE 5], with the constraint that “the rows of α and columns of β add up to 1” [PAGE 5]. The ADA discreteness condition is equally explicit: “βjl should only take on the value 0 or 1” [PAGE 6]. The SWAP algorithm then searches over observed replacements: “In the SWAP phase, for each archetypoid a, for each non-archetypoid data point o, swap a and o and compute the RSS of the configuration” [PAGE 7].

The gray-zone audit begins with a warning: “extreme” is not identical to “best.” The paper’s own examples include a benchwarmer-type profile and an “ineffective scorer” interpretation for Jason Kidd, even though Kidd was an elite assist player. The model identifies extremes in the measured feature space, not global athletic value. A second gray zone is the number \(k\). The elbow criterion is visual and subjective. A third is non-nesting: “Note that neither archetypes nor archetypoids are necessarily nested.” [PAGE 7]. A fourth is ranking validity. With \(k=2\), the paper says “the ranking procedure is equivalent to sorting by the best archetypoid’s alphas descending” [PAGE 9], but this requires a genuine worst-best axis. In the 2010/2011 NBA example, the paper reports that “a real ‘worst-best’ direction is not obtained since ‘bad’ players do not exist in our data set” [PAGE 15]. Thus a forced two-archetypoid ranking can be misleading. A fifth gray zone is standardization: variables are standardized so that all variables have equal weight, and the paper warns that positive skew affects percentile interpretation [PAGE 14]. A sixth is the sparse functional extension: FADA uses estimated principal-component scores, and the paper states that “the fourth eigenvalue, eigenfunction and their respective scores are almost negligible” [PAGE 22], so the effective dimensionality is smaller than the nominal \(m=4\). A seventh is the asymmetric team case: the h-plot preserves relationships between profiles, not exact pairwise dissimilarities, and the reported fit is “93%” [PAGE 26]. That is good but not exact. Finally, the authors themselves limit the claim: “This approach is not a definitive measure of sports value” [PAGE 29]. Any downstream inference that treats archetypoids as causal, weather-proof, biomechanically exhaustive, or universally best is outside the source’s evidential scope.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(X\in\mathbb R^{n\times m}\) be the observed data matrix of \(n\) athletes or teams and \(m\) features. The AA problem is
\[
\min_{\alpha,\beta}\ \|X-\alpha\beta X\|_F^2
\]
with
\[
\alpha_{ij}\ge 0,\quad \sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{ij}=1,\qquad
\beta_{jl}\ge 0,\quad \sum_{l=1}^{n}\beta_{jl}=1,
\]
where \(\alpha\in\mathbb R^{n\times k}\), \(\beta\in\mathbb R^{k\times n}\), and archetypes are \(Z=\beta X\). The source describes the objective as minimizing “the residual sum of squares (RSS)” [PAGE 5] and states that “the rows of α and columns of β add up to 1” [PAGE 5]. The RSS has squared units of \(X\); when \(X\) is standardized, as in the paper’s basketball examples [PAGE 10], RSS is dimensionless in squared standard-deviation units. The weights \(\alpha_{ij}\) are dimensionless proportions in \([0,1]\).

ADA replaces the continuous \(\beta\) simplex with an observed-case selector:
\[
\min_{\alpha,\beta}\ \|X-\alpha\beta X\|_F^2
\]
subject to
\[
\beta_{jl}\in\{0,1\},\quad \sum_{l=1}^{n}\beta_{jl}=1,
\]
and the same simplex constraint on each row of \(\alpha\). The source states that “βjl should only take on the value 0 or 1” [PAGE 6]. Thus each archetypoid is an observed row \(X_{l_j}\), not a synthetic point. For \(k=1\), “the archetype is the sample mean, whereas the archetypoid is the medoid” [PAGE 7]. For \(k\ge N\), where \(N\) is the number of convex-hull vertices, “archetypoids (and archetypes) coincide with the vertices” [PAGE 7]. The algorithm has BUILD and SWAP phases; the source describes the replacement search explicitly: “In the SWAP phase, for each archetypoid a, for each non-archetypoid data point o, swap a and o and compute the RSS of the configuration” [PAGE 7].

For sparse functional data, let \(x_i(t)\) be a smooth function observed at irregular times \(t_{i1},\dots,t_{in_i}\). The Karhunen-Loeve approximation is
\[
x_i(t)=\mu(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{ij}\phi_j(t)+\varepsilon_i(t),
\]
where \(\mu(t)\) is the mean function, \(\phi_j(t)\) are orthonormal eigenfunctions, \(\xi_{ij}\) are principal-component scores, and \(m\) is the number of retained components. The source states that “to obtain FADA we can apply ADA to the n × m matrix X, with the scores (the coefficients in the Karhunen-Loeve basis)” [PAGE 8]. Here \(t\) is in years of age, \(x_i(t)\) is Game Score with Game Score units, \(\phi_j(t)\) are dimensionless under orthonormality, and \(\xi_{ij}\) inherit Game Score units. The reported eigenvalues are in squared Game Score units: \(3.127180\times 10^2\), \(1.989077\times 10^2\), \(8.946427\times 10^1\), and \(3.68\times 10^{-13}\) [PAGE 22]. The fourth component is effectively a numerical null direction, so the effective rank is three.

For asymmetric proximities, let \(D\in\mathbb R^{N\times N}\) be a dissimilarity matrix. The h-plot uses the two largest eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the variance-covariance matrix \(S\) of \(D\):
\[
H_2=(\sqrt{\lambda_1}q_1,\sqrt{\lambda_2}q_2),
\qquad
g=\frac{\lambda_1^2+\lambda_2^2}{\sum_j\lambda_j^2}.
\]
The source states “H2 = (√λ1 q1, √λ2 q2)” and that “the closer it is to 1, the better the fit” [PAGE 4]. If \(D\) is a Gower coefficient matrix, entries are dimensionless in \([0,1]\), so \(H_2\) is dimensionless; if \(D\) carried units \(u\), then \(S\) would have units \(u^2\), \(\lambda\) would have units \(u^2\), and \(H_2\) would have units \(u\). The h-plot is not an exact Euclidean embedding of non-metric or asymmetric dissimilarities; it preserves relationships between profiles, not exact pairwise distances.

Ranking uses \(\alpha\). For \(k=2\), one archetypoid is the “best” extreme and the other the “worst”; because the two \(\alpha\) values sum to one, “the ranking procedure is equivalent to sorting by the best archetypoid’s alphas descending” [PAGE 9]. For \(k>2\), there is no unique ranking; there are archetypoid-specific rankings. The main breakdown regimes are: \(k=1\) degenerates to mean or medoid; \(k\ge N\) collapses to hull vertices; the elbow choice is subjective; BUILD/SWAP can settle in local optima; archetypoids are not necessarily nested across \(k\); SiVM assumes vertices and is greedy; SMRS cannot always select an exact \(k\); h-plot fit below one means distorted geometry; sparse FPCA can be unstable when curves have too few observations; and a two-endpoint worst-best ranking is invalid when the sampled population contains no real “bad” extreme [PAGE 15].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from Cutler and Breiman’s 1994 archetypal analysis, which introduced convex-mixture “pure types,” to the archetypoid correction in Vinué et al. (2015), which forces representatives to be observed cases. The sports-analytic ancestor is Eugster (2012), who applied AA to basketball performance profiles; the present paper explicitly positions ADA as an improvement because AA archetypes can be “fictitious” and because ADA avoids “mixtures of mixtures.” The functional-data branch descends from Ramsay and Silverman and from sparse FPCA methods such as Peng and Paul (2009), Yao and Müller, and James et al.; the paper uses the Peng-Paul geometric maximum-likelihood approach for sparse functional principal components. The asymmetric-data branch descends from Epifanio’s h-plot work for non-metric dissimilarity matrices and from Gower’s general similarity coefficient. The ranking branch descends from D’Esposito and Ragozini’s “worst-best” projection for multivariate performance. Competing representative-selection methods include SiVM, SMRS, Affinity Propagation, PAM, \(k\)-means, fuzzy \(k\)-means, and Bayesian partial-membership models.

The retrocausal traps are multi-week traps because they recur whenever a new season is analyzed. First, a user may treat archetypoids as convex-hull vertices; the paper warns they are not necessarily vertices. Second, a user may assume that the \(k=3\) solution contains the \(k=2\) solution; the paper explicitly denies nesting [PAGE 7]. Third, a user may compare RSS across methods without matching \(k\); the paper notes that SMRS could not select exactly three representatives in one example and that a seven-representative SMRS solution had RSS \(0.06982\), while ADA with \(k=7\) had RSS \(0.05301\) [PAGE 13]. Fourth, a user may treat the h-plot as an exact metric map; it is a two-dimensional representation with a reported 93% fit in the team example [PAGE 26]. Fifth, a user may treat post hoc awards as model validation; the paper uses awards to interpret archetypoids, but awards are not input features. Sixth, a user may leak future career information into a current-season ranking. The FADA example uses ages 19 to 40 and removes players under 19, over 40, and games under five minutes [PAGE 21]; that is a defensible cleaning rule for career curves, but it changes the population and therefore the meaning of “extreme.” Seventh, a user may overread the \(k=2\) ranking when no real worst case exists, as the paper itself cautions [PAGE 15].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when an analyst, facing noisy performance data, falls back to familiar central representatives: cluster centroids, average profiles, or established stars. The paper’s comparison section is designed to counter that habit: AP and clustering methods “return representatives … mainly in the middle of the data rather than at the boundaries” [PAGE 11], whereas ADA returns real extremes. The counter-trap is the opposite error: once an extreme is real, the analyst may assume it is causally decisive or universally best. The paper blocks that move by showing that an archetypoid can be a benchwarmer, a low-minute player, or an “ineffective scorer” in one feature space while still being elite in another. The model’s theory of mind must therefore represent three agents: the scout who trusts experience, the fan who trusts awards, and the analyst who trusts the algorithm. ADA mediates among them by making the composition weights explicit, but it does not settle their priors.

The clock problem is temporal. In the multivariate examples, the data are season-bounded, but in the FADA example the object is a career trajectory \(x_i(t)\) over age. The paper reports that LeBron James is a “consistently strong performer,” while Danny Granger “only performed well early in his career” and Stephen Jackson is “something of a late bloomer” [PAGE 23]. A ranking that ignores the clock can confuse an early peak with a late peak. The recursive failure mode is that the analyst updates beliefs about a player from a smoothed career curve, then treats the curve as if it were a current-season measurement. The paper’s use of sparse FPCA and \(m=4\) eigenfunctions partially regularizes this, but the fourth eigenvalue is nearly zero [PAGE 22], so the effective temporal signal is low-dimensional. The correct counter-trap is to keep the clock explicit: age windows, sampling density, and minimum-games filters must be reported alongside any archetypoid claim. Without that, “crisis habit regression” returns as narrative confirmation, and the “clock” becomes a hidden variable rather than a modeled one.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source does not contain weather variables, biomechanical load variables, injury status, travel distance, pitch or court conditions, or physical-force measurements. Its multivariate basketball examples use box-score features such as games played, minutes, field goals, rebounds, assists, steals, turnovers, blocks, fouls, and points [PAGE 12]. Its functional example uses Game Score over age [PAGE 21]. Its soccer example uses ordinal match outcomes at home and away, converted into a Gower dissimilarity matrix [PAGE 26]. Therefore, any claim that an archetypoid is “best” because of weather resilience, biomechanical superiority, or physical-force dominance is not identified by this source.

Formally, let \(Y\) be the performance outcome and let \(X_{\text{obs}}\) be the observed source features. The estimated model is
\[
Y=f(X_{\text{obs}})+\epsilon.
\]
If a proposed explanation requires \(X_{\text{weather}}\), \(X_{\text{biomech}}\), or \(X_{\text{load}}\), and those variables are absent, then \(f\) is misspecified for that explanation. A causal query such as \(P(Y\mid do(X_{\text{weather}}=w))\) cannot be identified from \(X_{\text{obs}}\) without additional assumptions, and the source supplies no such assumptions. The h-plot case is especially clear: the dissimilarity matrix is built from match results, not from weather or biomechanics, so the 93% h-plot fit [PAGE 26] is a fit to ordinal result relationships, not to physical mechanisms. The FADA case is also clear: Game Score is an outcome summary, not a biomechanical load signal. The paper’s cleaning rule removes games under five minutes and ages outside 19 to 40 [PAGE 21], which further means that physical exposure is truncated by design. Thus the cross-layer veto is: reject any physical, biomechanical, or weather explanation that is not represented in \(X_{\text{obs}}\) or \(D\). The source can support descriptive extreme-profile analysis; it cannot adjudicate the omitted layer.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
