For m = 23.75 habs: H(1) = 2.375 habs; H(5) = 23.75[1 - 0.9⁵] ≈ 9.726 habs; H(10) ≈ 15.472 habs; lim_{n→∞} H(n) = 23.75 habs.
For m = 70.14 habs: H(1) = 7.014 habs; H(5) ≈ 28.727 habs; H(10) ≈ 45.688 habs; lim_{n→∞} H(n) = 70.14 habs.

Breakdown / singularity regimes:
- F = 0 ⇒ H(n) = 0 ∀ n (no learning regardless of m).
- m = 0 ⇒ H(n) = 0 (reinforcer completely ineffective).
- Computed m > 100 habs ⇒ physiological ceiling violated; model must saturate at m_phys or predict breakdown.
- F ≥ 1 ⇒ fractional-increment interpretation fails; H(1) ≥ m implies one-trial overshoot of the asymptote.
- 0 < F < 1, n → ∞ ⇒ H(n) → m asymptotically but never reaches m in finite trials (no finite-n singularity, but no attainment of the limit).

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage traces through Hull's 1943 Principles of Behavior, a neobehaviorist hypothetico-deductive system in which habit strength H is an intervening variable inferred from reinforcement history. The amount-of-reinforcement hypothesis descends from Gantt's experiments on conditioned reflexes and from Grindley's work on reinforcement efficacy; both are invoked by analogy on [PAGE 129], with the explicit caveat that no direct adulterated-food experiment had been reported. Figures 27 and 28 establish the qualitative dose-response shape referenced here; Chapter VIII supplies the F = 1/10 illustration; Figure 29 integrates the two m values into acquisition curves. Multi-week traps: in longitudinal animal training, holding m fixed at a 100-hab physiological maximum across weeks ignores sensory-specific satiety, body-weight changes, and reinforcer devaluation. Holding F constant across weeks ignores reactive inhibition, fatigue accumulation, and circadian or seasonal shifts in drive. The page-ending truncation ("Let it be supposed that the habit...") conceals the subsequent implication—likely extinction or partial reinforcement—creating an epistemic trap: citing p. 129 alone cannot justify claims about what happens after the limit of practice is reached. Because the hab lacks an SI traceable realization, cross-species or cross-modality generalization (e.g., food vs. water vs. escape-from-shock) is not licensed by this page.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: under a drive shift (crisis, fear, sudden satiety, or deprivation change), the expression of acquired habit regresses even if H(n) is unchanged, because Hullian performance is a multiplicative function of habit, drive, incentive, and reactivity rather than H alone. Counter-trap: the error of assuming that because acquisition followed H(n) = m[1 - (1-F)^n] with constant F, post-crisis re-acquisition will retrace an identical curve. Stress can alter both F (learning rate) and the effective m (reinforcer value), so the curve after regression is not guaranteed to be the same curve shifted in time. Clock: n in the growth function is a trial counter, not wall-clock time. Over multi-week schedules, inter-trial intervals, feeding rhythms, and ambient conditions decouple trial count from elapsed time; a "fast" curve in trial space may appear slow in clock space if reinforcements are spaced widely, and vice versa. Thus the clock governing acquisition is the schedule of reinforcement, not the calendar, and any theory-of-mind model of the organism must represent the animal's expectation of the next reinforcement as a function of trial history, not of hours since training began.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto: the organism must physically ingest the reinforcer. Six grams of dry food per reinforcement may exceed gastric capacity or require more manipulation time than the inter-trial interval permits; if the animal cannot consume the full 6 g, effective a < 6 g and the predicted m = 70.14 habs is vetoed downward. Biomechanical layer veto: the instrumental response (lever press, runway traversal, key peck) has a maximum sustainable emission rate; if the reinforcement schedule demands responses faster than the biomechanical ceiling, the observed acquisition curve is capped by response topography, not by habit strength, and the inferred F will appear to decline artifactually. Weather layer veto: ambient temperature alters thermoregulatory load and hunger drive. High temperature reduces food intake (effective a and q drop), whereas cold increases it; thus the experimenter's gram setting no longer determines received reinforcement independent of weather, violating ceteris paribus. Veto proof: the formal equation H(n) = m[1 - (1-F)^n] is valid only under ad libitum consumption, controlled temperature, within gastric and response-rate limits, and with reinforcer quality held constant. Any observed plateau below predicted m, any F variation across a or q, or any acquisition curve shaped by response-rate ceiling rather than by n, triggers a physical/biomechanical/weather veto and invalidates the pure amount-of-reinforcement inference.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios, each with a source-based prediction and a falsifiable veto condition.

Scenario 1 — Satiety veto. Condition: rats are pre-fed to satiety before each session, yet the experimenter still presents 6 g of food per reinforcement. Source prediction ([PAGE 129], F = 0.1): m = 70.14 habs; H(10) ≈ 45.69 habs. Veto mechanism: if actual consumption per reinforcement falls below the satiety threshold (e.g., < 1 g), effective m collapses toward the 1-g value of 23.75 habs or lower. Verifiable prediction: hopper weighing before/after each session shows intake << 6 g; fitting H(n) to the acquisition data rejects m = 70.14 habs (confidence interval excludes the source value), and the curve instead matches a lower m with unchanged F.

Scenario 2 — Taste-aversion veto. Condition: adulterated food (as discussed on [PAGE 129] by analogy to Gantt and Grindley) is paired with an illness-inducing agent, producing conditioned taste aversion. Source prediction: adulterated agent is "more than half as effective" as unadulterated, so m > 0.5 m_unadulterated, with F unchanged. Veto mechanism: conditioned taste aversion drives q → 0 (the food becomes aversive), so the animal does not consume it and m → 0 despite caloric content. Verifiable prediction: a place-preference or consumption test shows avoidance of the adulterated food; the acquisition curve remains flat at H ≈ 0 habs, falsifying m > 40 habs and the claim that F alone governs the rate.

Scenario 3 — F-drift veto. Condition: extended training beyond n = 50 trials with a fixed 1 g reinforcer per trial. Source prediction ([PAGE 129]): F remains constant at 0.1, producing a smooth exponential approach to m = 23.75 habs. Veto mechanism: reactive inhibition or fatigue accumulates with trial count, so the effective fractional increment decays, F_n = F_0 e^{-λn}, λ > 0. Verifiable prediction: trial-by-trial response latency or error rate increases after a critical trial N_crit; estimating F_n = ΔH_n / (m - H_{n-1}) yields F_n significantly below 0.1 for n > N_crit, rejecting F-invariance and forcing a time-varying growth law.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
