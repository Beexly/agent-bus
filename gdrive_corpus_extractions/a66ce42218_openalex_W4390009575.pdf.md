Let \(x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^3\) be reference position in metres and \(\hat{x}_s(t)\) the EPTS estimate. The position error is \(e_s(t)=\hat{x}_s(t)-x(t)\) [m]. For the velocity validation that the source prefers for GNSS, define \(\epsilon_{v,s,i}=\hat{v}_{s,i}-v_i\) [m s\(^{-1}\)]. The mean bias and RMSD are
\[
B_s=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\epsilon_{v,s,i}\quad[\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}],\qquad
R_s=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\epsilon_{v,s,i}^{2}}\quad[\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}].
\]
The source states that “RMSD works by providing a non-negative value as a representation of accuracy, with readings closer to zero typically better” [PAGE 9], and that mean bias is used to determine whether an EPTS “will under or over-estimate” [PAGE 9]. Assumptions: matched time base, matched filter, and comparable body point. Breakdown: mean bias cancels when errors are large but opposite in sign; RMSD hides non-linearity, velocity-band dependence, and outliers [PAGE 9]. The source itself warns that when “substantial error both above and below the zero line” exists, mean bias alone “can be misleading” [PAGE 9].

For sample stabilization, let \(\Delta_s(n)\) be the between-system difference after \(n\) samples. A standard coefficient of variation is
\[
\mathrm{CV}(n)=\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{\Delta}(n)}{|\hat{\mu}_{\Delta}(n)|}\quad[\text{dimensionless}],
\]
with cut point \(n^\ast=\min\{n:|\mathrm{CV}(n)-\mathrm{CV}(n_{\mathrm{ref}})|\le\varepsilon\}\). This formalizes the source’s Figure 4 logic: “A flattening of the curve (stabilisation of the difference) can be used to inform the number of samples required when conducting comparisons” [PAGE 6]. The source’s example is \(N=2{,}500\) samples at \(f_s=10\,\mathrm{Hz}\), hence \(T=N/f_s=250\,\mathrm{s}\) [PAGE 7]. Breakdown: \(\hat{\mu}_{\Delta}\to0\) makes CV singular; nonstationary drift or fatigue can prevent a plateau.

For GNSS, the pseudorange measurement for satellite \(i\) is
\[
\rho_i=c(t_r-t_i)=\lVert x_r-x_i\rVert+c(\delta t_r-\delta t_i)+I_i+T_i+\epsilon_i\quad[\mathrm{m}],
\]
with \(c=299{,}792{,}458\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) as cited by the source [PAGE 7]. The source’s dilution operator is HDOP: \(\sigma_{\hat{x}}=\mathrm{HDOP}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{UERE}}\) [m], with HDOP dimensionless. The source says “the HDOP should be both measured and reported when establishing the accuracy of GNSS” [PAGE 2]. Assumption: zero-mean, comparable satellite geometry. Breakdown: near-singular satellite geometry makes HDOP diverge; multipath/shadowing violates the zero-mean error assumption [PAGE 2], [PAGE 7]. The source also notes multi-constellation gains: “static 3D position accuracy was improved 33.8%–38.5%, while dynamic accuracy by 12.2%–39.8%” [PAGE 2], while warning that a “saturation point” may exist [PAGE 2].

For LPS time/angle-of-arrival geometry, an audit-level Fisher-information operator is
\[
\mathbf{J}=\sum_{a=1}^{A}\frac{1}{\sigma_a^2}\mathbf{H}_a^\top\mathbf{H}_a\quad[\mathrm{m^{-2}}],\qquad
\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{x})=\mathbf{J}^{-1}\quad[\mathrm{m^2}],\qquad
\mathrm{MSE}=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{J}^{-1})\quad[\mathrm{m^2}],
\]
where \(A\) is anchor count, \(\sigma_a\) is range error [m], and \(\mathbf{H}_a\) is the unit-line-of-sight geometry matrix (dimensionless). This is a formal audit operator informed by the source’s LPS discussion, not a verbatim equation in the paper. The source reports that in a \(30\,\mathrm{m}\times30\,\mathrm{m}\) simulation, “mean squared error of positioning (∼0.5 m with three anchors) stabilised when 8–10 anchors were used to ∼0.1 m” [PAGE 3]. Breakdown: \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{J})<3\) for collinear/coplanar anchors makes covariance unbounded; adding anchors also “reduces the available bandwidth and therefore sample rate” and complicates synchronization [PAGE 3].

For filtering, a first-order low-pass response is
\[
H(f)=\frac{1}{1+j f/f_c},\qquad |H(f)|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}},\qquad \phi(f)=-\arctan(f/f_c),
\]
with \(f\) and \(f_c\) in Hz. Assumption: linear time-invariant preprocessing. Breakdown: if \(f_c\) lies inside the true movement bandwidth, real intra-step signal is removed; if \(f_c\) is too high, noise passes. The source demonstrates the practical scale: a 1 Hz filter can alter RMSD “from around 0.03 m/s to 0.08 m/s” [PAGE 8], and it recommends applying the same filter to criterion data [PAGE 8].

For synchronization, cross-correlation is
\[
R_{xy}(\tau)=\mathbb{E}[x(t)y(t+\tau)]\quad[\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}\ \text{for velocities}],\qquad
\hat{\tau}=\arg\max_{\tau}|R_{xy}(\tau)|\quad[\mathrm{s}],
\]
supplemented by mean offset. The source says “application of a cross-correlation and mean offset methodologies may present useful synchronisation approaches” [PAGE 7]. Breakdown: periodic gait can create multiple correlation peaks; nonstationarity makes the expectation time-dependent; large clock/coordinate offsets inflate both bias and RMSD.

For representativeness, a distributional audit can use
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\mathrm{comp}}\|P_{\mathrm{test}})=\sum_v p(v)\log\frac{p(v)}{q(v)}\quad[\mathrm{nats}],
\]
where \(p\) and \(q\) are competition and test velocity-bin probabilities. This is a formal audit metric, not a source equation; it is motivated by the source’s Figure 3 comparison of testing and competition velocity distributions [PAGE 5-6]. Breakdown: \(q(v)=0\) where \(p(v)>0\) gives \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}=\infty\); sparse high-speed bins make estimates unstable.
