<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
To rigorously quantify the phenomena described in [PAGE 233], we establish a formal framework integrating geometrical optics and information theory.

Let the visual environment be defined by a scalar field of luminance $L(\mathbf{r})$, where $\mathbf{r} \in \mathbb{R}^3$. Let the centers of rotation of the left and right eyes be positioned at $\mathbf{c}_L = (-d/2, 0, 0)$ and $\mathbf{c}_R = (d/2, 0, 0)$ respectively, assuming a symmetric inter-pupillary distance $d$. 

**Operator 1: Retinal Projection Mapping**
The projection of the visual field onto a specific retina $\alpha \in \{L, R\}$ is modeled by the mapping operator $\mathcal{P}_\alpha: \mathbb{R}^3 \rightarrow \Sigma_\alpha$, where $\Sigma_\alpha$ represents the curved surface of the retina parameterized by coordinates $(u,v)$. For a point source at $\mathbf{r}$, the image falls at:
$$ \mathcal{P}_\alpha(\mathbf{r}) = f \cdot \frac{\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{c}_\alpha}{|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{c}_\alpha|} \cdot \mathbf{n}_\alpha $$
where $f$ is the effective focal length, and $\mathbf{n}_\alpha$ is the orientation vector of the eye.

**Equation 1: The Law of Similar Points (Fusion Condition)**
James postulates that identical stimulation of geometrically similar points yields unitary perception. Formally, let $\phi: \Sigma_L \rightarrow \Sigma_R$ be the homeomorphism defining retinal correspondence. Fusion occurs if and only if:
$$ \forall \mathbf{r}, \quad \mathcal{P}_L(\mathbf{r}) = \mathbf{s} \implies \mathcal{P}_R(\mathbf{r}) = \phi(\mathbf{s}) $$
For parallel eyes viewing infinity (as in the starry vault example), $\mathbf{n}_L = \mathbf{n}_R = \hat{k}$. The mapping simplifies to horizontal translation:
$$ u_R = u_L, \quad v_R = v_L $$
This confirms that points sharing identical eccentricities relative to the fovea correspond.

**Operator 2: Information-Theoretic Mutual Information of Binocular Input**
We treat the signals received by the two eyes, $I_L(u,v)$ and $I_R(u,v)$, as random variables. The goal of stereopsis is to extract depth $Z$ from the correlation of these signals. The mutual information $I(I_L; I_R)$ quantifies the redundancy used for fusion:
$$ I(I_L; I_R) = \iint p(i_L, i_R) \log_2 \left( \frac{p(i_L, i_R)}{p(i_L)p(i_R)} \right) di_L di_R $$
When the law of similar points is perfectly satisfied and images are identical, $I(I_L; I_R)$ is maximized, representing perfect redundancy. However, introducing disparity (as in the artificial stereoscope setup) intentionally reduces mutual information in terms of absolute pixel match, but increases the *depth* information transmitted, provided the visual cortex can decode the disparity map.

**Equation 2: Accommodation-Convergence Dissociation Cost**
The footnote highlights the difficulty of decoupling accommodation ($A$, in Diopters, $D$) and convergence ($C$, in prism diopters, $\Delta$). Under normal conditions, they follow a linear relationship approximated by:
$$ C = k \cdot A + b $$
where $k$ is the AC/A ratio (typically 4-5 $\Delta/D$) and $b$ accounts for tonus. 
When viewing a stereoscopic slide at distance $z_{slide}$ with the eyes converged at a virtual distance $z_{virtual} > z_{slide}$, a mismatch $\Delta E$ (error energy) arises. We define a biomechanical cost function $J$:
$$ J(A, C) = w_1 (A - \frac{1}{z_{slide}})^2 + w_2 (C - \frac{100d}{z_{virtual}})^2 + w_3 (C - kA - b)^2 $$
Minimizing $J$ dictates the equilibrium state of the ocular muscles. The "difficulty" mentioned by James corresponds to the steepness of the penalty term $w_3$, which represents the physiological resistance to uncoupling these reflexes.

**Breakdown Regimes:**
1. **Singularity of Disparity:** If the separation of corresponding points exceeds Panum's Fusional Area (approx. 10-20 arcminutes horizontally), the equation $\mathcal{P}_L(\mathbf{r}) \approx \phi^{-1}(\mathcal{P}_R(\mathbf{r}))$ fails to yield a unique solution, resulting in diplopia.
2. **Vergence-Accommodation Decoupling Failure:** When the deviation from the line $C = kA + b$ surpasses the dynamic range of the ciliary and extraocular muscles, the perceptual system undergoes a bifurcation, forcing either relaxation of convergence (breaking fusion) or spastic accommodation (inducing asthenopia).

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The successful execution of the experiments detailed on [PAGE 233] is subject to rigorous vetoes originating from physical, biomechanical, and environmental layers. These vetoes can instantly collapse the higher-order cognitive feat of spatial unification.

First, consider the **Biomechanical Veto**. The extraocular muscles (medial, lateral, superior, inferior recti, and obliques) possess finite strength and range of motion. If an individual attempts to converge on a point extremely close to the nose (less than the near point of conjugate fixation, typically ~7-10 cm), the medial recti reach their physical limit. Even if the visual cortex strongly believes in the unity of the two images, the muscles cannot physically align the foveae on the target. This results in an exophoria or exotropia slip, vetoing the perception of a single object. Similarly, the footnote discusses dissociating accommodation. The ciliary muscle has limits to its contraction. If an attempt is made to focus (accommodate) on a very near slide while keeping the eyes parallel (zero convergence), the required ciliary tension may exceed the metabolic capability of the muscle or trigger a nociceptive pain response, forcing a break in concentration.

Second, the **Optical/Physical Veto** involves the depth of field and diffraction limits. In the ink-mark experiment, if the pane of glass and the objective point behind it are significantly separated in depth (large longitudinal disparity), and the pupil is dilated, the depth of field narrows. One mark will be crisp while the other becomes a blur circle larger than the resolution threshold of the fovea. The brain heavily relies on acuity cues for fusion; if the contrast of one image drops below a certain Weber fraction threshold, the system defaults to suppressing the blurry image rather than fusing them, effectively vetoing the binocular unity in favor of a monocular percept.

Third, the **Environmental/Lighting Veto**. James mentions looking at the "starry vault." This works flawlessly because stars are effectively at infinite distance, providing strictly parallel rays. However, if this experiment were attempted with bright, non-collimated point sources (like indoor LEDs) placed at intermediate distances, the slight divergence of the rays would introduce minute disparities. More critically, ambient light scattering within the vitreous humor or cornea (veiling glare) can reduce contrast. If the signal-to-noise ratio of the retinal stimulus falls below the threshold required for feature matching algorithms in V1/V2, the correspondence computation fails. The brain cannot find "similar points" amidst the noise, and the veto is executed: diplopia returns.

These vetoes prove that perception is not solely a matter of software (neural algorithms) but is strictly bounded by hardware constraints. The "faculty" James praises is actually a highly optimized negotiation between competing physical realities.
