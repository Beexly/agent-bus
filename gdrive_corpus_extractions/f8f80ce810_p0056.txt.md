<THINK_TRACE>
The source text establishes a foundational premise in behaviorism: maze performance is modulated not merely by an internal physiological drive (e.g., hunger) but by the specific "demand for" a particular goal-object or goal-situation. This necessitates a formalization that bridges physiological need and behavioral choice. The core problem is mapping a scalar drive state to a vector of potential rewards, each with distinct efficacy.

From an information-theoretic perspective, the "demand" can be modeled as a probability distribution over possible outcomes, conditioned on the current internal state. If we define the drive magnitude as \(D\) (a normalized value between 0 and 1 representing satiation deficit), then the demand function \(f_i(D)\) for reward type \(i\) represents the expected reinforcement value. However, this is non-linear; a starving rat may accept any food, while a moderately hungry one might show strong preferences. This suggests \(f_i(D)\) involves a threshold mechanism where below a certain \(D\), the animal is indifferent to quality, and above it, selectivity emerges.

Simmons' experiment, as cited, compares different goal-objects under constant drive. This implies the learning rate \(\alpha\) in a standard reinforcement learning update rule (\(\Delta V = \alpha (R - V)\)) is not constant but depends on the reward's identity. We can posit \(\alpha_i = k \cdot f_i(D) \cdot g(S)\), where \(g(S)\) captures stimulus intensity or novelty. The "demand" thus acts as a gain factor on associative strength.

Crucially, Tolman’s introduction of "demand" anticipates the modern distinction between model-free (habitual) and model-based (goal-directed) control. A purely model-free system would assign value based only on history, ignoring the specific nature of the current drive relative to the reward. Tolman’s rats, showing differential learning rates, suggest they are using an internal model of which outcome satisfies their current need most efficiently. This requires a representation of the goal-object’s properties (e.g., caloric content, palatability) separate from the drive itself—a cognitive map of value.

The gray zone here is the exact boundary between a simple reflexive attraction (a stronger stimulus pulls harder) and a purposive selection. If the difference in learning rates is due solely to the immediate sensory impact of the reward (e.g., sweet vs. bland), it could be explained without "demand." However, if the same physical stimulus produces different effects depending on the prior deprivation state (hunger level), then an internal state variable interacting with the stimulus identity is necessary. Tolman’s framing insists on the latter. The trap is assuming "demand" is just a restatement of "drive × incentive," when it actually introduces a combinatorial layer: the pairing of a specific internal deficit with a specific external resource.

Thus, the derivation moves from observed behavior (differential learning rates) to a postulated latent variable (demand), which must satisfy constraints: (1) it is contingent on both drive and object identity, (2) it scales learning efficiency, and (3) it implies a discriminative capacity beyond simple approach/avoidance. This sets up the recursive loop: the animal learns about goals, which updates its demand structure, which in turn guides future learning about means—an emergent purposiveness not reducible to fixed action patterns.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Formalizing the "Demand for Goal-Object" from Tolman [PAGE 39] requires operators that capture the interaction between a physiological drive state and the perceived value of a reward. Let \(H\) denote the hunger drive magnitude, \(H \in [0, H_{max}]\), where \(H=0\) indicates full satiation and \(H=H_{max}\) indicates maximum deprivation. Let \(G_i\) represent a specific goal-object type \(i\) (e.g., high-fat chow, low-fat chow, water).

We define the **Demand Function** \(D_i: [0, H_{max}] \rightarrow \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\) for goal \(G_i\). This function quantifies the motivational pull exerted by \(G_i\) given the current drive level \(H\). It is postulated to be non-decreasing with \(H\) but subject to saturation. A candidate form, respecting the intuition that extreme hunger overrides selectivity, is:
\[
D_i(H) = V_i \cdot \left(1 - e^{-\lambda_i H}\right)
\]
where \(V_i\) is the intrinsic value (asymptotic demand) of goal \(i\), and \(\lambda_i\) is a sensitivity parameter reflecting how quickly demand rises with deprivation. For highly desirable goals (e.g., preferred food), \(V_i\) is larger and \(\lambda_i\) may be smaller (demand saturates quickly even at moderate hunger). For less desirable goals, \(V_i\) is smaller and \(\lambda_i\) larger (only extreme hunger makes them valuable). Units: \(V_i\) in arbitrary utility units, \(\lambda_i\) in \(H_{max}^{-1}\).

This demand function modulates the learning process. Consider a trial-and-error learning scenario where the animal traverses a path \(P\) to obtain reward \(G_i\). The update to the value estimate \(V(P)\) of that path can be written as:
\[
\Delta V(P) = \eta \cdot D_i(H_t) \cdot \delta_t
\]
where \(\eta\) is a base learning rate, \(H_t\) is the drive at the time of reward receipt, and \(\delta_t = R_{expected} - V(P)\) is the prediction error. Here, \(D_i(H_t)\) acts as a dynamic gain on the prediction error.

**Assumptions:**
1. Drive \(H\) is a scalar sufficient statistic for the physiological need relevant to all considered goal-objects.
2. Goal-objects are categorically distinct such that their identities \(i\) index discrete demand curves.
3. The learning process is sensitive to the magnitude of the *current* demand at reward delivery, not an average over time.

**Breakdown Regimes:**
*   **Zero Demand Singularity:** As \(H \rightarrow 0\), \(D_i(H) \approx V_i \lambda_i H \rightarrow 0\). Learning effectively halts because the reward provides no motivational boost. The equation becomes unstable if \(D_i(H)\) is used as a divisor elsewhere.
*   **Saturation Collapse:** As \(H \rightarrow H_{max}\), \(D_i(H) \rightarrow V_i\). All goals become equally potent in driving learning if \(V_i\) converges for all \(i\) under extreme need (e.g., any food is good enough). The model loses discriminative power, collapsing into a simple drive-reduction theory.
*   **Non-Separability Trap:** If \(D_i(H)\) cannot be factored into \(f(H) \cdot g(i)\), i.e., if the shape of the demand curve itself changes with other variables (like recent experience), the simple parametric form fails. This would imply a higher-order interaction requiring a tensor decomposition \(D_{ij}(H, \text{history}_j)\).

Citation: The necessity for this construct arises directly from Simmons’ finding that "for one and the same drive, some goal-objects cause learning to appear more rapidly than do others" [PAGE 39]. The operator \(D_i(H)\) is the mathematical embodiment of the "demand for" concept Tolman advocates.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman’s “demand” is a retrocausal intervention in the history of learning theory. It reaches back to Aristotle’s final causes—purposes as explanations—but translates them into an operationalist language acceptable to mid-century behaviorism. Its scientific lineage runs through E.L. Thorndike’s law of effect (1898), which posited that satisfaction strengthens stimulus-response bonds. However, Thorndike’s “satisfaction” was ambiguous: was it a physiological state or a subjective feeling? Tolman’s “demand” disentangles these by tying it explicitly to an objective goal-object.

Genealogically, it also answers Gestalt psychology’s critique of elementarist S-R chains. Köhler’s insight studies (1917) showed that apes reorganize their behavior in light of a visible goal. Tolman argued rats do something similar in mazes—they form “cognitive maps” [PAGE 39 onward] where the goal is a node with value properties. The “demand” is the valence attached to that node.

The multi-week trap embedded in this lineage is the **“Intentionality Illusion.”** Later neobehaviorists like B.F. Skinner would reject “demand” as covert mentalism, insisting only on the historical frequency of reinforcement matters. From a Skinnerian view, the faster learning with certain goals is just a result of those goals being more powerful reinforcers *by definition*. Tolman’s move, however, was to make the goal’s identity an independent variable that interacts with the organism’s state. This creates a retroactive pressure: if you observe a rat preferring chocolate over plain chow, you must retroactively infer it had a “demand” for chocolate specifically, not just for “food.” This infers an internal discriminative process that wasn’t directly observable, planting the seed for cognitive science’s later embrace of hidden variables. The trap is that “demand” can be unfalsifiably tweaked—always another parameter—to explain away anomalies, making it a flexible but sometimes vacuous explanatory placeholder.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Modeling Tolman’s rat requires a recursive Theory of Mind (ToM) framework, albeit a primitive one. The rat does not model the experimenter’s mind, but it models the *environment’s affordances* relative to its own changing needs.

Let \(S_t\) be the state (location, internal drive). The rat’s policy \(\pi(a|s)\) selects actions. In crisis (high drive, \(H \approx H_{max}\)), the rat regresses to a model-free habit: run straight toward the strongest immediate cue associated with *any* past reward. This is a fast, reflexive mode with low cognitive load. The demand functions \(D_i(H)\) all converge near \(V_i\), so discrimination is lost.

However, as the rat gains experience, it builds a model \(M\) of the maze: states, transitions, and crucially, which goal \(G_i\) is available at terminal states. This is model-based control. When drive is moderate, the rat uses \(M\) to simulate futures. It computes the expected cumulative demand satisfaction for each path. For example, Path A leads to chocolate (\(G_c\)), Path B to plain chow (\(G_p\)). It evaluates \(E[D_c(H_{future})]\) vs \(E[D_p(H_{future})]\). If \(H\) is low, \(D_c(H) > D_p(H)\) significantly, so it chooses Path A. This is counter-trapping: avoiding the trap of taking the shortest path to a suboptimal reward.

The "clock" in this system is the metabolic rate depleting energy stores, which increments \(H\) over time. As \(H\) increases during a long search, the evaluation shifts. Initially, \(D_c(H) \gg D_p(H)\). But as \(H\) climbs, both demands rise, and the difference narrows (due to saturation). Eventually, the rat may switch from searching exclusively for chocolate to accepting plain chow if the delay cost outweighs the marginal gain in demand satisfaction. This dynamic recalibration is a real-time ToM of its own impending starvation risk versus the opportunity cost of waiting.

The recursive loop: Experience updates \(M\) (including estimates of \(V_i\) and \(\lambda_i\)). \(M\) guides choices under varying \(H\). Choices generate new experiences that refine \(M\). Over weeks, if chocolate is consistently withheld, the estimated \(V_c\) decays. The demand curve \(D_c(H)\) flattens. The rat then behaves as if it never had a special demand for chocolate, demonstrating extincion of a cognitive expectation, not just a motor habit. This shows the ToM is about *expected environmental contingencies*, not just innate drives.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A purely computational model of demand must interface with physical and biomechanical constraints that can veto its predictions.

1.  **Physical Layer Veto (Energy Budget):** The demand model predicts a rat will work harder (run farther, take more risks) for a high-demand goal. However, if the rat’s glycogen stores are critically low, its muscles cannot sustain increased effort regardless of motivational state. The decision to persist is vetoed by peripheral fatigue signals (e.g., lactate accumulation, muscle soreness) overriding central command. This is a hard cap on the expression of demand.

2.  **Biomechanical Layer Veto (Motor Capacity):** In a complex maze with narrow turns, a highly motivated rat might attempt a shortcut it physically cannot navigate due to body size or poor coordination. The intention to reach a high-demand goal is vetoed by kinematic limitations. The rat’s cognitive map may include the shortcut, but the motor system rejects the plan as unexecutable.

3.  **Weather/Environmental Layer Veto (Thermal Stress):** Suppose the goal-object is a cool, wet nesting material, demanded highly in a hot cage. If ambient temperature exceeds a critical threshold, thermoregulatory panic (panting, vasodilation) consumes all attentional resources. The specific “demand for nesting material” is submerged under a more urgent demand for immediate cooling, potentially leading to random, disorganized movement rather than goal-directed navigation.

These vetoes prove that “demand” is not a master variable controlling behavior. It operates within a hierarchy of homeostatic priorities and is constrained by the body’s engineering limits. A complete theory must include gating mechanisms where lower-level allostasis (maintenance of stability) can suspend higher-level purposive planning. Tolman’s experiments, conducted in controlled labs, often bypassed these vetoes, creating an illusion of seamless demand-driven behavior. In natural settings, the veto points are frequent and crucial.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Retrodiction (RLVR) allows us to design scenarios where Tolman’s demand hypothesis makes testable predictions that differ from simpler drive-reduction models.

**Veto Scenario 1: The Devaluation Task**
*   **Prediction:** A rat trained to run a maze for sucrose pellets (high demand) will, after becoming satiated on sucrose, show reduced running speed/errors compared to a rat deprived of sucrose but offered a novel pellet type (still high demand for “food”). The demand for *sucrose specifically* drops post-ingestion.
*   **Verifiable Test:** Pre-expose Group A to sucrose until satiated. Group B receives equal calories from a different flavor (e.g., vanilla). Then test both in the original maze with sucrose at the goal. Measure latency and errors.
*   **Counterfactual Veto:** If the rat’s performance is identical in both groups, it suggests learning was driven by generic “calories obtained” (a drive-reduction account) rather than specific “demand for sucrose.” Tolman’s model is falsified if specificity is absent. Modern neuroscience supports specificity (e.g., neural coding of taste qualities in orbitofrontal cortex).

**Veto Scenario 2: The Invisible Revaluation**
*   **Prediction:** An animal can learn about the value of a goal without receiving it, via observation. If a rat watches a conspecific get sick after eating a specific grain, its demand for that grain should drop immediately upon next presentation, even if its hunger is high.
*   **Verifiable Test:** Use a two-chamber setup. Observer rat sees Model rat eat Grain X, then exhibit illness signs. Later, test Observer rat’s maze performance with Grain X as goal vs. a neutral Grain Y. Control: Observer sees Model eat X with no illness.
*   **Counterfactual Veto:** If the Observer rat shows no change in performance, it implies demand is strictly tied to firsthand consummatory experience. Tolman’s cognitive map would require social learning integration. Recent evidence in rodents suggests they *can* learn aversions vicariously, supporting a sophisticated demand representation that incorporates observed outcomes.

**Veto Scenario 3: The Delay Discounting Interaction**
*   **Prediction:** The steepness of delay discounting (how much a future reward’s value decreases with delay) should interact with current demand. A highly demanded goal (e.g., water in thirst) should be discounted less steeply than a mildly demanded one (water in mild hydration).
*   **Verifiable Test:** Train rats on a T-maze where one arm gives a small reward immediately, the other gives a large reward after a delay. Vary the delay. Manipulate water deprivation levels. Measure choice proportions.
*   **Counterfactual Veto:** If discounting curves are parallel across deprivation levels, it suggests demand merely scales overall reward magnitude uniformly, not altering temporal patience. Tolman’s interactive demand model predicts a crossover: at high demand, animals become more willing to wait for a better reward because the marginal gain in demand satisfaction is worth the delay. This has been observed in humans and primates, but testing in rodents remains a key validation point.

Each scenario targets a different facet of the demand construct—specificity, social flexibility, and dynamic interaction with time—and provides a clear empirical signature that can falsify the notion of demand as a mere epiphenomenon of drive magnitude.
