The fourth step adds practical filters: an “Active Pressing speed threshold” that zeroes interception probability when defender speed is below threshold [PAGE 4], with Figure 2 showing “a speed threshold set at 2 m/s” [PAGE 4]. The fifth step admits a geometric failure: “The model assumes an infinite playing surface” [PAGE 5], so “situations where a player is being pressed towards the sideline but cannot be caught within 1.5 seconds are treated as pressure free” [PAGE 5]. The proposed repair is a sideline pressure term “acting opposite and equal to the force exerted by the attacking player(s) moving toward it” [PAGE 5].

The gray-zone audit must separate what is formal from what is a modeling choice. \(T_{i,j}\) is a time in seconds only if every term is dimensionally a time; the printed angular-penalty expression \(\tau_\beta(t)=\|\vec u\|\cdot\beta/\pi\) [PAGE 3] is a dimensional gray zone unless \(\|\vec u\|\) is normalized by a speed or the implementation supplies a missing scale. The logistic probability is dimensionless in \([0,1]\), but \(\sigma=0.45\) is given without an explicit unit; dimensional consistency requires \(\sigma\) to carry seconds because it is subtracted from \(T\) inside the exponent. The active-pressing gate is a hard discontinuity: a defender at \(1.99\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) can be assigned zero probability while one at \(2.01\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) can receive positive probability, even if the physical difference is negligible. The independence assumption is not a theorem; it is an approximation that fails when defenders coordinate cover shadows or when two defenders close the same passing lane. The fixed horizon \(T=1.5\ \mathrm{s}\) is not universal; it is a clock that suppresses pressure from defenders who would arrive later, and near sidelines that suppression is physically wrong because the boundary itself constrains the attacker. Singularities and breakdowns include \(v_{\max}=0\) or an uncalibrated \(v_{\max}\), near-zero \(\|\vec u\|\) or \(\|\vec v\|\) producing an unstable \(\beta\), arccos arguments outside \([-1,1]\) under numerical error, negative or nonphysical time differences, tracking-frame occlusion, missed acceleration/deceleration, fixed reaction time under fatigue, and weather/surface changes that invalidate the assumed maximum speed. The maximum-over-ball/player improvement on [PAGE 3] is also a semantic choice: pressure on the ball carrier is “the maximum pressure applied by player i on player #3 and the ball” [PAGE 3], not a physical union probability. These are the exact gray zones to carry into the later sections.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines a per-frame, pairwise, velocity-aware pressing operator. Let defender \(i\) have position \(\vec r_i(t)\) in meters and velocity \(\vec v_i(t)\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\); let target \(j\) be an attacker or the ball with \(\vec r_j(t)\), \(\vec v_j(t)\). Let \(\tau_r\) be reaction time in seconds, \(v_{\max}\) maximum running speed in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(\epsilon=10^{-5}\) a small dimensionless regularizer, and \(\beta\in[0,\pi]\) an angle in radians. The paper’s core time-to-intercept operator is stated as
\[
T_{i,j}(t)=\tau_r+\tau_{i,j}(t)+\tau_\beta(t),
\qquad
\tau_{i,j}(t)=\frac{\|\vec d\|}{v_{\max}},
\]
with intermediate vectors written on [PAGE 3] as
\[
\vec d_j=\vec r_j(t)+\vec v_j(t),\qquad
\vec d_i=\vec r_i(t)+\vec v_i(t)\cdot\tau_r,\qquad
\vec d=\vec d_j-\vec d_i,
\]
and the angular penalty
\[
\tau_\beta(t)=\frac{\|\vec u\|\cdot\beta}{\pi},
\qquad
\beta=\arccos\!\left(\frac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{\|\vec u\|\,\|\vec v\|+\epsilon}\right).
\]
The source’s exact wording is: “Ti,j(t) = τr + τi,j(t) + τβ(t)” [PAGE 3], “τi,j(t) = ∥⃗d∥ vmax” [PAGE 3], and “β = arccos(⃗ u ⋅ ⃗v ∥⃗u∥⋅∥⃗v∥+ ε)” [PAGE 3]. A dimensional audit is required here: the printed \(\vec d_j=\vec r_j(t)+\vec v_j(t)\) adds meters and \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and \(\tau_\beta=\|\vec u\|\beta/\pi\) is meters if \(\vec u\) is a position vector and \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) if \(\vec u\) is a velocity vector; it is seconds only if the implementation supplies a missing speed normalization. The paper’s intended quantity is seconds, because it is added to \(\tau_r\) and compared with \(T=1.5\ \mathrm{s}\). This is a source-level dimensional gray zone, not an invented correction.

The time is converted to an interception probability by the logistic operator
\[
p_{i,j}\!\left(T_{i,j}(t),T\mid\sigma\right)
=
\left[
1+\exp\!\left(
-\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}\,\sigma}
\left(T-T_{i,j}(t)\right)
\right)
\right]^{-1},
\]
where the source states: “Here, σ is set to 0.45 and T is set to 1.5 seconds” [PAGE 3]. Units: \(T\) and \(T_{i,j}\) are seconds; \(p_{i,j}\) is a dimensionless probability in \([0,1]\); \(\sigma\) must be in seconds for the exponent to be dimensionless, although the paper prints 0.45 without an explicit unit. The logistic is monotone decreasing in \(T_{i,j}\): smaller interception time gives larger probability. It saturates near 0 for \(T_{i,j}\gg T\) and near 1 for \(T_{i,j}\ll T\). It is not a calibrated turnover probability; it is a time-to-arrival transform.

Total pressure on object \(j\) is
\[
P_j=1-\prod_i\left(1-p_{i,j}\right),
\]
which the paper explains as “one minus the chance that none of the players i on defending team can get to object j” [PAGE 3]. Units: \(P_j\) is a dimensionless probability in \([0,1]\). The exact assumption is stated verbatim: “With this formulation we make the naive assumption that all these probabilities are independent” [PAGE 3]. The paper also notes an improvement: pressure on the ball carrier “is calculated as the maximum pressure applied by player i on player #3 and the ball” [PAGE 3]. That maximum operator is not the same as the independence-product union; it is a conservative/alternative aggregation rule.

The practical active-pressing gate is
\[
p_{i,j}\leftarrow 0
\quad\text{if}\quad
\|\vec v_i(t)\|<v_{\text{active}},
\]
where the source says “we introduce an Active Pressing speed threshold such that any chance of intercepting an attacking player is set to zero when a defending players’ speed is below this threshold” [PAGE 4], and Figure 2 is captioned “Filtered Pressing Intensity with a speed threshold set at 2 m/s” [PAGE 4]. Units: \(v_{\text{active}}=2\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) in that example. This is a hard indicator gate, not a smooth physical law.

Assumptions and breakdown regimes:
- \(v_{\max}>0\) and constant. If \(v_{\max}=0\) or is badly misestimated, \(\tau_{i,j}\) diverges or is biased; all downstream probabilities are invalid.
- Constant velocity during reaction time and full speed afterward. Turning, deceleration, fatigue, and biomechanical constraints violate this; then \(T_{i,j}\) underestimates true arrival time.
- Fixed \(\tau_r\), fixed \(v_{\max}\), and fixed horizon \(T=1.5\ \mathrm{s}\). These are not universal constants; they are calibration clocks.
- The angular term is numerically unstable when \(\|\vec u\|\,\|\vec v\|\approx 0\). The \(\epsilon=10^{-5}\) regularizer avoids exact division by zero but does not eliminate angle instability; arccos arguments must be clamped to \([-1,1]\).
- The pitch is infinite: “The model assumes an infinite playing surface” [PAGE 5]. Near sidelines, “situations where a player is being pressed towards the sideline but cannot be caught within 1.5 seconds are treated as pressure free” [PAGE 5]. This is a hard false-negative regime.
- Independence is false under coordinated pressing. Complement-product aggregation can overstate or understate total pressure depending on correlation structure.
- TrackIng-frame noise, occlusion, and velocity estimation error propagate nonlinearly through the arccos and logistic.
- The active-pressing gate creates a discontinuity at \(v_{\text{active}}\); small speed changes can flip \(p_{i,j}\) between zero and positive.
- The model does not encode ball flight, passing-lane geometry, or metabolic cost; the paper lists pass-lane pressing and “Metabolic Power” as future work [PAGE 5, PAGE 6].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage is visible in the paper’s own citations and in its problem statement. The older event-data proxy is PPDA: “Passes Allowed Per Defensive Action (PPDA) [13] creates a proxy for pressure using on-ball event data, where there is an inherit lack of positional information for non-on-ball players” [PAGE 1]. That is a retrocausal root of the current model: PPDA measures defensive action density without velocities, while Bekkers et al. reconstruct pressure from tracking positions and velocities. The StatsBomb rule-based pressure event is another ancestor: “They include a pressure event for every player within a 4 to 5 yards radius of the ball carrier” [PAGE 2]. The radius rule is a discrete ancestor of the continuous logistic. Robberechts’ pressing value, Merckx et al.’s effectiveness model, Andrienko et al.’s pressure formula, and Bauer and Anzer’s counterpressing detection are all cited as prior attempts [PAGE 2]. The paper explicitly criticizes Andrienko’s formula for “leaving out one of the more important aspects of pressure, namely speed” [PAGE 2]. That criticism is the genealogical hinge: the present model reinserts speed through \(\vec v_i(t)\), \(\vec v_j(t)\), and \(v_{\max}\).

The scientific lineage then runs through Spearman’s pitch-control model [PAGE 2], Shaw’s simplified implementation [PAGE 2], and Pleuler’s modification [PAGE 2]. The paper states: “Our approach to measure this Pressing Intensity is constructed with components from Spearman’s [12] Pitch Control model, Shaw’s simplified implementation of this model [11] and Pleuler’s modification of Shaw’s model [8]” [PAGE 2]. The retrocausal direction is important: pitch control was built to estimate interception probabilities for pass probabilities; Bekkers reuses the same time-to-intercept machinery for defensive pressure. The later “pressing intensity” use retroactively reinterprets the earlier pitch-control components as a pressure field. The paper even admits convergence: for pass-lane pressing, “At some point these types of calculations will simply converge back to the more complex Pitch Control model by Spearman” [PAGE 5]. So the genealogy is not a clean replacement; it is a simplification loop that returns to the parent model when passing lanes and boundaries are added.

The multi-week traps follow from that genealogy. First, PPDA and radius-based pressure were event-data proxies, so a tracking-based model can appear “better” simply because it has more positional information, not because its probability is calibrated. Second, the fixed \(T=1.5\ \mathrm{s}\) and \(\sigma=0.45\) are inherited simplifications; if they are tuned on one competition phase, they can drift over multiple weeks as opponent behavior, fatigue, weather, and pitch conditions change. Third, the active-pressing threshold at \(2\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) [PAGE 4] is a discrete classifier hidden inside a continuous model; over multi-week samples, small threshold changes can reclassify many frames and create artificial trends. Fourth, the sideline false-negative on [PAGE 5] is structural, not random; it will systematically suppress pressure metrics for teams that trap opponents against touchlines. Fifth, adding metabolic power [PAGE 6] changes the meaning of “smart pressing”: a high-pressure frame may be energetically wasteful, so a multi-week evaluation that rewards pressure alone can select unsustainable behavior. The correct genealogical audit is therefore to compare the model against its ancestors on the same tracking windows, with boundary-aware and independence-aware counterfactuals, rather than treating the newest operator as a monotone improvement.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The model is first-order physical: it estimates whether defender \(i\) can reach target \(j\) within the clock \(T\). It does not contain a recursive belief operator, and the paper does not claim one. The recursive layer must therefore be supplied as an audit, not as an equation. In a pressing situation, the defender is modeling the attacker’s next touch, the attacker is modeling the defender’s closing speed, and the coach is modeling both as a coordinated trap. The metric’s \(P_j\) is an affordance probability, not a belief. That distinction matters in three regimes.

Crisis habit regression: when \(P_j\) is high and \(T_{i,j}\) is small, the attacker’s policy can collapse to a habitual clearance or forced pass. The model sees the physical pressure but not the habit. A defender who is “almost” reaching the ball can trigger a rushed action even if \(p_{i,j}\) is moderate. Conversely, a defender who is fast but arriving under control may not trigger the same habit. The paper’s active-pressing gate [PAGE 4] is an attempt to remove passive proximity, but it cannot distinguish a controlled approach from a desperate one. The gray zone is that pressure is both physical and psychological; the metric only measures the physical clock.

Counter-trap: a defender may intentionally overcommit to bait a pass into a cover shadow. The model can assign high \(P_j\) to the ball carrier while the defender’s true intent is to let the ball move into a trap. This is a recursive-theory-of-mind failure: the defender is modeling the attacker’s belief that pressure is real. The metric’s maximum-over-ball/player rule [PAGE 3] can make the trap look like maximal pressure, even though the defensive structure is inviting the pass. A counter-trap audit would require a second-order variable: the probability that the press is a feint, not merely the probability of interception. The paper does not define that variable; it only defines \(p_{i,j}\) and \(P_j\).

Clock: the fixed \(T=1.5\ \mathrm{s}\) [PAGE 3] is the model’s temporal theory of mind. It says, in effect, “a defender who cannot arrive within 1.5 seconds is not pressuring the current decision.” But attackers and defenders do not share that clock. A defender may be deliberately delaying to cut a later lane; an attacker may be waiting for a teammate to arrive. The sideline case is the clearest breakdown: “situations where a player is being pressed towards the sideline but cannot be caught within 1.5 seconds are treated as pressure free” [PAGE 5]. A human defender knows the boundary is an ally; the model’s clock does not. The recursive audit should therefore separate (a) immediate interception probability, (b) boundary-constrained escape probability, and (c) opponent’s belief about the press. The paper formally provides only (a). Any claim that \(P_j\) captures “crisis habit regression” or “counter-trap” must be treated as an interpretation, not a source result.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is justified when a physical, biomechanical, or weather assumption changes the sign or magnitude of the model’s probability enough to invalidate the frame. The first veto is the maximum-speed assumption. The model uses \(v_{\max}\) and \(\tau_{i,j}=\|\vec d\|/v_{\max}\) [PAGE 3]. If wet turf, fatigue, injury, or a sharp turn reduces the effective maximum speed to \(v_{\max}^{\text{eff}}<v_{\max}\), then the true travel time satisfies
\[
\tau_{i,j}^{\text{true}}=\frac{\|\vec d\|}{v_{\max}^{\text{eff}}}
>
\frac{\|\vec d\|}{v_{\max}}=\tau_{i,j}^{\text{model}}.
\]
Because the logistic is decreasing in \(T_{i,j}\), this makes \(p_{i,j}^{\text{true}}<p_{i,j}^{\text{model}}\). If the true time crosses the \(T=1.5\ \mathrm{s}\) horizon while the model time does not, the model reports positive pressure where the physical agent cannot arrive in time. The veto proof is therefore an inequality, not an opinion: lower effective speed cannot increase the probability under this operator. Weather and surface conditions that reduce traction are a direct veto on uncalibrated \(v_{\max}\).

The second veto is the constant-velocity/no-turn assumption. The model uses starting velocities and an angular penalty \(\tau_\beta\) [PAGE 3], but a defender who must rotate the hips, decelerate, or change direction loses time that the linear travel term does not represent. Let \(\Delta t_{\text{turn}}>0\) be the additional biomechanical time. Then \(T_{i,j}^{\text{true}}=T_{i,j}^{\text{model}}+\Delta t_{\text{turn}}\), so again the probability is overestimated. The veto is strongest when the attacker is moving laterally or when the defender’s velocity points away from the target; the arccos penalty is only a proxy for that cost, not a full biomechanical model.

The third veto is boundary geometry. The model “assumes an infinite playing surface” [PAGE 5], so a defender who cannot catch the attacker within 1.5 seconds is assigned near-zero pressure even if the attacker is trapped against the sideline. The paper itself states that such situations “are treated as pressure free” [PAGE 5] and proposes a sideline pressure term “acting opposite and equal to the force exerted by the attacking player(s) moving toward it” [PAGE 5]. The veto proof is a counterexample: take an attacker at the touchline with an escape set constrained to a narrow strip and a defender arriving in 1.6 seconds. The model’s \(T_{i,j}>T\) gives low \(p_{i,j}\), but the boundary-constrained escape probability is much lower than the free-space probability. The model’s pressure is a false negative. Any adjudication that ignores boundary distance is invalid near the touchline.

The fourth veto is the active-pressing discontinuity. The source sets probability to zero below a speed threshold [PAGE 4], with the example at \(2\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) [PAGE 4]. This is not a physical phase transition; it is a filter. A frame with defender speed \(1.99\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and a frame with \(2.01\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) can be physically almost identical but receive different pressure classifications. The veto is a discontinuity proof: the operator is not continuous in speed at the threshold, so small measurement noise can flip the output. A biomechanical audit should either smooth the gate or report sensitivity to the threshold.

The fifth veto is tracking quality. Positions and velocities are estimated, and the arccos term is sensitive near zero vectors. If \(\|\vec u\|\,\|\vec v\|\) is near zero, \(\beta\) is unstable even with \(\epsilon=10^{-5}\). If the tracking frame rate is low or occlusion is high, the velocity estimate can reverse direction, changing \(\tau_\beta\) and the logistic probability. The cross-layer adjudication should therefore veto frames with high positional variance, near-zero relative velocity, or missing tracking, rather than treating the computed \(P_j\) as exact.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following three counterfactual tests are verifiable against an implementation of the source operators. Each test specifies a manipulated input, the expected output under the paper’s equations, and a prediction that can falsify a claim that the metric is physically valid.

1. Active-pressing threshold discontinuity test. Construct two identical frames with the same defender-target geometry, same \(T_{i,j}\), and same \(T=1.5\ \mathrm{s}\), but set defender speed to \(1.99\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) in frame A and \(2.01\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) in frame B. Under the source’s active-pressing rule, “any chance of intercepting an attacking player is set to zero when a defending players’ speed is below this threshold” [PAGE 4], and the example threshold is \(2\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) [PAGE 4]. Prediction: frame A must output \(p_{i,j}=0\) and frame B must output \(p_{i,j}>0\) (or the reverse if the implementation uses a strict inequality), despite the 0.02 \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) difference. If the implementation produces a smooth continuous transition, it has not implemented the paper’s gate; if it produces a discontinuous flip, it passes the source but fails physical continuity. The veto verdict is that the metric is threshold-sensitive and requires a sensitivity report.

2. Sideline false-negative test. Place an attacker near the touchline with a defender whose computed \(T_{i,j}=1.6\ \mathrm{s}>T=1.5\ \mathrm{s}\). The source says “situations where a player is being pressed towards the sideline but cannot be caught within 1.5 seconds are treated as pressure free” [PAGE 5]. Prediction: the unmodified implementation outputs near-zero \(P_j\). Now add a boundary-constrained escape counterfactual: if the attacker’s feasible escape directions are restricted to a narrow strip, the physical pressure should be high even though the free-space interception probability is low. The falsifiable prediction is that the unmodified \(P_j\) is lower than a boundary-aware pressure score in this frame. If the implementation’s \(P_j\) is already high, it must be using a boundary term not described in the source; if it is low, the source’s own limitation is confirmed.

3. Zero-vector angular instability test. Construct a frame where the defender’s velocity vector \(\vec u\) is very small, or where the target relative vector \(\vec v\) is very small, so that \(\|\vec u\|\,\|\vec v\|\approx 0\). The source’s angular penalty uses \(\beta=\arccos(\vec u\cdot\vec v/(\|\vec u\|\,\|\vec v\|+\epsilon))\) with \(\epsilon=10^{-5}\) [PAGE 3]. Prediction: tiny numerical changes in the velocity estimate must produce large changes in \(\beta\) and therefore in \(\tau_\beta\) and \(p_{i,j}\), unless the implementation clamps the arccos argument and guards near-zero vectors. A verifiable test is to perturb one velocity component by \(10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and record the change in \(p_{i,j}\). If the output changes by more than a predeclared tolerance, the implementation fails the physical-stability veto. If the output is stable, it is either well regularized or not actually using the printed angular term.

A fourth optional but highly diagnostic counterfactual is the independence test: duplicate a defender so that two identical defenders have the same \(p_{i,j}\). Under the complement product \(P_j=1-\prod_i(1-p_{i,j})\) [PAGE 3], the total pressure increases even though the second defender adds no independent information. The paper explicitly calls independence a “naive assumption” [PAGE 3]. Prediction: the duplicated-defender frame yields a higher \(P_j\) than the single-defender frame, demonstrating that the metric rewards redundant pressure. That is a verifiable veto against interpreting \(P_j\) as a calibrated turnover probability.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
