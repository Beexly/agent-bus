\]

\[

\]
with domain-knowledge inheritance required so that analysts can “evaluate the results of their analysis” and “validate the processes” [PAGE 10]. The paper’s Figure 1(a) describes this as a “performance analysis mapping” and “domain knowledge inheritance” [PAGE 31].

Preprocessing is a composition of operators:
\[
P=\Delta\circ\Sigma\circ E\circ\Phi\circ F,
\]
where \(F\) is filtering, \(\Phi\) is format conversion, \(E\) is extraction, \(\Sigma\) is structural conversion, and \(\Delta\) is descriptive conversion [PAGE 10–11]. For a successive multi-event sport such as triathlon, descriptive conversion produces differential time
\[
\delta_i^{(e)}=t_i^{(e)}-\min_{j}t_j^{(e)}\quad [\mathrm{s}],
\]
for each component \(e\). The source states: “pre-processing the times and converting them into differential times becomes crucial to data mining in the triathlon” [PAGE 12]. This operator is necessary because raw rank and raw absolute time can be misleading when events occur in immediate succession. The reported triathlon result is: “with a ∼87% certainty, a male triathlete can only win a medal if their differential running time is ≤26 seconds,” and for females “the certainty level is ∼86% and the affordable differential running time for winning a medal is 28 seconds” [PAGE 12]. These are probabilities or certainty statements, not additive time guarantees.

Structural conversion maps fine-grained ranks into coarse classes:
\[
\sigma_\theta(R)=
\begin{cases}
\text{medal}, & R\le 3,\\
\text{non-medal }4\text{--}10, & 4\le R\le 10,\\
\text{non-medal }>10, & R>10,
\end{cases}
\]
as used for track cycling omnium because of small historical data [PAGE 11–12]. This is a discretization operator; its breakdown regime is small \(n\) per parameter, where finer categories become unreliable. The paper says structural conversion depends on “the amount of data that is available” and that “small amounts of data pertaining to specific parameters may necessitate generalization of the values into more coarse-grained values” [PAGE 11].

\[
\hat{c}(x)=\arg\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\hat{P}(c\mid x),
\]
where \(x=P(r(s))\) and \(\mathcal{C}\) is a pre-defined label set. The source defines classification as predicting “group memberships for data instances” using “pre-defined and existing class labels” [PAGE 7]. Relationship modeling fits

\theta^*=\arg\min_\theta\sum_i \ell\big(y_i,f_\theta(x_i)\big),

\]
where \(d\) is a similarity/dissimilarity metric; the source defines clustering as finding groups whose members are “similar to each other” and “most dissimilar” to other groups [PAGE 7]. Association and sequential rule mining can be represented as

X\Rightarrow Y,\qquad
\mathrm{supp}(X\Rightarrow Y)=\Pr(X\cup Y),\qquad
\mathrm{conf}(X\Rightarrow Y)=\Pr(Y\mid X),
\]
with probabilities dimensionless and sequential patterns ordered by timestamp [PAGE 8]. These support/confidence expressions are standard formalizations of the source’s verbal association/sequential distinction, not additional empirical claims.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The paper identifies a communication gap between sports professionals and data analysts: one barrier is “the lack of confidence and mutual understanding between sports data analysts and professional coaches/athletes” [PAGE 5]. It also notes that “much of the coaches’ decision-making in sport has been based on tradition and emulation” [PAGE 4]. In a crisis, this creates three interacting policies: coach habit regression, analyst counter-trap, and clock pressure. Coach habit regression means that under time pressure the coach falls back on tradition and emulation rather than a complex model. The analyst’s counter-trap is to respond to mistrust with more model complexity or more output, which lowers interpretability and increases the chance of rejection. The clock is the real-time constraint: the source says real-time decision-making should “mainly be addressed using relationship modeling methods” [PAGE 19], and Table 2 requires “very high” interpretability with “very low” flexibility [PAGE 29].

A recursive belief model makes the failure explicit. Let \(b_C(\theta_A)\) be the coach’s belief about the analyst’s competence and \(b_A(b_C(\theta_A))\) be the analyst’s belief about that coach belief. If the divergence between these beliefs is large, the coach discounts model output and the analyst over-explains or overfits. The decision rule should be constrained:
\[
m^*=\arg\max_m \mathbb{E}\big[U_C(a(m))\big]
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
I(m)\ge I_{\min},\qquad
\tau(m)\le \tau_{\max},
\]
where \(m\) is the message or decision aid, \(I\) is ordinal interpretability, and \(\tau\) is latency in seconds. If \(I(m)<I_{\min}\) or \(\tau(m)>\tau_{\max}\), the advice is vetoed regardless of its retrospective accuracy. This is consistent with the source’s claim that “Processes that generate large amounts of output information, generally, need higher levels of interpretability” [PAGE 20], and that longer time-frames permit more flexibility: “The longer the time-frame of utilizing the results of the analyzes is, the more flexibility is desired” [PAGE 21]. In a crisis, the correct counter-trap is not to mirror the coach’s tradition or to maximize model sophistication, but to translate the model into RTS-level, actionable options that satisfy the clock.

The cross-layer veto is an omitted-variable and non-identifiability argument. Let the full physical state be
\[
s=(x_{\mathrm{phys}},x_{\mathrm{biomech}},x_{\mathrm{weather}},x_{\mathrm{tactical}}),
\]
and let the observed RTS vector be \(r=M(s)\). If a model uses only \(\tilde{x}\subset s\) and omits a variable that affects both \(r\) and the outcome \(y\), then
\[
\mathbb{E}[y\mid \tilde{x}]
\]
is biased relative to \(\mathbb{E}[y\mid \tilde{x},x_{\mathrm{omitted}}]\). The paper explicitly places “conditions” and “abilities” in the sports domain [PAGE 10] and defines biomechanics as “the application of mechanical principles to human’s biological system” [PAGE 4]. Therefore, biomechanical efficiency, injury status, fatigue, equipment, course, and weather can veto a data-mining conclusion if they alter the RTS generating process but are absent from the feature set.

The strongest veto proof is representational: if two distinct physical states \(s_1\neq s_2\) map to the same RTS observation,
\[
M(s_1)=M(s_2),
\]
but have different outcome distributions \(p(y\mid s_1)\neq p(y\mid s_2)\), then no classifier, clusterer, regression, or rule miner using only \(r\) can distinguish them. The source’s mapping from sport to data mining is thus only as valid as its domain-knowledge inheritance [PAGE 10]. Weather is not named explicitly; the paper names “conditions” generally [PAGE 10], so a weather veto is an external physical-layer test, not a source-confirmed result. If adding physical/biomechanical/weather covariates improves prospective calibration, the omission is harmful; if it does not, the omission is not veto-triggering for that dataset. The adjudication must be: algorithm choice cannot rescue a non-injective RTS representation.
