<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage traces from Pavlov’s experimental psychology (early 1900s; cited here as reference 16, p. 77 ff.) through Hull’s 1943 systematization on [PAGE 282]. Pavlov’s conditioned inhibition—where a neutral stimulus paired with extinction acquires the power to suppress an existing conditioned reflex—is the direct ancestor of Hull’s “negative habit” and Corollary I. Hull’s innovation is to re-describe Pavlovian inhibition in drive-reduction terms: inhibitory potential Ir accumulates and is conditioned to stimuli present at need-decrement, linking Pavlov’s salivary physiology to Hull’s broader postulate system.

Later descendants include Konorski’s distinction between excitatory and inhibitory conditioning and, anachronistically but informatively, the Rescorla-Wagner model (1972), in which a conditioned inhibitor acquires negative associative strength only when its presence predicts the absence of an expected US. Hull’s formulation anticipates this by making the whistle a conditioned signal of extinction rather than a signal of food.

Multi-week trap: Extrapolating from a single laboratory session (minutes, as indexed by 3:16/3:25/3:30) to durable human behavior change over weeks ignores three decay channels documented in the broader learning literature but not established on [PAGE 282]: spontaneous recovery (inhibition weakens across time even without re-exposure), renewal (the inhibitory stimulus loses power in a context different from extinction training), and reinstatement (an unconditioned stimulus can restore the extinguished excitatory response). A clinician or model treating Hull’s “negative habit” as a permanent deletion of behavior would overpredict suppression at week two or three and underpredict relapse following stress or context shift. The text provides no consolidation parameter, no circadian modulation term, and no context-binding variable, so any multi-week forecast derived solely from [PAGE 282] is under-specified and likely overconfident.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: Under acute stress or need-decrement recurrence, the organism tends to regress to the previously extinguished positive reaction. In Hull’s framework, the inhibitory trace \(S_{\text{whistle}} \to \mathcal{I}_r\) competes with the original excitatory trace \(S_{\text{tac}} \to R_{\text{defensive}}\). During a crisis, elevated drive can re-instate the excitatory pathway, causing the “habit of not doing something” to collapse and the suppressed response to re-emerge. This is not new learning but the regression of a crisis-vulnerable hierarchy.

Counter-trap: The practitioner may treat the whistle-like inhibitor as a reliable control device. The trap is believing that one extinction pairing permanently inoculates against the excitatory stimulus. Because inhibition is contingent on the original extinction contingency \((S_{\text{met}}+S_{\text{whistle}}) + \neg U_{\text{food}}\) [PAGE 282], presenting the inhibitor without maintaining the extinction contingency erodes \(\mathcal{I}_r\). The counter-trap is therefore to mistake the *signal* (whistle) for the *cause* (need-decrement-linked cessation conditioned to the signal), leading to over-reliance on a labile inhibitory cue.

Clock: The timestamps on [PAGE 282] impose a minute-scale clock on the contingency. Inhibition is strongest when the inhibitory stimulus co-occurs with or immediately precedes the excitatory eliciting stimulus (3:25 pairing with 3:16 and 3:30 baselines). Delayed or asynchronous presentation breaks the contiguity required by Hull’s law of reinforcement application, driving \(\Delta t_{\text{contiguity}}\) beyond the effective trace window and collapsing \(\phi\) toward zero. Recursive theory of mind enters when the observer attributes agency to the whistle itself (“the whistle stops salivation”) rather than modeling the third-order contingency: the organism has learned that the whistle predicts extinction of an expected outcome. That recursive misattribution generates overconfidence in the inhibitor’s standalone efficacy.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
