The exact boundary is operational: can a decision-maker specify a σ-algebra and a dominating reference measure μ such that dP/dμ is estimable from data? If not, Knightian uncertainty obtains; if yes but with sparse data, the problem is statistical risk with high variance, not unmeasurable uncertainty.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Formal operators and equations, with citations to [PAGE 246].

Risk operator. Let (Ω, 𝓕, P₀) be a probability space where P₀ is known either a priori or from statistics [PAGE 246]. The expectation operator is
𝔼_{P₀}[X] = ∫_Ω X(ω) dP₀(ω).
Unit: same as X (e.g., currency, seconds, dimensionless utility). Breakdown: none if P₀ is correctly specified and stationary.

Uncertainty operator. When no group of instances can be formed because the situation is "in a high degree unique" [PAGE 246], there is no identifiable P₀. Define an ambiguity set
𝒫 = { P ≪ μ : dP/dμ ∈ ℒ }
where μ is a σ-finite reference measure and ℒ is a set of likelihood densities. Knightian uncertainty holds when ℒ cannot be identified from data; equivalently, when 𝒫 is not a singleton. The decision criterion is maxmin expected utility:
V* = max_{a∈𝒜} min_{P∈𝒫} ∫_Ω u(x(a,ω)) dP(ω).
Breakdown regime: if 𝒫 = Δ(Ω) (all probability measures on Ω), the inner minimization yields min_{ω∈Ω} u(x(a,ω)), producing pure maximin and possible decision paralysis. Units: probability dimensionless; utility dimensionless or in cardinal units; information in bits H(P) = −∫ log₂(dP/dμ) dP when P is identifiable, otherwise undefined.

Singularity conditions. (i) n = 1: no repeated instances, so the likelihood L(θ) has no curvature and the Fisher information matrix I(θ) is singular (rank 0). (ii) Non-stationarity: P_t ≠ P_{t−1}; the reference class drifts within the decision horizon. (iii) Misspecification: truth P* ∉ {P_θ}; the posterior concentrates on θ_KL = argmin_θ D_KL(P*‖P_θ), and D_KL = ∞ if supports mismatch, which is a hard singularity.

Verbatim anchors [PAGE 246]: "the distribution of the outcome in a group of instances is known"; "impossible to form a group of instances, because the situation dealt with is in a high degree unique"; "opinions ... actually guide most of our conduct."
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical lineage. Knight's chapter [PAGE 246] belongs to the interwar probability revolution: Keynes's Treatise on Probability (1921) argued that many probabilities are non-numeric and only partially ordered; Knight operationalized this by reserving "uncertainty" for cases where no frequency distribution exists. The line continues through Ellsberg's (1961) ambiguity experiments, which showed subjects prefer known urns to unknown urns even when expected values match, violating subjective expected utility; Anscombe and Aumann (1963) restored a subjective expected utility framework via acts and lotteries; Gilboa and Schmeidler (1989) axiomatized maxmin expected utility over a set of priors, formalizing Knightian uncertainty; Hansen and Sargent (2000s) connected it to robust control and model misspecification in macroeconomics. Modern descendants include climate sensitivity ensembles, where structural model disagreement produces an ambiguity set rather than a single predictive distribution, and pandemic early-stage modeling, where n is too small to identify transmission parameters.

Multi-week traps. The atmosphere has a Lyapunov time of roughly 10–14 days; beyond that horizon, initial-condition uncertainty grows exponentially (δ(t) ≈ δ₀e^{λt}), so a precise weather distribution cannot be known [cf. Knight's "high degree unique," PAGE 246]. Treating day-15 weather as insurable risk is a calibration-window trap: fitting a distribution to the previous 30 days assumes stationarity that physics forbids. A second trap is regime-shift blindness: applying pre-crisis parameter estimates to a novel crisis because a reference class exists formally but its parameters have jumped. A third trap is over-precision under recursive social uncertainty: when agents model other agents who are themselves modeling them, the effective sample size for each belief collapses, converting calculable risk into unmeasurable uncertainty within weeks.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. Under acute stress, bounded agents abandon deliberative belief updating and regress to cached heuristics: the "crisis habit" is a zero-depth theory of mind in which counterparties are assumed to follow fixed scripts rather than to model the agent in return. Let μ_i^t denote agent i's hierarchical belief over others' strategies at recursion depth d. Crisis compresses d → 0, so each agent treats others as non-strategic nature.

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is to anticipate the opponent's regression without exploiting it exploiter-style: if both sides regress, coordination collapses into mutually destructive worst-case play (the maxmin corner of Knightian uncertainty). The counter-trap prescribes maintaining d ≥ 1—modeling that the other party is itself regressing—and choosing robust actions that do not depend on precise estimates of the other's utility function.

Clock. The decision clock τ imposes a hard deadline on belief propagation. If τ is shorter than the mixing time required for beliefs to converge across the hierarchy, the recursion is truncated at depth d̂ < d, producing systematic overconfidence in the precision of P₀. The exact failure condition is τ < T_mix(𝒫), where T_mix is the time for posterior uncertainty over the ambiguity set 𝒫 to shrink below the action-relevant threshold. When the clock binds, the agent must switch from expected-utility maximization to maxmin veto logic: refrain from any action whose worst-case payoff violates a hard constraint, regardless of expected value.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto. Decision theory cannot override hard physical constraints. Consider a structural element subject to load uncertainty. Even if the load distribution P₀ is known (risk in Knight's sense [PAGE 246]), the veto condition is absolute: if max_{ω} σ(ω) > σ_y (material yield stress), failure occurs with certainty, and no expected-utility calculation licenses proceeding. The breakdown is not probabilistic but deterministic: the safety margin is negative.

Biomechanical veto. Tissue injury is governed by force-time and strain-rate thresholds. If the peak strain rate ε̇ exceeds the failure strain rate of the tissue, rupture occurs; probabilistic tradeoffs between expected benefit and injury risk are void once the physical threshold is crossed. The biomechanical layer thus vetoes any action whose worst-case kinematic profile violates tolerance limits, independent of the estimated distribution of loads.

Weather veto. Atmospheric predictability is bounded by the Lyapunov time λ⁻¹ ≈ 10–14 days. Beyond this horizon, δ(t) ≈ δ₀e^{λt} renders any precise outcome distribution unknowable; the situation becomes "in a high degree unique" in Knight's terms [PAGE 246]. The veto rule: do not issue point forecasts or risk-priced contracts beyond λ⁻¹; treat the domain as Knightian uncertainty and apply robust, low-leverage actions.

Adjudication hierarchy. Physical/biomechanical constraints are layer-0 vetoes. Statistical risk models (layer-1) may operate only within the feasible region defined by layer-0. Knightian uncertainty (layer-2) applies where layer-0 constraints exist but no reliable P₀ can be formed. No layer may delegate its veto to a higher layer.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR = Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards/Constraints. Three scenarios:

Scenario 1: Autonomous ground vehicle in unprecedented freezing fog. Verifiable condition: measured visibility R < d_brake = v²/(2 μ g), where μ is tire-road friction and g is gravitational acceleration. Because μ is unknown under novel icing conditions, no historical distribution of μ exists (Knightian uncertainty [PAGE 246]); the layer-0 physical veto is: if R < d_brake(μ_min) for μ_min at the physical lower bound of friction, command immediate deceleration to a safe stop. Counterfactual without veto: the RL policy optimizes expected travel time using a prior over μ; as μ → μ_min, stopping distance diverges and collision occurs with high probability. Prediction: veto-equipped agent stops; non-veto agent collides when actual μ falls below the prior's lower support.

Scenario 2: Electrical grid operator during a record heatwave. Verifiable condition: ambient temperature T_amb exceeds transformer design temperature T_design by ΔT > ΔT_critical, where ΔT_critical is derived from thermal runaway physics. Historical load data cannot form a reference class for this unique event [PAGE 246]. Veto: preemptively shed non-critical load when T_amb > T_design + ΔT_critical, regardless of expected-load forecasts. Counterfactual without veto: the operator relies on a statistical risk model of load and keeps transformers online; thermal runaway cascades into regional blackout. Prediction: veto prevents cascade; non-veto incurs blackout with probability approaching 1 as ΔT passes critical.

Scenario 3: Pandemic triage algorithm allocating ventilators. Verifiable condition: the patient's phenotype lies outside the support of the training population (e.g., rare multi-morbidity combination), so the survival model's likelihood has no mass at the observed covariate vector. Veto: suspend algorithmic output and defer to clinician judgment under uncertainty. Counterfactual without veto: the algorithm assigns a point-estimate survival probability by extrapolating the model beyond its support; allocation is systematically biased against the unique phenotype. Prediction: veto yields outcome distribution matched to individualized physiology; non-veto yields excess mortality among out-of-support patients.

Each scenario is verifiable because the veto condition is a checkable inequality against physical or sensor data, not a bet on odds.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
