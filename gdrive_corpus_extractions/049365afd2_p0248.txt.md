<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: When the compiler or coder is under personal or occupational stress, the default retrieval schema shifts toward the more accessible negative-trait representation. Ambiguous behaviors are then coded as class 4 or 5 (“bad”) because negativity bias makes the “bad” category a cognitive attractor. This predicts inflated bad-temper marginals and an exaggerated spousal concordance in bad temper during high-stress coding sessions.

Counter-trap: A second-order analyst who expects crisis habit regression may over-correct by reassigning ambiguous entries toward “good” (classes 1–2). This leniency bias suppresses the bad-bad cell and can mask genuine assortative mating in negative temper, producing a false null result.

Clock: Let τ denote the lag between the observed marital interaction and the record entry. For short τ (e.g., < 48 hours), ratings are contaminated by transient mood (state), attenuating the true trait correlation. For long τ (e.g., > 6 months), ratings regress toward stable stereotypes of temperament, inflating correlation through shared rater schemas (common-method variance). The observed correlation can be modeled as ρ_obs(τ) = ρ_trait · f(τ) + ρ_state · (1 − f(τ)), where f(τ) is the trait-weighting function increasing with recall interval. Galton’s observation that compilers write “in much the same fair frame of mind that most people force themselves into when they write their wills” [PAGE 231] suggests a deliberate attempt to set τ to infinity and adopt a testamentary, stable-trait frame—but this very effort introduces its own reconstruction bias.
