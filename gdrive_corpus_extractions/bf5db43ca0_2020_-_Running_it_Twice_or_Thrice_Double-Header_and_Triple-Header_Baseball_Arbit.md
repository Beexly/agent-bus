<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s central move is a mechanism substitution: replace a binary final-offer award with a run-length-averaged award. The gray-zone audit must separate three objects that the paper sometimes blends: the poker analogy, the binomial/LLN probability model, and the finite-pool combinatorial error calculation. The poker analogy is rhetorical and historical; the binomial model is an independence assumption; the finite-pool calculation is a combinatorial average over possible arbitrator panels. The paper’s own Betances-Yankees example uses a five-arbitrator pool with three team-leaning and two player-leaning arbitrators, team bid \(B_T=\$3\)M, player bid \(B_P=\$5\)M, and reference mean \(\mu=\$3.8\)M. Under current final offer arbitration (FOA), a three-person panel deciding collectively is modeled as majority rule: seven of ten possible panels award \(B_T\), three award \(B_P\), giving mean absolute error \(\$0.92\)M. Under Double-Header Baseball Arbitration (DHBA), two independent arbitrators produce three team awards, six midpoint awards, and one player award, giving \(\$0.48\)M. Under Triple-Header Baseball Arbitration (THBA), three independent arbitrators produce one unanimous team award, six two-to-one team awards at the one-third point, and three two-to-one player awards at the two-thirds point, giving \(\$0.32\)M. The first gray zone is that “average error” is not a standard expected absolute error over a random sample; it is the arithmetic mean of absolute deviations from a constructed five-arbitrator mean over equiprobable panels. It is a useful audit statistic, but it is not the same as mean squared error, variance, or expected utility. The second gray zone is independence. The paper says DHBA uses “two different arbitrators, independently of each other” [PAGE 4] and THBA uses “three arbitrators each decides the case independently of the others” [PAGE 8]. But the same paper says a majority-vote rule “appears to be how MLB Arbitration is conducted” [PAGE 5], which is an empirical claim about collective panel behavior, not a guarantee of independent votes. If arbitrators share the same evidence, legal standards, and institutional training, positive correlation is plausible; then the variance reduction is smaller than the binomial calculation suggests. The third gray zone is finite-population sampling. The paper’s pool is “about 15 arbitrators” [PAGE 5], but the worked example uses five. A two-of-five draw without replacement is hypergeometric, not binomial. The LLN analogy is therefore an approximation. The fourth gray zone is behavioral: the paper claims DHBA gives players “confidence to submit a number that is closer to an ‘optimal’ number” [PAGE 6], but the evidence in Table 1 is descriptive, not causal. The fifth gray zone is tie-breaking. The individual arbitrator’s choice rule is unspecified when the arbitrator’s ideal salary is exactly midway between the bids; without a deterministic tie rule, the selection operator is undefined at that singularity. These gray zones do not invalidate the proposal, but they define the conditions under which its numerical claims are valid.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(B_T\) and \(B_P\) be the team and player salary bids in USD, with \(B_T \le B_P\), and let \(\Delta=B_P-B_T\) be the bid gap in USD. For arbitrator \(j\), let \(V_j\) be that arbitrator’s preferred salary in USD. The single-arbitrator FOA selection operator is
\[
S_j(B_T,B_P)=\arg\min_{b\in\{B_T,B_P\}} |V_j-b|.
\]
The paper’s binary-choice diagnosis is stated exactly: “The root of the problem is that because the award is a binary choice, FOA results in too much variance with (potentially) million-dollar swings in the outcome.” [PAGE 1] The DHBA operator is defined as: “If both agree on a number, then that is the award. If they disagree, then the award is the midway point between the two parties’ numbers.” [PAGE 4] The THBA operator is defined as: “If all three agree on a number, then that is the award; but, if the arbitrators split 2-1, the award is set at the applicable two-thirds point between the parties’ numbers.” [PAGE 8]

A compact formal representation for \(n\) independent arbitrators is
\[
K_n=\sum_{j=1}^{n} I\{S_j=B_P\},\qquad
A_n=B_T+\frac{K_n}{n}\Delta,
\]
where \(I\{\cdot\}\) is the indicator function, \(K_n\) is dimensionless, \(A_n\) is in USD, and the award is linearly interpolated between the bids. Under independent and identically distributed votes with \(p=\Pr(S_j=B_P)\), \(K_n\sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n,p)\), so
\[
\mathbb E[A_n]=B_T+p\Delta,\qquad
\mathrm{Var}(A_n)=\Delta^2\frac{p(1-p)}{n}.
\]
For \(n=1\) this is ordinary FOA; for \(n=2\) it is DHBA; for \(n=3\) it is THBA. The paper’s mean absolute error statistic is
\[
\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathcal C}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal C|}\sum_{c\in\mathcal C}|A(c)-\mu|,
\qquad
\mu=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{j=A}^{E}A_j,
\]
with \(\mathcal C\) the set of possible panels, \(A(c)\) the award under panel \(c\), and \(\mu\) the five-arbitrator reference mean in USD. In the Betances-Yankees example, \(B_T=3\), \(B_P=5\), and \(\mu=3.8\) in millions of USD. The paper reports FOA \(\mathrm{MAE}=0.92\)M, DHBA \(\mathrm{MAE}=0.48\)M, and THBA \(\mathrm{MAE}=0.32\)M; the corresponding quotes are “the average error under this process is $480,000” [PAGE 6] and “the average error is now reduced to $320,000” [PAGE 8].

Assumptions and breakdown regimes are explicit. First, if \(\Delta=0\), all awards equal \(B_T=B_P\), the variance is zero, and the mechanism has no effect; this is an exact singularity. Second, if \(p=0\) or \(p=1\), every arbitrator votes the same way, \(\mathrm{Var}(A_n)=0\) for every \(n\), and running the case twice or thrice cannot reduce variance. Third, if votes are equicorrelated with correlation \(\rho\), then
\[
\mathrm{Var}(K_n)=n p(1-p)[1+(n-1)\rho],
\]
so
\[
\mathrm{Var}(A_n)=\Delta^2 p(1-p)\frac{1+(n-1)\rho}{n}.
\]
As \(\rho\to 1\), \(\mathrm{Var}(A_n)\to \Delta^2 p(1-p)\) and the LLN gain vanishes; as \(n\to\infty\) with fixed \(\rho>0\), the variance tends to \(\rho p(1-p)\Delta^2\), not zero. Fourth, finite-pool sampling without replacement changes the distribution from binomial to hypergeometric; with pool size \(N\), \(M\) player-leaning arbitrators, and panel size \(n\),
\[
\mathrm{Var}(K_n)=n\frac{M}{N}\left(1-\frac{M}{N}\right)\frac{N-n}{N-1}.
\]
Fifth, if \(B_T>B_P\), the interpolation reverses and the operator must be redefined with ordered bids; otherwise the “midway” and “two-thirds” points are not well ordered. Sixth, if \(V_j=(B_T+B_P)/2\), the argmin is tied and the model requires a deterministic tie-break; without it, the selection operator is undefined at that measure-zero but real singularity. The units are USD for bids, awards, and errors; probabilities and vote shares are dimensionless; no information-unit quantity is defined in the source.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs backward from the paper’s proposal to three prior traditions. The oldest is final-offer arbitration in MLB, justified because it “incentivizes each of the parties to submit a reasonable number so that it will be selected by the arbitrator” and because it is “a fast and simple process” needed so that salaries are determined “before the start of the season” [PAGE 3]. The paper cites Tulis (2010), Monhait (2013), and Chetwynd (2009) for the institutional and legal background, and it notes that FOA is also called “baseball arbitration” because of MLB’s use of the procedure [PAGE 3]. The second tradition is the poker procedure “Running it Twice,” which the paper says was used on the televised program High Stakes Poker [PAGE 2, footnote 5] and which motivates the idea of averaging over multiple independent resolutions. The third tradition is the probability-theoretic Law of Large Numbers and binomial experiment, which the paper uses to argue that more runs produce awards closer to the expected award [PAGE 2]. The paper distinguishes itself from earlier FOA variations: “Nedelescu (2013) provides a literature review of previous proposed variations to FOA—such as Combined Arbitration, Double Offer Arbitration, Amended Final Offer Arbitration, and Alpha-Final Offer Arbitration—none of which are based on the ‘Running it Twice’ concept.” [PAGE 4, footnote 7]

The multi-week traps are institutional, not physical. The paper describes a file-and-trial strategy in which teams “refuse to negotiate with the players during the period of approximately one month between the date numbers are submitted to the arbitrators and the date the arbitration hearing takes place” [PAGE 4]. That one-month window is a clock that pressures players to settle before numbers are submitted. The paper reports that “89% of eligible cases in 2018 and 95% of eligible cases in 2019” settled despite file-and-trial [PAGE 4], and it cites evidence that in 2019 “the 191 settling players received an average of $90,000 less” than projected salaries [PAGE 4]. The upstream causal chain is therefore: binary award → high variance → team risk-bearing advantage → file-and-trial commitment → early settlement at team-favorable numbers → player perception of unfairness. The paper also records the rhetorical escalation: Jeff Berry’s memo called on players to “attack” the system and said players are “victimized” by it [PAGE 3]; the MLBPA Executive Director accused the league of making “sport” of arbitration and undermining “a process designed to produce fair settlements” [PAGE 3]; and The Athletic reported that teams were settling at “target” numbers “almost two out of three times” [PAGE 3]. The forward-looking clock is the CBA expiration in 2021: “With the MLB Collective Bargaining Agreement set to expire in 2021, this paper presents a new option” [PAGE 8]. The retrocausal lesson is that the proposal is not merely a statistical smoothing device; it is a response to a multi-year institutional drift in which teams learned to exploit the binary rule.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis habit regression is a recursive belief loop. Players believe teams will file low and refuse to negotiate; teams believe players will settle rather than risk a binary hearing; agents believe coordination can counter team coordination; and the union believes the process is being made a “sport” [PAGE 3]. The paper argues that the players’ ire is “misdirected as the teams are playing within the rules as currently constituted” [PAGE 4], and that greater player coordination is “both impractical (30 stable teams compared to more than 150 players and about 60 agents who come and go) and likely ineffective” [PAGE 4]. The habit regression is visible in bid behavior: the paper reports that in fully half of the players’ 2018–2019 “wins,” “the player submitted a number that was either equal to or less than the player’s projected salary” [PAGE 6]. That is a safe-bid habit: the player chooses a lower number to guarantee a win rather than an aggressive number that may produce a larger award in one state of the world. The paper’s illustrative arithmetic shows the trap: a player may submit \(\$1.5\)M to guarantee victory even though an “optimal” \(\$2.9\)M bid yields a higher average result, because the aggressive bid loses two of three arbitrations [PAGE 6]. The counter-trap is that teams may resist DHBA or THBA for the same reason “some of the most well-resourced poker professionals turn down requests from their less-resourced peers to ‘Run it Twice’” [PAGE 7]: the risk-bearing advantage is a profit center. The clock is both the one-month file-and-trial window [PAGE 4] and the CBA expiration in 2021 [PAGE 8]. A recursive theory of mind therefore predicts three reactions: players should become more aggressive in bids if the award distribution is compressed; teams should oppose compression because it reduces their bargaining leverage; and the union should support compression only if it believes the rule change is enforceable before the next CBA. The paper’s own claim is that DHBA “should give players the confidence to submit numbers that are closer to optimal because it spreads the risk over two arbitrations” [PAGE 7]. If that belief is not reciprocated by teams, the mechanism may be blocked at the bargaining table even if it is analytically sound.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
