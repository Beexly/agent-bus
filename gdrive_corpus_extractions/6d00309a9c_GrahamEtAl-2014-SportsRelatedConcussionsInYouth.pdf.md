The audit target is not a clinical guideline alone; it is an evidence-and-inference system. The supplied drive excerpt runs through [PAGE 180] of 357, so any claim about later chapters, appendices, or full-report recommendations is outside this audit except where the excerpt itself summarizes them. The first derivational step is definitional: the committee adopts the Zurich consensus language, quoting that “Concussion is a brain injury and is defined as a complex pathophysiological process affecting the brain, induced by biomechanical forces” [PAGE 43]. This matters because it blocks the old habit of treating a concussion as a transient “bell rung” event [PAGE 40]. The second step is epidemiological: reported rates are counts divided by athletic exposures, but the source repeatedly warns that underreporting and definitional drift make the numerator soft and that missing denominators make some surveillance data unusable for rate estimation. For example, the excerpt states that “NEISS-AIP data cannot be used to estimate injury rates per 1,000 AEs or relative risks” [PAGE 33]. A third step is biomechanical: linear acceleration alone is not a robust injury surrogate in non-centroidal impacts, because “the linear acceleration component does not describe all of the brain’s deformation response, and therefore when used alone, is not a robust predictor of injury risk” [PAGE 65]. The source then states that “higher rotational velocities and accelerations—rather than linear accelerations—can cause larger diffuse brain deformations and worse diffuse brain injuries” [PAGE 66]. But it also closes the gray zone: existing biomechanics are “inadequate to define the direction- and age-related thresholds for linear and rotational acceleration specifically associated with concussions in youth” [PAGE 83]. Therefore any exact youth threshold claim is a breakdown regime, not a finding.

A fourth step is metabolic. The source gives normal adult CBF as “50ml/min/100g” [PAGE 58] and says CMRglc “plateaus at 50-60 µmol/min/100g between 4 and 10 years” [PAGE 58]. It then says “there is a lack of data on changes in blood flow and glucose metabolism following mTBI and concussions in both males and females and at different ages during childhood and adolescence” [PAGE 73]. That is a hard evidentiary veto against using adult severe-TBI metabolic curves as if they were pediatric concussion curves. A fifth step is diagnostic. The source reports a prevalence-sensitive predictive-value trap: “more than 97 percent of the athletes the test identified as having a recent concussion actually would be diagnosed in error” when prevalence is under 1 percent [PAGE 124]. That is not a small statistical nuance; it is a gray-zone collapse of universal post-game screening logic. A sixth step is psychometric. ICC values are variable, and “there is no agreement on the minimum ICC value that is considered ‘acceptable’ for computerized neuropsychological tests” [PAGE 128]. Thus a claim that a test is “validated” because it has an ICC cannot pass without naming the composite, interval, population, and decision threshold. A seventh step is protective-equipment inference. The source says “There is limited evidence from epidemiological and biomechanical studies that current helmet designs reduce the risk of sports-related concussions” [PAGE 31], and “There is currently no evidence that mouthguards or facial protection, such as facemasks worn in ice hockey, reduce concussion risk” [PAGE 31]. It also states that “reductions in a specific biomechanical parameter, such as head acceleration, by a particular protective device do not correspond to an equivalent reduction in concussion risk” [PAGE 31]. That kills proportional-equivalence arguments. Finally, the hit-count gray zone is explicit: “implementing a specific threshold for the number of impacts or the magnitude of impacts per week or per season is without scientific basis” [PAGE 32]. The audit therefore must separate a sound concept—limiting cumulative head contact—from an unsupported numerical cutoff.

The gray-zone audit also exposes cultural non-identifiability. The excerpt says “41 percent of subjects reported not wanting to leave the game as their reason for not reporting a possible concussion” and “66 percent said they did not report their symptoms because they did not think their injury was serious enough to warrant medical attention” [PAGE 101]. It also says “Too many times the committee read or heard first-person accounts of young athletes being encouraged by coaches or peers to ‘play through it’” [PAGE 28]. Any model that treats symptom report as a clean measurement of injury severity will therefore fail. The correct inference is that self-report is both a biological signal and a social decision. That is the central gray-zone problem: the same word, “concussion,” refers to a pathophysiological process, a clinical diagnosis, a reporting event, and a policy trigger. The source’s own definition is intentionally broad enough to include all four, but the data are not equally strong across all four.

\qquad

\qquad

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage is a movement from dismissal to pathophysiology to policy. The source records the older habit: “Historically most concussions were not considered serious, and athletes who sustained them might be said to have been ‘dinged’ or had their ‘bell rung’” [PAGE 40]. The modern lineage runs through consensus definitions, with the committee choosing the international consensus definition because it has been reviewed and revised over time [PAGE 43]. That lineage matters because each revision changes the denominator of “concussion”: a broader definition captures more cases, which can look like an epidemic even when true incidence is stable. The source itself warns that the increase in reported concussions “may have been caused by a greater awareness of concussions” [PAGE 23]. This is a retrocausal trap: the observed time series is shaped by the definition and the surveillance system, not only by the injury.

The surveillance genealogy is layered. NEISS-AIP captures emergency-department visits for all ages but only primary diagnoses and no exposure denominator [PAGE 33]. NCAA ISS began in 1982 and can calculate rates because it captures athletic exposures [PAGE 33]. High School RIO began in 2005-2006 and also captures exposures but only in participating high schools with athletic trainers [PAGE 36]. The source says these systems are “the only ongoing, comprehensive sources of sports-related injury data, including data on concussions, in youth athletes” [PAGE 28], while also saying equivalent data are absent for younger athletes, club sports, and nonacademic settings [PAGE 47]. The multi-week trap is that a 2-week recovery window is clinically typical but epidemiologically incomplete: “Eighty to 90 percent of individuals who sustain sports-related concussions fully recover within 2 weeks following injury” [PAGE 42], leaving 10 to 20 percent with longer recovery. If a study stops at 2 weeks, it systematically undercounts the prolonged-recovery tail.

The neurobiological lineage adds another multi-week trap: symptom resolution can precede metabolic or cognitive recovery. The source says neuropsychological recovery “tended to lag 2 to 3 days behind symptomatic recovery” [PAGE 154], and NAA/Cr abnormalities can persist after symptoms resolve [PAGE 80]. Thus a “return to baseline” claim depends on which clock is being read: symptom clock, cognitive clock, balance clock, or metabolic clock. The source’s animal work on injury spacing reinforces the interval trap: “as the interval between injuries decreased, the duration of cognitive impairments increased” [PAGE 100]. The human corollary appears in the statement that “the time interval between concussions may be an important factor in the risk for and severity of subsequent concussions” [PAGE 30]. The long-term lineage is even less settled: “Whether repetitive head impacts and multiple concussions sustained in youth lead to long-term neurodegenerative diseases, such as chronic traumatic encephalopathy (cte), remains unclear” [PAGE 30]. Genetic claims also sit in a small-sample gray zone: APOE associations are preliminary and mixed, with prospective studies limited by small samples [PAGE 82]. The retrocausal lesson is that later severe outcomes cannot be read backward into every youth concussion without a measured exposure history, a measured interval, and a validated outcome definition.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis decision is recursive because each actor is modeling another actor’s model. The athlete asks: “If I report, will the coach think I am weak, will the team lose, will my parents be disappointed?” The coach asks: “If I remove the player, will the player or parents think I am overreacting, and will the team’s competitiveness suffer?” The clinician asks: “Is the athlete underreporting because of team norms?” The parent asks: “Is the coach protecting my child?” These nested beliefs can stabilize a nondisclosure equilibrium even when everyone knows the medical facts. The source documents the inputs: “41 percent of subjects reported not wanting to leave the game as their reason for not reporting a possible concussion,” and “66 percent said they did not report their symptoms because they did not think their injury was serious enough to warrant medical attention” [PAGE 101]. It also records the cultural pressure: “Too many times the committee read or heard first-person accounts of young athletes being encouraged by coaches or peers to ‘play through it’” [PAGE 28]. The recursive trap is that education can raise knowledge without changing the payoff structure. The source states: “Increased knowledge about concussions in the absence of changes in attitudes may not be enough to modify reporting behavior among athletes” [PAGE 44].

Crisis habit regression is the reversion to the pre-existing team script under acute arousal: “shake it off,” “don’t let the team down,” “the game matters.” The counter-trap is that a purely medical message—“when in doubt, sit them out”—can be heard as an external threat rather than a cooperative norm, especially where trained personnel are absent. The source notes that “the vast majority of young athletes practice and play in circumstances where trained personnel are not routinely available to make sideline injury assessments” [PAGE 100]. The clock is the third layer: symptoms may evolve over minutes to hours, and “50 percent of a sample of collegiate athletes who sustained a diagnosed concussion (with athletic trainers present for all games and practices) did not experience an ‘immediate or near immediate’ onset of symptoms” [PAGE 101]. A same-day clearance decision therefore samples too early. The source’s consensus position is that athletes “should not return to play on the same day as the injury” [PAGE 176]. The recursive theory-of-mind conclusion is that the safe decision is not merely a fact transfer; it is a norm shift that changes what athletes believe coaches, peers, and parents expect them to do when the clock is uncertain.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A claim passes cross-layer adjudication only if it satisfies four necessary conditions: a measured exposure, a defined outcome, a denominator or contrast, and a mechanism that is not contradicted by a higher layer. The physical layer vetoes same-day return after a suspected concussion because symptoms can be delayed and because early return occurs inside a possible window of vulnerability.
