Tolman’s 1932 account on [PAGE 1] sits at a crossroads between three traditions. From Thorndike’s Law of Effect (1911) he inherits the instrumental idea that outcomes “stamp in” connections, but he rejects Thorndike’s blind trial-and-error by inserting sign-gestalts as cognitive intermediaries between stimulus and response. From Gestalt psychology (Köhler, Koffka) he borrows the notion that the animal responds to organized wholes—“right-turning alleys” or “white alleys”—rather than to elemental sensory points. From Watsonian behaviorism he retains the vocabulary of “readinesses” and “habits,” but he redefines them as purposive, goal-directed dispositions rather than reflexes. The immediate consequence on [PAGE 1] is the theory of transfer: specific concrete sign-gestalt-expectations back-act upon general readinesses, which the animal then carries to subsequent problems [PAGE 1].

The downstream lineage runs through Tolman & Honzik’s latent-learning experiments (1930), Tolman’s cognitive-map paper (1948), the Rescorla-Wagner prediction-error model (1972), and modern model-based reinforcement learning (Sutton & Barto, 1998). In each successor, the same structural problem appears: when does a learned value or transfer, and when does it become a rigid habit? The multi-week trap is longitudinal rather than trial-by-trial. If a rat establishes a right-turn readiness in Week 1 and the experimenter tests transfer in Week 4 without re-exposing the animal to the original contingency, the readiness may have decayed, been overwritten by intervening experiences, or become fixated through overtraining. Treating Week-4 performance as pure transfer ignores the intervening weeks of uncontrolled experience. Conversely, if the apparatus is altered between weeks (different maze texture, lighting, or room cues), the similarity condition \( \kappa(s,s') \) may collapse even though the experimenter assumes continuity. The trap, therefore, is twofold: assuming that readinesses are “relatively permanent” [PAGE 1] across weeks while simultaneously assuming that the sign-objects remain similar enough to trigger them. Both assumptions can fail independently, producing false-positive transfer claims or false-negative fixation claims in multi-week designs.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock.

Recursive theory of mind applied to Tolman’s rat posits that the animal’s “mind” is not a single policy but a stacked archive of means-end-readinesses. Under normal conditions, the top layer reflects the most recently reinforced contingency. Under crisis—error, non-reward, or novelty—the system regresses down the stack to earlier, more deeply reinforced readinesses. In Tolman’s terms, the rat who has learned right-turn alleys and then encounters a blocked right arm does not immediately compute a new optimal policy; it regresses to the prepotent sign-gestalt “to-the-right-leading alleys are more likely to be thus good” [PAGE 1]. This crisis habit regression is recursive because each layer of readiness was itself formed by back-action from previous expectations, creating a nested generational model of what constitutes a “probable” means-object.

The counter-trap belongs to the observer, not the rat. An experimenter who sees the rat enter the right arm of a new maze may credit transfer of a generalized readiness [PAGE 1], when in fact the animal is simply regressing to a motor habit that happens to be reinforced by chance alignment. The counter-trap is misattributing structured cognitive transfer to an outcome produced by stochastic habit regression. The antidote is to manipulate the similarity condition \( \kappa(s,s') \): if performance collapses when sign-objects are made dissimilar, transfer was genuine; if performance persists despite dissimilarity, crisis regression to a motor habit is the better explanation.

The clock is the ratio of two timescales: \( \tau_R \), the decay/update timescale of the readiness, and \( \tau_E \), the timescale on which the environment changes the goal-contingency mapping. If \( \tau_E \ll \tau_R \), the environment changes faster than the readiness can update, and Tolman’s “relatively permanent” readinesses [PAGE 1] become maladaptive liabilities. If \( \tau_E \gg \tau_R \), the readiness tracks the environment closely and transfer is adaptive. The fixation phenomenon introduced at the bottom of [PAGE 1] corresponds to \( \tau_R \to \infty \): the clock stops, the readiness locks, and the animal cannot respond to a changed contingency. Thus the same mechanism that enables transfer across mazes becomes a trap when the clock of environmental change outruns the clock of cognitive updating.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof.

A veto operator \( V_\ell \in \{0,1\} \) is defined for each layer \( \ell \); if \( V_\ell = 0 \), the layer blocks behavioral expression regardless of the strength of the cognitive readiness \( R \). The adjudication hierarchy is strict: a higher-layer readiness cannot override a lower-layer veto.

Physical layer. Tolman’s mazes on [PAGE 1] consist of alleys with definite topology. If the right-turning alley is physically blocked by a barrier, \( V_{\text{physical}} = 0 \). The rat’s readiness \( R(\text{turn right} \mid s) \) may be maximal from prior learning, but the animal cannot execute the act. Physical veto proof: seal the right arm of Maze B; record first-choice distribution. Prediction: \( P(\text{right}) = 0 \) in trial 1 despite prior right-turn reinforcement. The readiness is cognitively present but behaviorally nullified.

Biomechanical layer. Even when the alley is open, the rat’s body imposes constraints: turning radius, whisker length, limb laterality, and body mass affect the ease of entering right- versus left-turning alleys. A rat with strong left-paw motor laterality may exhibit reduced velocity or increased collision errors on right turns, effectively \( V_{\text{biomech}} < 1 \) as a graded gain on the readiness. This is independent of Tolman’s sign-gestalt psychology; a “probable” right-turn alley [PAGE 1] is not equally probable biomechanically for all individuals.

Weather layer. Tolman’s laboratory description on [PAGE 1] contains no outdoor or weather-dependent manipulation; ambient temperature, humidity, barometric pressure, and precipitation are null variables for this source text. Therefore \( V_{\text{weather}} \) is undefined/not applicable to the claims printed on [PAGE 1]. If one extrapolates to field mazes, temperature below the thermoneutral zone reduces total locomotor output but does not selectively veto right-turn versus left-turn sign-gestalts; weather is a scalar on energy, not a categorical gate on means-end-readiness.

Adjudication result: For the specific phenomena reported on [PAGE 1]—right-turn propensities and white-alley preferences—the only operative vetoes are physical (maze geometry) and biomechanical (motor execution). Weather is not a relevant veto layer in Tolman’s controlled setting, and any model that treats weather as a decisive moderator of sign-gestalt transfer is importing an assumption unsupported by the source.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR (reinforcement-learning-from-verifiable-rewards) counterfactual veto scenarios with predictions.

Scenario 1: Physical occlusion veto.
Setup: Rat learns in Maze A that right-turn alleys lead to food and left-turn alleys do not [PAGE 1]. In Maze B, the right arm is physically sealed at the choice point. Verifiable reward: food delivery is contingent on entry; the rat can verify non-reward at the blocked right arm.
Veto: \( V_{\text{physical}} = 0 \) for right-turn actions in Maze B.
Prediction: Trial 1 shows perseverative right-directed head movements or attempts at the blocked arm (carried-over readiness), followed by rapid drop to \( P(\text{right}) = 0 \). Left-turn learning begins at chance; no facilitated transfer to left-turn is observed because the readiness cannot be expressed. Verifiable signature: latency to first left-arm entry exceeds that of a naive control group.

Scenario 2: Sensory degradation veto (lighting).
Setup: Rat learns in a discrimination box that a white alley leads to food and a black alley does not, establishing whiteness as a means-end-readiness [PAGE 1]. Test occurs in near darkness where the rat cannot discriminate white from black.
Veto: \( V_{\text{sensory}} = 0 \) because the sign-object property (whiteness) is not perceptible; the readiness \( R(\text{enter white}) \) cannot be addressed by the sign \( s \).
Prediction: Choice proportion \( P(\text{white}) \approx 0.5 \) in the first session; signal-detection index \( d' \approx 0 \). The readiness exists in memory but is behaviorally inaccessible. Verifiable signature: introduction of illumination restores white-alley preference within one session, demonstrating that the readiness was latent, not extinguished.

Scenario 3: Fixation/overtraining veto.
Setup: Rat receives 500 trials of white-alley-correct training (overtraining), far beyond asymptote. Contingency is then reversed: black-alley-correct, white-alley non-rewarded.
Veto: \( V_{\text{fixation}} = 1 \), modeled as \( \alpha \to 0 \) or \( \nabla_R \mathcal{L} \to 0 \); the readiness is locked as an attractor [PAGE 1].
Prediction: Perseverative white-alley choices persist through at least 20 consecutive non-rewarded trials; trials-to-criterion on black exceed those of a minimally trained control group. Negative transfer: initial performance on black is worse than chance because the white readiness actively suppresses the black alternative. Verifiable signature: run lengths of white choices after reversal follow a geometric decay with a much larger mean than in control groups.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
