<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The document is, at its core, a theory about what radar operators *fail to perceive*. The report models the operator as a detector whose reporting behavior is endogenous to the interference itself — a recursive loop where the harm suppresses its own evidence.

Habit regression. Under normal operation, operators build a mental baseline: marginal targets near the noise floor intermittently appear and fade. The report's central operational finding weaponizes this habit. At low I/N (typically < +3 dB), "target losses were not accompanied by any other indications that interference was occurring. Were such losses to occur during normal radar operations, the operators would probably not be aware that targets that should otherwise have been detected were missing" [PAGE 57]. The operator's trained regression-to-baseline treats interference-induced dropouts as the same benign fading they habitually dismiss. CFAR compounds this: as noise/interference rises, "operators will often be unaware that CFAR-controlled detection thresholds are gradually creeping upward" [PAGE 18] — the machine's threshold migrates beneath conscious notice.

Counter-trap (the anti-detection mechanism). The report's authors, uniquely, substituted themselves as the detection instrument. Sanders manually counted targets rather than trusting the built-in tracker, because "a human operator... was more skilled at identifying and discriminating radar targets than the automated routines" [PAGE 36]. But they acknowledge this choice biased the test *toward tolerating more interference before documenting degradation* [PAGE 37] — meaning the published thresholds are conservative (real-world degradation could begin earlier). The counter-trap is the deliberate adoption of a fixed 90%-Pd baseline so that "meaningful performance tests" are possible against a stable reference [PAGE 33].

The clock. Two clocks interact. The CFAR clock: "the radar took 8–10 scans before it would reach a new steady state after the target power was adjusted" [PAGE 65], forcing a mandatory 10-scan settling wait between every I/N step to avoid cross-contamination of measurements. The live-sky clock: interference toggling showed "a two-scan lag between the moment that interference was switched on or off and the moment at which the resulting effect was observed" [PAGE 48]. An operator's cognitive refresh cycle is far slower than a 2.3–2.5 s maritime antenna rotation [PAGE 106, 108]; the human clock cannot track scan-to-scan dropout statistics, guaranteeing under-reporting. The report closes the loop: since low-level interference "is not expected to be identified or to generate reports when it occurs, lack of such reports cannot be taken to mean that such interference does not occur" [PAGE 20] — an explicitly self-sealing epistemic hazard.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A "veto" here is any cross-layer factor that overrides a purely RF-domain I/N threshold prediction. Three layers adjudicate.

Physical / RF-front-end veto. The I/N calibration framework (Eqs. 9–12) assumes linearity, but the front end has a hard overload ceiling independent of duty cycle. Radar F's LNA gain-compression point is "−43 dBm at the LNA input" and "The radar receiver interference suppression circuitry and software cannot mitigate the effects of receiver saturation" [PAGE 113]. At the inadvertent +60 dB I/N injection, "the LNA at the radar RF front end was gain-compressed and radar performance was seriously compromised" [PAGE 92]. Proof: at extreme pulsed I/N, the OTR log-scaling [Eq. 10b] is physically vetoed by saturation — the response transitions from graceful (I+N)/N addition [Figure 2] to hard clipping, a distinct singularity regime the linear I/N metric cannot represent.

Biomechanical / operator-vision veto. The maritime qualitative results are explicitly gated by human visual limits. The report concedes "Determining an exact boundary for acceptable levels of interference effects for these types of radars can be somewhat subjective due to such variables as the eyesight and experience of radar operators, possible fatigue due to staring at PPI displays for long periods, and grading of the brightness and distinguishability of the targets" [PAGE 115]. Proof: test observers "knew in advance where the targets would be displayed and that knowledge made it easier for them to distinguish the targets from interference speckling" [PAGE 109], whereas real operators lack this. Thus the biomechanical layer *vetoes downward* the reported thresholds for real-world deployment — a trained-with-hint threshold is optimistic versus an unaided operator. Mitigation is partially restored by using manufacturer engineers and MCA instructors who "all concurred with the results" [PAGE 115].

Weather / propagation veto. For the fixed meteorological radar, the mission product itself is weather, so attenuation, clutter, and the receiver's own noise-removal processing veto the clean I/N model. The system processes signals below the noise floor (usable to −3 dB SNR) [PAGE 69], but residual noise removal introduces a "divergence... at −6 dB" between injected and detected interference [PAGE 76], corrupting the spectrum-width 1 m/s determination and forcing the Table 11 algebraic correction [PAGE 79]. Proof: the theoretical −14.4 dB spectrum-width vulnerability [PAGE 91] is not presently observable — the radar is "presently sensitive to interference down to an I/N of approximately −6 dB" [PAGE 82], the divergence masking the deeper theoretical limit until processing upgrades land. The weather layer thus vetoes the theory-to-measurement mapping over the band −6 to −14.4 dB, resolving only after coherent integration + frequency-domain detection deliver the promised net +10 dB [PAGE 82]. Separately, frequency agility vetoes interference entirely for the airborne set: the radar "ceases operations on a frequency on which interference is detected" [PAGE 134].
