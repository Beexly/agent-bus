The source creates a translational gap that must be audited as a chain of measurement, mechanics, and exposure ecology. The laboratory claim is direct: the padded shell cover reduces angular kinematic and injury-risk metrics in controlled impacts. The exact source statement is: “The addition of the padded helmet shell cover was effective in attenuating the magnitude of angular head accelerations and two brain injury risk metrics (DAMAGE, HARM) across most laboratory impact conditions, but did not significantly attenuate linear head accelerations for all helmets.” [PAGE 1] The field claim is null: “However, on the field, no significant differences in any measure of head impact magnitude were observed between the bare helmet impacts and padded helmet impacts.” [PAGE 1]

The first multi-hop derivation is mechanical. The padded cover is described as adding compressible exterior foam, reducing exterior friction, and permitting independent rotation along the shell [PAGE 2]. The authors later infer that the dominant mechanism is not linear force attenuation: “the primary mechanism of impact attenuation offered by the padded helmet shell cover is not linear force attenuation; rather, it is the low surface friction and independent movement from the helmet shell that consistently redirects impact energy throughout extended use.” [PAGE 10] This matters because angular acceleration is the pathway most associated with brain injury risk in the source’s reasoning: “Considering that angular accelerations are believed to contribute more to brain injury risk than linear accelerations” [PAGE 9]. Therefore, a technology that primarily changes rotational coupling can reduce PAA, DAMAGE, and HARM without reliably reducing PLA.

The second multi-hop derivation is exposure composition. In the laboratory, the cover is equipped to the helmet and to the linear impactor end cap: “an identical padded helmet shell cover was equipped to the end cap of the linear impactor.” [PAGE 3] That is a best-case shell-contact geometry. In the field, the cover leaves the facemask unmodified. The source reports: “Both players’ facemasks were considered the primary point of helmet contact in 37% of the bare helmet impacts and 67% of the padded helmet impacts.” [PAGE 8] It also states: “Over half of the impacts recorded in our on-field dataset of athletes wearing padded helmet shell covers involved both athletes’ facemasks as a primary point of contact.” [PAGE 10] If a pure facemask-to-facemask impact is unchanged, then the field effect is diluted by the facemask fraction. In the padded dataset, at most about one-third of verified impacts could be pure shell-class events under the authors’ classification, because 67% had both facemasks as primary contact [PAGE 8]. This creates a gray-zone inference: a real shell-only laboratory benefit could become statistically invisible in a small mixed-location field dataset.

The third multi-hop derivation is statistical power and exposure control. The on-field analysis retained only helmet-to-helmet impacts and used Mann-Whitney tests: “Mann-Whitney test results revealed that there were no significant differences between the bare and padded helmet impacts in any metric (PLA: p = 0.394; PAA: p = 0.245; DAMAGE: p = 0.093; HARM: p = 0.398).” [PAGE 8] The medians moved in the expected direction for PAA, DAMAGE, and HARM, but not enough to cross the significance threshold. The source acknowledges that a larger sample might have yielded significance, and that video resolution was insufficient to separate pure facemask impacts from mixed shell-facemask impacts [PAGE 10]. Therefore, the field null is not a proof of zero effect; it is a bounded inference under small sample, exposure mixing, and classification uncertainty.

The fourth gray-zone audit concerns temporal and environmental boundary conditions. The laboratory repeated-impact test used 80-second intervals and found stable HARM: “HARM values from full helmet repeated impacts ranged from 1.55 to 1.79; results of a simple linear regression showed that the regression line slope was not significantly different from zero and impact count was not associated with HARM magnitude (R2 = 0.04187, p = 0.830)” [PAGE 8]. But the source warns that real impacts may occur at shorter intervals [PAGE 12]. Temperature testing found a 13% difference between cold and hot peak force: “the 13% difference between means in peak force at the cold and hot temperature conditions was statistically significant (p = 0.011)” [PAGE 8]. This is not a catastrophic failure, but it is enough to require conditional predictions for cold-weather or hot-weather deployments.

The final audit point is that the source’s strongest claim is mechanistic, not clinical. No diagnosed concussions occurred in the field dataset, and the study did not assess concussion incidence [PAGE 11]. The laboratory metrics are surrogates: PLA, PAA, DAMAGE, and HARM. Their relationship to actual brain injury is probabilistic and model-dependent. Therefore, the correct frontier conclusion is: padded shell covers are physically plausible angular-attenuation devices in controlled shell impacts, but field efficacy remains unresolved because of facemask contamination, small sample size, temporal confounding, and unverified behavioral adaptation.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
1. Laboratory velocity operator and tolerance.

The source specifies three target velocities and a tight tolerance:

\[
v_{\text{target}} \in \{3.5, 5.5, 7.4\}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}
\]

\[
\frac{|v_{\text{measured}} - v_{\text{target}}|}{v_{\text{target}}} \le 0.02
\]

Citation: “All impacts were within +/- 2% of the target impact velocity” [PAGE 3]. Units: velocity in meters per second. Breakdown regime: if the velocity gate fails or the impact is oblique, the normal component of velocity may differ from the gate value, causing mis-specified energy input.

2. Sensor sampling and filtering operators.

Laboratory impacts captured linear acceleration and angular velocity at 20,000 Hz [PAGE 4]. Field mouthguards recorded linear acceleration at 1000 Hz and angular velocity at 8000 Hz [PAGE 5]. Laboratory signals were filtered by a fourth-order Butterworth filter at 300 Hz [PAGE 4]. Field signals were filtered by a fourth-order Butterworth filter at 160 Hz [PAGE 5].

Let \(a_{\text{raw}}(t)\) be raw linear acceleration and \(\alpha_{\text{raw}}(t)\) be raw angular acceleration. The filtered signals are:

\[
a_f(t) = \mathcal{B}_{4, f_c}[a_{\text{raw}}](t)
\]

\[
\alpha_f(t) = \mathcal{B}_{4, f_c}[\alpha_{\text{raw}}](t)
\]

where \(f_c = 300\ \mathrm{Hz}\) for laboratory data and \(f_c = 160\ \mathrm{Hz}\) for field data. Units: \(a_f\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), \(\alpha_f\) in \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}\). Breakdown regime: filter cutoff mismatch can create non-comparable peak values, especially for short-duration impacts.

3. Angular acceleration from angular velocity.

The source says a five-point stencil derivative was used [PAGE 4]. For sampled angular velocity \(\omega_i\) at sampling interval \(\Delta t\), a standard five-point first derivative is:

\[
\alpha_i \approx \frac{-\omega_{i+2} + 8\omega_{i+1} - 8\omega_{i-1} + \omega_{i-2}}{12\Delta t}
\]

Units: \(\omega\) in \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\), \(\Delta t\) in seconds, \(\alpha_i\) in \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}\). Assumptions: uniform sampling, sufficiently high sampling rate, negligible quantization noise. Breakdown regime: near impact onset, derivative amplification can make peak angular acceleration sensitive to noise and filtering.

4. Peak linear acceleration and peak angular acceleration.

The source defines peak linear acceleration and peak angular acceleration as extracted metrics [PAGE 4]. A formal resultant-peak operator is:

\[
\mathrm{PLA} = \max_{t \in W} \left\| a_{\text{cog}}(t) \right\|_2
\]

\[
\mathrm{PAA} = \max_{t \in W} \left\| \alpha(t) \right\|_2
\]

where \(W\) is the verified impact window. Units: PLA in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\); PAA in \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}\). The source reports PLA in \(g\), where:

\[
1g = 9.80665\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}
\]

Thus, on-field bare helmet median PLA of 23.61 \(g\) is approximately \(231.5\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), and padded helmet median PLA of 24.21 \(g\) is approximately \(237.4\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) [PAGE 8]. Breakdown regime: PLA is not a direct brain-injury criterion; it is a kinematic surrogate.

5. DAMAGE operator.

The source states that DAMAGE was calculated according to Gabler et al. to estimate maximum brain strain [PAGE 4]. A formal strain-based definition is:

\[
\mathrm{DAMAGE} = \max_{t \in W} \lambda_{\max}\left( \varepsilon(t) \right)
\]

where \(\varepsilon(t)\) is the brain strain tensor and \(\lambda_{\max}\) is the maximum principal strain. Units: dimensionless strain. The strain tensor is estimated from filtered linear and angular kinematics through a head-brain kinematic transfer model. Assumptions: the transfer model is valid for the impact direction, magnitude, and headform or athlete population; the brain is treated as a viscoelastic continuum; the kinematic input is not truncated before peak strain occurs.

Breakdown regime: DAMAGE is model-dependent. If the 200 ms mouthguard window truncates late brain deformation, or if the filter removes energy relevant to strain rate, DAMAGE may be biased [PAGE 5].

6. HARM operator.

The source states that HARM was calculated according to Bailey et al. [PAGE 4]. The source does not print the algebraic HARM formula, so the safe formal operator is a monotone composite risk score:

\[
\mathrm{HARM} = G\left( a_{\text{peak}}, \alpha_{\text{peak}} \right)
\]

where \(G\) is an increasing function of linear and angular kinematic severity, calibrated to concussion-associated impact response. Units: dimensionless score. Assumptions: the version of HARM is fixed; the same filtering and windowing rules are used; the score is not directly interpretable as a probability unless explicitly calibrated as such.

Breakdown regime: HARM values are not interchangeable across sensor systems, filter cutoffs, impact locations, or metric versions. The source itself compares laboratory and field data only qualitatively after separate filtering regimes: 300 Hz laboratory versus 160 Hz field [PAGE 4][PAGE 5].

7. Percentage attenuation operator.

For any metric \(X \in \{\mathrm{PLA}, \mathrm{PAA}, \mathrm{DAMAGE}, \mathrm{HARM}\}\), the source reports percentage reduction as:

\[
R_X = 100 \times \frac{X_{\text{bare}} - X_{\text{padded}}}{X_{\text{bare}}}
\]

Units: percent. Breakdown regime: if \(X_{\text{bare}} = 0\), the expression is undefined. If \(X_{\text{padded}} > X_{\text{bare}}\), \(R_X\) is negative. The source reports negative ranges: “HARM values were reduced in laboratory impact tests by an average of 25% at 3.5 m/s (range: 9.7 – 39.6%), 18% at 5.5 m/s (range: -5.5% – 40.5), and 10% at 7.4 m/s (range: -6.0 – 31.0%).” [PAGE 7]

8. Two-way ANOVA operator for laboratory attenuation.

For laboratory shell and facemask tests, the source used two-way ANOVAs with impact condition and padded cover use [PAGE 5]. A formal model is:

\[
Y_{ijk} = \mu + L_i + C_j + (LC)_{ij} + \varepsilon_{ijk}
\]

where \(Y_{ijk}\) is one of PLA, PAA, DAMAGE, or HARM; \(L_i\) is impact condition; \(C_j\) is cover use; \((LC)_{ij}\) is interaction; and \(\varepsilon_{ijk}\) is residual error. Units of \(Y\) follow the metric: \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}\), or dimensionless for DAMAGE and HARM. Breakdown regime: if the interaction is significant, the effect of cover use depends on impact condition and cannot be summarized by a single main effect.

9. Repeated-impact regression operator.

For 100 consecutive full-helmet impacts, the source tested whether HARM changed with impact count:

\[
\mathrm{HARM}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad i = 1, \dots, 100
\]

Null hypothesis:

\[
H_0: \beta_1 = 0
\]

Reported result: \(R^2 = 0.04187\), \(p = 0.830\) [PAGE 8]. Units: \(i\) is dimensionless impact count; HARM is dimensionless. Breakdown regime: this regression applies only to one helmet, one location, one velocity, and 80-second inter-impact delay [PAGE 3][PAGE 8]. It cannot be extrapolated to rapid repeated impacts without additional evidence.

10. Temperature operator.

For drop tests, peak reaction force is a function of temperature:

\[
F_{\text{peak}} = F(T)
\]

The tested temperature conditions were ambient \(21^\circ\mathrm{C}\), cold \(-18^\circ\mathrm{C}\), and hot \(50^\circ\mathrm{C}\) [PAGE 4]. In SI absolute temperature:

\[
T \in \{294.15, 255.15, 323.15\}\ \mathrm{K}
\]

The source reports: “the 13% difference between means in peak force at the cold and hot temperature conditions was statistically significant (p = 0.011)” [PAGE 8]. Units: force in newtons, temperature in kelvin or Celsius. Breakdown regime: the source does not specify the denominator for the 13% difference, so the exact reference force is underdetermined from the text alone. The result should be treated as a reported relative difference, not as a universal transfer function.

11. Field event verification operator.

The mouthguard trigger condition was:

\[
\max_t \| a(t) \|_2 \ge 10g
\]

with a 200 ms window: “The mouthguards were set to record a 200 ms time window of data upon any direction of linear acceleration exceeding a 10 g threshold (50 ms pre-trigger, 150 ms post-trigger).” [PAGE 5] The verified event set is:

\[
\mathcal{H} = \{ e : M(e) = \text{true} \land V(e) = \text{helmet-to-helmet} \}
\]

where \(M(e)\) is MiGNet classification and \(V(e)\) is video verification [PAGE 5]. Breakdown regime: true impacts below the trigger threshold are missed; false positives from handling are possible; video verification may fail to distinguish shell-only from mixed facemask-shell contact.

12. Field distributional comparison operator.

For each metric \(X\), the source compared bare and padded impact distributions using Mann-Whitney tests [PAGE 8]. Formally:

\[
H_0: F_{\text{bare}}(x) = F_{\text{padded}}(x)
\]

\[
H_1: F_{\text{bare}}(x) \neq F_{\text{padded}}(x)
\]

The reported field medians were: bare PLA 23.61 \(g\), PAA 1349 \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}\), DAMAGE 0.1256, HARM 2.37; padded PLA 24.21 \(g\), PAA 1099 \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}\), DAMAGE 0.1158, HARM 2.18 [PAGE 8]. Breakdown regime: Mann-Whitney tests assess distributional shift, not mechanism; with small samples, nonsignificance does not establish equivalence.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage begins with the classical insight that angular head motion is central to traumatic brain injury. The source cites Holbourn’s 1943 work on mechanics of head injuries [PAGE 14], and later states that angular accelerations are believed to contribute more to brain injury risk than linear accelerations [PAGE 9]. This lineage explains why the padded shell cover is evaluated through angular metrics even when linear metrics are inconsistent.

The next lineage step is the shift from skull fracture protection to concussion-risk metrics. The source notes that helmets are evaluated for serious head injury and concussion, and that head kinematics correlate with concussive injury risk [PAGE 2]. This creates a chain: impact kinematics → brain strain or risk score → helmet ranking → equipment adoption. The laboratory metrics in this paper sit inside that chain: PLA, PAA, DAMAGE, and HARM are not ends in themselves; they are surrogates for injury risk.

A third lineage step is the instrumented mouthguard. The source states that mouthguards provide accurate kinematics because they are rigidly connected to the maxilla and skull [PAGE 2]. The MiG2.0 device records linear acceleration and angular velocity, then uses MiGNet to separate true impacts from false positives [PAGE 5]. This lineage allows field verification but introduces its own failure modes: trigger threshold, short recording window, video review ambiguity, and classification error.

A fourth lineage step is the padded shell cover itself. The source describes the product as adding external compressible foam, lowering surface friction, and allowing independent rotation [PAGE 2]. This places it in a family of decoupled-layer technologies used in cycling and snow sports, which the authors cite as evidence that such designs can attenuate angular acceleration [PAGE 10]. The retro-causal trap is to assume that because decoupled layers work in other helmets, they must work identically in American football. The sport-specific geometry, mass, impact velocity, and facemask exposure break that assumption.

The multi-week traps are explicit. The field study used different seasons: “In the first year (2019), the team wore bare helmets without the padded helmet shell cover; in the second year (2021), coaching staff required athletes to wear the padded helmet shell covers.” [PAGE 4] This is not a randomized within-season crossover. It is a temporal comparison. The source admits: “our results could have been affected by differences in helmet models worn in the two years of study, which we did not track.” [PAGE 11] It also notes that practice approach or intensity could have changed [PAGE 12]. Therefore, any causal claim that the cover did or did not work on the field must account for year, coaching, athlete selection, practice design, and equipment drift.

Another retro-causal trap is the 2020 gap. The team wore padded covers during parts of 2020, but data collection did not occur due to the pandemic [PAGE 4]. This creates a missing middle period in which behavioral adaptation could have occurred without measurement. If athletes became habituated to the cover before 2021, the 2021 field data may reflect an adapted population rather than the acute effect of the device.

A further trap is impact-location evolution. The laboratory locations were based on concussion data [PAGE 12], but the most frequent subconcussive locations may differ from the most frequent concussive locations. If the cover helps most at locations that are less frequent in the field, the field effect will be attenuated even if the laboratory effect is real. This is a retro-causal weighting problem: laboratory conditions are selected from injury data, but field exposure is governed by play style, position, and contact type.

The final genealogical trap is metric drift. HARM and DAMAGE are derived through models and filters. Laboratory filtering at 300 Hz and field filtering at 160 Hz are not the same measurement pipeline [PAGE 4][PAGE 5]. Therefore, even before biology or behavior enters, the measurement lineage already contains a transformation that can weaken comparability.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The first recursive layer is crisis habit regression. When a team mandates padded covers, athletes and coaches may change behavior. The source explicitly states: “the addition of padded helmet shell covers could have a behavioral effect on athletes, which was not investigated in this study.” [PAGE 12] This creates two opposite regressions.

If athletes feel safer, they may engage more aggressively or accept more contact. Then the number of impacts or the severity distribution may rise, offsetting per-impact attenuation. A formal cumulative exposure operator is:

\[
C = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \mathrm{HARM}_k
\]

where \(N\) is the number of retained head impacts and \(\mathrm{HARM}_k\) is the dimensionless risk score for impact \(k\). If padded impacts have lower HARM per event but \(N\) increases sufficiently, cumulative exposure \(C\) can remain unchanged or increase. The source does not measure this, but the operator shows why per-impact efficacy cannot be inferred directly from cumulative risk without knowing \(N\).

If athletes become more cautious, impacts may decrease in frequency or severity for reasons unrelated to the cover. Then the field effect of the cover is confounded by training behavior. In that case, the observed null could hide a real device effect, because the device and behavior changed together.

The second recursive layer is the counter-trap. The laboratory result is positive for angular metrics and HARM, but the field result is null. A naïve counter-trap would be to conclude that the laboratory test is invalid. A more disciplined counter-trap is to recognize that the field test was underpowered and compositionally contaminated. The source reports that padded impacts had a higher facemask fraction: 67% versus 37% for bare impacts [PAGE 8]. If facemask-to-facemask impacts are not protected, then the field comparison is not comparing the same physical mechanism across conditions. The counter-trap is therefore: do not infer biological failure from an exposure-mix failure.

The third recursive layer is the clock. The source’s temporal machinery defines what can be seen. The mouthguard records only 200 ms after trigger [PAGE 5]. This is sufficient for many peak kinematic events but may truncate slower brain strain evolution. The repeated-impact protocol used 80-second delays [PAGE 3], while real football impacts may occur at shorter intervals [PAGE 12]. Temperature conditioning used 18-hour exposure and testing within 90 seconds [PAGE 4]. Recovery testing after drop impacts used a 24-hour delay [PAGE 4]. These clocks define the boundary conditions of the evidence.

A clock-based gray-zone model is:

\[
\text{Observed attenuation} = f(\text{impact kinematics}, \text{filter window}, \text{recovery interval}, \text{temperature history})
\]

If the cover’s benefit depends on recovery time, then an 80-second protocol may overestimate real-world persistence. If the cover’s benefit depends on temperature equilibration, then a 90-second post-exposure test may not represent long-duration cold or hot play. The recursive question is not merely “did the cover work?” but “under which clock did it work?”

The fourth recursive layer is institutional expectation. The source notes that padded covers have become mandated at elite levels [PAGE 2]. Once mandated, there is pressure to demonstrate benefit. The authors resist this by emphasizing the lack of field significance and the need for further research [PAGE 12]. The recursive audit is therefore: the paper’s language should be read as a cautious translation claim, not as a marketing claim. The strongest defensible statement is that the cover reduces laboratory angular severity under controlled shell impacts, while field evidence remains inconclusive because of exposure mix, sample size, and temporal confounding.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer supports the laboratory angular result. The cover adds compressible foam, lowers surface friction, and permits independent shell rotation [PAGE 2]. These mechanisms plausibly reduce tangential coupling and angular acceleration. The source states that angular accelerations are believed to contribute more to brain injury risk than linear accelerations [PAGE 9]. Therefore, a reduction in PAA, DAMAGE, and HARM is physically coherent.

However, the physical layer also vetoes a simple linear-attenuation claim. The source found inconsistent linear attenuation: only one helmet showed statistically significant PLA reduction at shell locations, and the overall conclusion is that linear attenuation was not consistent [PAGE 6][PAGE 9]. The authors infer that the main mechanism is redirection and friction reduction rather than linear force attenuation [PAGE 10]. This is a cross-layer veto against any claim that the cover works primarily by lowering peak linear acceleration.

The biomechanical layer introduces a facemask veto. The cover does not protect the facemask. The source says a purely facemask-to-facemask impact would remain essentially unchanged [PAGE 10]. In the field, 67% of padded impacts had both facemasks as primary contact [PAGE 8]. If most padded impacts are facemask-dominant, the expected field effect is diluted. A simple exposure-mix proof is:

\[
E[R_{\text{field}}] \approx p_{\text{shell}} R_{\text{shell}} + (1 - p_{\text{shell}}) R_{\text{facemask}}
\]

Assume \(R_{\text{facemask}} \approx 0\) for pure facemask-to-facemask impacts. If \(p_{\text{shell}} \approx 0.33\) because 67% were facemask-dominant, and if the relevant laboratory shell HARM reduction is around 25%, then:

\[
E[R_{\text{field}}] \approx 0.33 \times 25\% = 8.25\%
\]

This is a small expected effect in a small sample. It can easily fail to reach statistical significance even if the shell mechanism is real. This is a cross-layer veto against interpreting the field null as proof of no mechanism.

The weather layer introduces a temperature veto. The source reports a significant 13% difference in peak force between cold and hot conditions [PAGE 8]. The tested conditions were \(-18^\circ\mathrm{C}\), \(21^\circ\mathrm{C}\), and \(50^\circ\mathrm{C}\) [PAGE 4]. Polyurethane foam is temperature-sensitive; stiffness and energy return can change with thermal history. If cold increases stiffness or reduces compressible travel, attenuation may weaken in cold environments. If hot softens the foam excessively, bottoming-out or reduced rebound control could also weaken performance. The source does not provide full-helmet temperature-dependent HARM curves, so the safe adjudication is conditional: ambient laboratory efficacy does not guarantee cold-weather or hot-weather field efficacy.

The final cross-layer adjudication is therefore:

1. Laboratory shell impact: padded cover likely reduces angular severity and HARM.  
2. Field shell-only impact: plausible benefit, but not directly proven because shell-only field events were not isolated.  
3. Field facemask-dominant impact: likely little or no benefit from the shell cover.  
4. Cold or hot extremes: performance may shift; the 13% force difference prevents unconditional extrapolation.  
5. Overall field claim: null result is consistent with exposure dilution and small sample, not with a definitive physical failure.

This adjudication preserves the source’s strongest supported claim while vetoing overextension into facemask protection, all-weather efficacy, or proven on-field concussion reduction.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Scenario 1: Shell-only video-adjudication veto.

A verifiable counterfactual is to re-review the existing field video with higher-resolution footage, multiple camera angles, and frame-by-frame contact classification. The verification rule would be: retain only impacts where at least one player’s shell is the primary contact and no pure facemask-to-facemask event is included. The prediction is that if the laboratory shell benefit is real, the shell-only padded subset should show lower median PAA, DAMAGE, and HARM than the shell-only bare subset.

Formal prediction:

\[
\mathrm{PAA}_{\text{padded, shell}} < \mathrm{PAA}_{\text{bare, shell}}
\]

\[
\mathrm{DAMAGE}_{\text{padded, shell}} < \mathrm{DAMAGE}_{\text{bare, shell}}
\]

\[
\mathrm{HARM}_{\text{padded, shell}} < \mathrm{HARM}_{\text{bare, shell}}
\]

with a statistically detectable effect after controlling for athlete and practice session. If the shell-only subset still shows no difference, the veto is triggered: the field null cannot be explained solely by facemask dilution, and the laboratory-to-field translation claim is weakened.

Scenario 2: Within-athlete crossover veto.

A verifiable counterfactual is a within-season crossover in which the same linebackers wear bare helmets and padded covers in matched practice blocks, with helmet model, player position, drill type, and practice intensity recorded. The verification model is a mixed-effects model:

\[
X_{ij} = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 C_{ij} + u_j + \epsilon_{ij}
\]

where \(X_{ij}\) is PAA, DAMAGE, or HARM for athlete \(j\) in condition \(i\), \(C_{ij}\) is cover use, and \(u_j\) is an athlete random intercept. The prediction is:

\[
\gamma_1 < 0
\]

for angular metrics if the cover is efficacious. If \(\gamma_1\) is not negative or the confidence interval includes zero after controlling for practice intensity and helmet model, the field efficacy claim is vetoed. This scenario directly addresses the source’s limitation that year, helmet model, and practice approach could confound the two-season comparison [PAGE 11][PAGE 12].

Scenario 3: Temperature-conditioned full-helmet veto.

A verifiable counterfactual is to repeat full-helmet laboratory tests at cold, ambient, and hot conditions using the same impact locations and velocities. For each temperature \(T\), compute the paired HARM reduction:

\[
R_{\mathrm{HARM}}(T) = 100 \times \frac{\mathrm{HARM}_{\text{bare}}(T) - \mathrm{HARM}_{\text{padded}}(T)}{\mathrm{HARM}_{\text{bare}}(T)}
\]

The prediction is that if the cover is robust across environments, then:

\[
R_{\mathrm{HARM}}(T_{\text{cold}}) > 0
\]

and

\[
R_{\mathrm{HARM}}(T_{\text{hot}}) > 0
\]

with confidence intervals excluding zero or at least not reversing sign. If cold testing yields \(R_{\mathrm{HARM}}(T_{\text{cold}}) \le 0\), then cold-weather deployment claims are vetoed. If hot testing yields \(R_{\mathrm{HARM}}(T_{\text{hot}}) \le 0\), then hot-weather deployment claims are vetoed. This scenario is grounded in the source’s finding that cold and hot peak force differed by 13% [PAGE 8] and its caution that temperature performance remains incompletely characterized [PAGE 10].

These three RLVR veto scenarios are verifiable because they specify data, model, metric, and decision rule. They also prevent reward hacking: a positive result must come from pre-specified shell-only, within-athlete, or temperature-conditioned tests, not from post hoc selection of favorable laboratory conditions.
