The recursive theory-of-mind structure in padel shot selection can be decomposed into three coupled layers: crisis habit regression, counter-trap anticipation, and the temporal clock. At layer zero, under score pressure a player regresses to high-frequency habitual shots. The data show women in the final favor an offensive style centered on forehands and smashes, while men rely more on baseline control and extended rally construction [PAGE 7]. At layer one, the opponent forms a second-order belief P(counter | s, c), positioning to intercept the expected habitual shot; this creates a counter-trap where the regressing player’s predictable shot becomes exploitable. At layer two, the original player may anticipate the counter and deviate from the habit, generating a regressed-counter-regressed loop that is not observable from frame-level pose alone.

The dataset’s operationalization of this recursion is partial and bounded by the clock. A failure mode arises when a model treats each shot frame as conditionally independent of the recursive belief state, ignoring that the observable shot at time t is the equilibrium output of a multi-agent dynamic played out over the preceding seconds. Because head keypoints are excluded [PAGE 10] and gaze direction is not annotated, the dataset cannot directly observe where a player is looking before the shot, which is often the leading indicator of intention in the recursive loop. Consequently, any inference about crisis habit regression versus counter-trap must be treated as a latent variable estimated from aggregate shot frequencies, not as a directly measured frame-level variable.

The single camera resides 7.6 m above the floor and 15.5 m behind the glass panels [PAGE 8], and the mesh panel occludes the region "from the bottom of the net to near the opposite service line" [PAGE 8]. Veto rule: if the ball’s projected pixel location falls behind the mesh-panel occlusion zone or outside the camera frustum, the ball-tracking output is vetoed and the missing value must remain unfilled, exactly as the source protocol dictates [PAGE 10]. Interpolation across such gaps is not licensed by the data.

Biomechanical layer. Pose estimation relies on YOLOv8x detection with confidence c > 0.7 [PAGE 9], ViTPose-L 17-keypoint estimation [PAGE 9], and manual verification that all four players are present with accurate ankle keypoints [PAGE 10]. Head keypoints are excluded because players often face away [PAGE 10]. Veto rule: if any of the four players lacks detection confidence above 0.7, or if both ankle keypoints are occluded, the homography-projected location_m is vetoed because the center-of-mass proxy \mathbf{p}_{CM} = (\mathbf{p}_{\text{L\_ankle}} + \mathbf{p}_{\text{R\_ankle}})/2 is undefined or unreliable [PAGE 10]. The reported player IoU of 0.826 indicates residual disagreement that must be propagated as positional uncertainty.

Weather/environmental layer. All footage is from the indoor Palau Sant Jordi with controlled lighting and a single court type [PAGE 2][PAGE 11]; the authors explicitly cite "lack of variation in playing conditions (e.g., lighting, court type)" as a limitation [PAGE 11]. Veto rule: deployment in an environment with illumination shift \Delta L beyond the indoor baseline, or on a court with different surface friction or dimensions, voids the generalization claim. The homography H is calibrated to this specific court geometry [PAGE 11]; applying it to a different court without recalibration is a rank-valid but semantically invalid transformation.

Adjudication order: physical visibility is checked first (ball in FoV), then biomechanical detectability (four players, conf > 0.7, visible ankles), then environmental stationarity (indoor conditions). Only if all three pass does the homography-transformed metric output enter downstream tactical or trajectory modeling. This is a veto proof, not a confidence weighting: a single failed layer produces a null output rather than a degraded prediction.

Three verifiable RLVR-style veto scenarios, each with a formal condition and a falsifiable prediction.

Veto Scenario 1 — Prolonged ball occlusion. Condition: Let b_t \in \{0,1\} indicate ball visibility in frame t. If b_t = 0 and \sum_{i=t}^{t+W} b_i < \theta_{vis} for W = 15 frames (0.5 s at 30 fps), veto all trajectory and shot-prediction outputs for that interval. Rationale: 64.19% of male-final frames lack the ball [PAGE 6], and the protocol leaves missing values unfilled [PAGE 10]. Prediction: Under ballistic flight, extrapolation error after gap \Delta t grows as \epsilon(\Delta t) = \frac{1}{2} g (\Delta t)^2 in the vertical component absent drag corrections; for g = 9.81 m/s^2 and \Delta t = 0.5 s, \epsilon > 1.22 m, exceeding court-relevant tolerance. Therefore the system must reject shot-type inference during the gap rather than hallucinate a landing zone.

Veto Scenario 2 — Player detection collapse. Condition: If \max_{j \in \{1..4\}} c_j < 0.7 for any frame, or if ankle-keypoint visibility v < 2 for a player, veto the homography location_m and all derived heatmaps. Rationale: detection threshold c > 0.7 is explicitly enforced [PAGE 9], and ankle averaging is the only sanctioned center-of-mass proxy [PAGE 10]. Prediction: Position error \delta x in meters scales with the uncertainty in the ankle proxy; when visibility drops below v = 2, the projected court coordinate error exceeds 0.5 m with high probability, invalidating net-approach versus baseline classification. The system must output a null player-coordinate flag rather than an interpolated heatmap value.

Veto Scenario 3 — Shot-class prior collapse. Condition: If the empirical frequency of dropshot in a deployment window falls below 0.002 (0.2%) while the training prior is P(dropshot) = 0.000544 (female) or 0.001223 (male) [PAGE 6], veto deployment of the shot-type classifier without rebalancing.
