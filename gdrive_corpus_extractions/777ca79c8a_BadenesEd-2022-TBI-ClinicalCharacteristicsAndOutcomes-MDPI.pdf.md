My multi-hop derivation therefore treats each article as a distinct operator with its own reference standard, time constant, and failure mode. The first hop is physical: NIRS is governed by near-infrared photon transport. The source states that “tissues are relatively translucent to near-infrared light with wavelengths of 700–1000 nm” and that light “can penetrate to a depth of up to 8 cm” [PAGE 11]. The Beer–Lambert relation is given as \(A=\log(I_0/I)=\alpha c d\), then corrected to \(A=\alpha c d\,DPF+G\) because scattering dominates [PAGE 11]. This immediately creates a gray zone: a normal regional cerebral oxygen saturation reading is not a direct CBF measurement. The article reports that cerebral oximetry identified “only about 15% of the ischemic episodes detected by the PbtO2 measurement” and that “NIRS continued to show normal rScO2 values, while PbtO2 dropped to 0 mmHg” [PAGE 12]. A later page states “reports of measuring devices registering normal cerebral oximetry values in patients with confirmed absence of cerebral perfusion” [PAGE 16]. Thus NIRS cannot serve as a veto over CTP or clinical BD/DNC; its physical contamination by extracerebral blood flow, CSF, skull thickness, and myelin sheaths [PAGE 15] is a hard lower-layer constraint.

The second hop is temporal and definitional. Tracheostomy timing appears simple but is a multi-week trap: the source’s included studies defined early tracheostomy as ≤3 days, 3–5 days, ≤6 days, ≤7 days, ≤8 days, and ≤10 days [PAGE 22]. The abstract says early tracheostomy “reduced risk for incidence of pneumonia, ICU length of stay, hospital length of stay and duration of mechanical ventilation, but not mortality,” yet the same abstract concludes that early tracheostomy “might … increase the risk of mortality” [PAGE 20]. The results state “Increased risk of mortality was found in the ET group (OR = 1.56, 95%CI = 1.06, 2.3, I2 = 38.3%, p = 0.11)” [PAGE 22], while the discussion says “an increased risk of mortality was found in the LT group” and later “We found that the LT group had an increased risk of mortality” [PAGE 26]. This is a direct internal contradiction—an audit flag. The correct inference is not to pick a side but to recognize that “early” is a moving exposure and that mortality is a composite outcome. The meta-analysis’s own trial sequential analysis says the “current evidence was inconclusive” [PAGE 22].

The third hop is dose–response and organ cross-talk. Hyperosmolar therapy is titrated to reduce ICP, but the source states the “commonly recommended osmolality of 320 mOsm/kg H2O seems to be the maximum level, although an increase in plasma osmolality above 310 mOsm/kg H2O may already induce cardiac and immune system disorders” [PAGE 30]. An osmolal gap >10 mOsm/L documents osmotically active agents; >20 mOsm/L suggests intoxication with strong osmotic substances [PAGE 32]. The cardiac threshold is sharper: mannitol-induced osmolality “above 313 mOsm/kg H2O significantly increased the risk for prolongation of corrected QT interval above 500 ms” [PAGE 33]. Renal injury has a dose threshold: mannitol daily dose “cut-off … at 1.34 g/kg body weight” [PAGE 35]. Endothelial loss is most pronounced “with serum osmolarity above 320 mOsm/L” [PAGE 36]. Therefore the gray zone is 310–320 mOsm/kg H2O: therapeutic benefit is plausible, but cardiac, immune, renal, and BBB effects are already measurable.

The fourth hop is prehospital oxygen. The source excludes patients with prehospital SpO2 <94% and compares no oxygen, low-flow 1–5 L/min, mid-flow 6–14 L/min, and high-flow 15 L/min [PAGE 118]. The first Polish CTA study reported sensitivity 96.3% against DSA using a 4-point scale; the second reported CTA sensitivity 86% and CTP sensitivity 100% against clinical BD/DNC supported by TCD [PAGE 129]. CTP criteria are CBF <10 mL/100 g/min and CBV <1.0 mL/100 g in all ROIs [PAGE 134]. The failure regime is timing: “if CTA tests were performed shortly after the appearance of brainstem areflexia, more widespread opacification of cerebral vessels … was recorded,” because “the intracranial pressure (ICP) did not exceed the mean arterial pressure (MAP)” [PAGE 131]. Thus premature CTA is a false-negative trap. Conversely, CTP can be decisive when CTA is inconclusive, but isolated craniectomy or open-fracture areas above threshold do not exclude CCA if other ROIs, including brainstem, are below threshold [PAGE 135]. Specificity is not fully established; the source quotes that “Specificity is assumed on basis of experimental data but should be interpreted with caution” [PAGE 133].

The sixth hop is psychometric and outcome-measurement validity. CENTER-TBI’s reliability criteria use Cronbach’s alpha ≥0.70 as acceptable and >0.90 as excellent [PAGE 56], and item–total correlations below 0.30 as poor discrimination [PAGE 57]. The RPQ one-factor solution “demonstrated a rather poor fit” [PAGE 69], and the SF-36v2/SF-12v2 factorial structures were not cleanly replicated [PAGE 70]. Therefore, at six months, patient-reported outcomes are reliable at scale level but not interchangeable at individual level. The gray-zone audit is that a statistically significant group correlation does not license individual clinical prediction.

Overall, the source supports a layered adjudication: physical optical limits veto NIRS-only conclusions; biomechanical ICP/MAP and CTA timing veto premature CTA; dose thresholds veto unbounded osmotherapy; SpO2/flow interaction vetoes indiscriminate high-flow oxygen; and psychometric factorial limits veto overconfident individual PROM interpretation. The contradictions in tracheostomy mortality and the NIRS–PbtO2 discordance are not noise; they are boundary markers that define where the evidence stops.

1. NIRS photon attenuation. The source gives the Beer–Lambert form
\[
A=\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{I_0}{I}\right)=\alpha\,c\,d,
\]
and the scattering-corrected form
\[
A=\alpha\,c\,d\,DPF+G,
\]
where \(A\) is dimensionless attenuation, \(I_0\) and \(I\) are incident and transmitted intensities, \(\alpha\) is the extinction coefficient, \(c\) is chromophore concentration, \(d\) is path length, \(DPF\) is the differential pathlength factor, and \(G\) is a light-loss factor [PAGE 11]. Units: \(\alpha\) is commonly \(\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}\,cm^{-1}}\), \(c\) in \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\), \(d\) in \(\mathrm{cm}\); \(DPF\) and \(G\) are dimensionless. Assumptions: approximately homogeneous tissue, known chromophore extinction, and stable optical coupling. Breakdown: scattering dominates over absorption, extracerebral blood flow, CSF, skull thickness, myelin, skin pigmentation, myoglobin, hematoma, and edema corrupt the path [PAGE 15]. The source explicitly warns of “normal cerebral oximetry values in patients with confirmed absence of cerebral perfusion” [PAGE 16], so the operator has a false-normal singularity regime.

2. Cerebrovascular autoregulation index. The source describes \(COx\) as a correlation between \(rScO_2\) and MAP: \(COx\approx 0\) means pressure is within the autoregulation range; \(COx\approx 1\) means strong correlation and impaired autoregulation or MAP beyond the autoregulatory limit [PAGE 13]. Formal operator:
\[
COx(t)=\operatorname{corr}\!\left(rScO_2(t-\tau:t),\,MAP(t-\tau:t)\right),
\]
dimensionless in \([-1,1]\). Assumptions: stable oxygen metabolism over the window, sufficient signal-to-noise, and no extracerebral contamination. Breakdown: motion, low perfusion, optical contamination, and nonstationarity; correlation is not causality.

3. Plasma osmolality and osmolal gap. The Worthley equation is
\[
Osm_{calc}=2[Na^+]+\frac{[Glucose]_{mg/dL}}{18}+\frac{[BUN]_{mg/dL}}{2.8}
=275\text{–}295\ \mathrm{mOsm/kg\,H_2O},
\]
with the gap
\[
G_{osm}=Osm_{measured}-Osm_{calc}.
\]
The source states physiological \(G_{osm}\) is “−10 to +10 mOsm/L,” \(>10\) documents osmotically active agents, and \(>20\) suggests intoxication with strong osmotic substances [PAGE 32]. Units: mOsm/kg H2O or mOsm/L. Assumptions: valid conversion factors and no unusual osmoles. Breakdown: ethanol, mannitol, hypertonic saline, renal failure, and hyperglycemia; measured osmolality by cryoscopy is the reference [PAGE 32]. Clinical thresholds: \(>310\) mOsm/kg H2O may induce cardiac/immune disorders [PAGE 30], \(>313\) mOsm/kg H2O is linked to QTc >500 ms [PAGE 33], and \(>320\) mOsm/kg H2O is associated with endothelial cell loss [PAGE 36].

4. Trauma scoring. The Revised Trauma Score is
\[
RTS=0.9368\,GCS_c+0.7326\,SBP_c+0.2908\,RR_c,
\]
where the subscripted values are coded values from the source table [PAGE 43]. \(RTS\) is dimensionless. The MEWS is
\[
M=\sum_{i=1}^{5}m_i(x_i),
\]
where \(x_i\) are respiratory rate, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, temperature, and AVPU neurological response, and \(m_i\) are integer points from the MEWS table [PAGE 44]. Assumptions: stable measurement at triage and correct coding. Breakdown: intubated patients, sedation, beta-blockade, temperature site variation, and inter-rater GCS/AVPU variability [PAGE 49]. The reported AUCs were 0.799 for MEWS, 0.742 for RTS, 0.638 for ISS, and 0.524 for Shock Index [PAGE 42].

5. Logistic regression and interaction. The source uses multivariable logistic regression and interaction terms. General form:
\[
\operatorname{logit}(p)=\ln\!\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)
=\beta_0+\beta_F F+\beta_S S+\beta_{FS}FS+\gamma^\top Z,
\]
where \(p\) is in-hospital mortality probability, \(F\) is oxygen flow category, \(S\) is prehospital saturation category, and \(Z\) is the covariate vector [PAGE 124]. The exponentiated coefficient \(OR=\exp(\beta)\) is dimensionless. Assumptions: logit linearity, independent observations, no unmeasured confounding, and correct category coding. Breakdown: residual confounding by injury severity, indication bias, and missing PaO2 or oxygen duration [PAGE 125]. Reported interaction results: low-flow oxygen at SpO2 94–98% AOR 0.80 (0.67–0.95); low-flow at SpO2 ≥99% AOR 0.69 (0.53–0.91); high-flow at SpO2 ≥99% AOR 1.33 (1.01–1.74) [PAGE 124].

6. CTA 4-point scale and CTP criteria. For the 4-point CTA scale, let
\[
S_4=\mathbf{1}\{MCA\text{-}M4_L\text{ absent}\}
+\mathbf{1}\{MCA\text{-}M4_R\text{ absent}\}
+\mathbf{1}\{ICV_L\text{ absent}\}
+\mathbf{1}\{ICV_R\text{ absent}\},
\]
with CCA positive when \(S_4=4\) [PAGE 131]. CTP criteria in the source are
\[
CBF<10\ \mathrm{mL/100\,g/min}
\quad\text{and}\quad
CBV<1.0\ \mathrm{mL/100\,g}
\]
in all ROIs [PAGE 134]. Assumptions: adequate contrast bolus, whole-brain coverage, and correct ROI placement. Breakdown: premature CTA before ICP exceeds MAP [PAGE 131], isolated craniectomy/open-fracture areas above threshold that do not exclude CCA if other ROIs are below threshold, and multiple scattered areas above threshold that are inconsistent with CCA [PAGE 135]. Specificity is not fully proven; the source says specificity is “assumed on basis of experimental data but should be interpreted with caution” [PAGE 133].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from Jobsis’s 1977 description of near-infrared monitoring [PAGE 11] through the Beer–Lambert scattering correction and into CENTER-TBI’s multilingual outcome instruments. The brain-death lineage is explicitly historical: the first Polish BD/DNC criteria in 1984 were “similar to the so-called ‘Harvard brain death criteria’”; in 1990 they were converted into brainstem-death criteria; in 2007 they were reversed back to whole-brain-death criteria; and they were amended in 2020 [PAGE 129]. CTA was initially rejected by the Polish Task Force in 2007 because of “insufficient evidence in the literature and lack of experience in Poland”; the response was a national multicenter trial and later validation against DSA and TCD [PAGE 130]. This is a retrocausal genealogy: today’s CTP threshold is not an isolated discovery but the end of a chain of failed implementation, scanner-generation limits, and revalidation.

The multi-week traps are several. Tracheostomy “early” is not a single week: definitions ranged from ≤3 days to ≤10 days [PAGE 22], so pooling creates exposure misclassification. Osmotherapy accumulates over days: the same plasma osmolality that reduces cerebral edema can prolong QTc and injure renal tubules, with thresholds at 310, 313, and 320 mOsm/kg H2O [PAGE 30, PAGE 33, PAGE 36]. CENTER-TBI outcome assessment at six months is a multi-week trap because the psychometric instruments are reliable at group level but the RPQ and SF-36v2/SF-12v2 factorial structures did not replicate cleanly [PAGE 69, PAGE 70]. CTA timing is a multi-hour trap with multi-week consequences: a CTA done too soon after brainstem areflexia can show opacification because ICP has not yet exceeded MAP [PAGE 131]. Prehospital oxygen is a minutes-to-hours trap, but the interaction with saturation is measurable at the population level [PAGE 124]. Thus the lineage is not linear progress; it is successive narrowing of valid domains.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears at four agent levels. The prehospital clinician under time pressure defaults to high-flow oxygen because “there is no clear flow rate or method of oxygen administration or target saturation level” [PAGE 119]. The counter-trap is the source’s interaction evidence: low-flow oxygen at SpO2 94–98% and ≥99% was associated with lower mortality, while high-flow at ≥99% was associated with higher mortality [PAGE 124]. The habit “more oxygen is safer” regresses under uncertainty, but the measured reward reverses at the high-flow boundary.

The intensivist under osmotherapy pressure defaults to chasing a single osmolality number. The counter-trap is the organ cross-talk: >310 mOsm/kg H2O may already affect cardiac and immune function [PAGE 30], >313 mOsm/kg H2O is linked to QTc >500 ms [PAGE 33], and mannitol >1.34 g/kg/day is associated with AKI [PAGE 35]. The clock is not the ICU day but the cumulative osmotic load and the QTc/creatinine trajectory.

The neurointensivist under brain-death uncertainty defaults to CTA as a visual “yes/no.” The counter-trap is that CTA can be false-negative if performed too early [PAGE 131], and CTP can be decisive when CTA is inconclusive [PAGE 129]. The clock is the recommended 6-hour observation before CTA/CTP in Polish and French protocols [PAGE 131, PAGE 132]. The recursive theory of mind here is that the clinician knows the family wants certainty, the family knows the clinician wants legal safety, and the guideline writer knows both—so the protocol inserts a clock and a second test rather than relying on a single image.

The psychometrician under six-month follow-up pressure defaults to treating a PROM score as an individual diagnosis. The counter-trap is that reliability is satisfactory to excellent at scale level, but factorial validity failed for RPQ and generic SF instruments [PAGE 69, PAGE 70]. The clock is six months; the recursive trap is that patients with more severe TBI may not complete PROMs, so the observed sample is not the full severity spectrum [PAGE 73]. The counter-trap is to use PROMs for group comparison, not individual prediction, unless measurement invariance and item-level behavior are verified.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto. NIRS cannot veto CCA or deep ischemia because photon transport is contaminated by extracerebral blood flow, CSF, skull thickness, myelin, skin pigmentation, myoglobin, hematoma, and edema [PAGE 15]. The source reports normal rScO2 in a patient with PbtO2 0 mmHg [PAGE 12] and normal oximetry in confirmed absence of cerebral perfusion [PAGE 16]. Therefore, if CTP shows CBF <10 mL/100 g/min and CBV <1.0 mL/100 g in all ROIs [PAGE 134], a normal NIRS reading does not overrule CTP. The physical veto is asymmetric: CTP can veto NIRS, but NIRS cannot veto CTP.

Biomechanical veto. CCA requires effective cerebral perfusion to be absent. The source explains that premature CTA can show widespread opacification because “the intracranial pressure (ICP) did not exceed the mean arterial pressure (MAP)” [PAGE 131]. Formal condition: if \(CPP=MAP-ICP>0\), CTA may still opacify; therefore a negative CTA before the 6-hour observation window is not a valid veto against clinical BD/DNC. Conversely, if CTA shows bilateral absence of MCA-M4 and ICV opacification on the 4-point scale [PAGE 131], and CTP confirms CBF/CBV below threshold, the biomechanical evidence is concordant. The veto rule is: timing must precede interpretation; a premature CTA is a false-negative regime, not proof of perfusion.

Weather/environmental veto. The source does not measure weather directly, so the only defensible environmental proxies are season, transport time, and body temperature. Season was not significantly associated with oxygen flow category in the prehospital table (\(p=0.57\)) [PAGE 122], so season alone is a weak veto. Body temperature is stronger: hypothermia ≤35°C was present in 4.0% overall, 1.8% of survivors, and 14.8% of non-survivors (\(p<0.001\)) [PAGE 45], and MEWS includes temperature as a scored component [PAGE 44]. Transport time was associated with oxygen flow category (\(p<0.01\)) [PAGE 123]. Therefore, the environmental veto is not “bad weather” but “hypothermia plus prolonged transport.” If core temperature is ≤35°C or transport is prolonged, the prehospital oxygen and MEWS signals are confounded and cannot be interpreted as isolated brain-injury effects.

The cross-layer adjudication is therefore a precedence order: physical optical limits > biomechanical perfusion/timing > environmental confounders > clinical gestalt. A higher-layer impression cannot veto a lower-layer measurement when the lower-layer measurement is in its valid domain. The source’s repeated warnings about NIRS limitations [PAGE 15–16], CTA timing [PAGE 131], and CTP specificity [PAGE 133] are exactly such veto proofs.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Prehospital oxygen counterfactual clamp. Intervention: for severe blunt TBI with SpO2 ≥99% after oxygen, clamp flow to low-flow 1–5 L/min rather than high-flow 15 L/min. Comparator: high-flow 15 L/min at the same saturation. Endpoint: in-hospital mortality and poor neurologic outcome by Glasgow Outcome Scale 3–5. Prediction: low-flow will show lower mortality, with AOR approximately 0.69 (0.53–0.91) at SpO2 ≥99%, while high-flow will show AOR approximately 1.33 (1.01–1.74) [PAGE 124]. Verification: repeat the interaction model with flow category, saturation category, and the \(F\times S\) term; a null or reversed interaction vetoes the “more oxygen is better” habit. This is verifiable because the exposure is a recorded flow rate, the outcome is in-hospital mortality, and the model is prespecified by the source [PAGE 124].

Scenario 2: CTA timing counterfactual. Intervention: perform CTA/CTP immediately after first brainstem areflexia versus after the recommended 6-hour observation window. Comparator: same patients imaged again after 6 hours. Endpoint: CTA opacification of MCA-M4/ICV and CTP CBF <10 mL/100 g/min plus CBV <1.0 mL/100 g. Prediction: early CTA will show more frequent opacification and lower sensitivity because ICP has not yet exceeded MAP; delayed CTA will show higher concordance with CTP. The source’s own experience is that premature CTA produces “more widespread opacification of cerebral vessels” [PAGE 131], and the Polish protocol recommends “a minimal 6-h observation time before CTA/CTP examinations” [PAGE 131]. Verification: paired early-versus-delayed CTA/CTP with the 4-point scale [PAGE 131] and CTP thresholds [PAGE 134]. If early CTA sensitivity does not fall relative to delayed CTA, the timing-veto hypothesis is falsified.

Scenario 3: CTP versus NIRS counterfactual. Intervention: use CTP as the reference for CCA and ask whether NIRS rScO2 can veto the CTP diagnosis. Comparator: CTP CBF <10 mL/100 g/min and CBV <1.0 mL/100 g in all ROIs. Endpoint: discordance rate between normal NIRS and CTP-confirmed CCA. Prediction: NIRS will fail to detect a substantial fraction of ischemic/CCA episodes; the source reports NIRS identified “only about 15% of the ischemic episodes detected by the PbtO2 measurement” [PAGE 12] and can register normal values in confirmed absence of cerebral perfusion [PAGE 16]. Therefore, a normal NIRS reading should not veto CTP. Verification: paired NIRS-CTP in patients undergoing BD/DNC evaluation; the predicted discordance is high enough that NIRS-alone would miss most CTP-positive cases. If NIRS normalizes in CTP-positive cases at a low rate, the physical-contamination veto is weakened. This scenario is directly verifiable because CTP thresholds and NIRS limitations are both explicitly stated in the source [PAGE 12, PAGE 16, PAGE 134].
