<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s central derivation is a replacement for the home-and-home direct comparison that the original LRMC model used in college basketball. The source states the football problem directly: “Most teams only play 3-4 games outside their conference, making it difficult to compare teams from different conferences” [PAGE 2]. The model therefore builds a state for each team in a Markov chain, estimates a logistic function \(r_x\) for the probability that the home team is better than the away team given a score differential \(x\), and then converts those estimates into transition probabilities. The source defines “\(r_x(g)\)” as “an estimate of the probability that the home team of game \(g\) is better than the away team of game \(g\) given that the home team won by \(x(g)\) points, where \(x(g)\) is negative if the home team lost” [PAGE 3]. The Markov chain then uses these probabilities to construct \(t_{ij}\) and \(t_{ii}\), and “By ordering this stationary distribution in decreasing order, we obtain a ranking of the college football teams” [PAGE 3].

The multi-hop derivation runs as follows. First, for each game \(g=(i,j)\), where \(i\) is the visiting team and \(j\) is the home team, the paper defines common opponents \(C(g)\). Second, it defines \(\mu(G_j(g))\) as the number of games team \(j\) won against those common opponents, and \(\nu(G_i(g))\) as the number of games team \(i\) lost against those common opponents. Third, it estimates \(r_x\) by pooling those common-opponent results over all games in \(G_x\), the set of games with an \(x\)-point home win. Fourth, it smooths the pooled estimates with a logistic regression. Fifth, it builds a row-stochastic transition matrix \(T\). Sixth, it solves for the stationary distribution \(\pi\). Seventh, it ranks teams by decreasing \(\pi\). Eighth, it compares the ranking order of the two bowl teams and counts a correct bowl prediction when the winning team is ranked above the losing team. Each hop introduces an assumption: common opponents must be exchangeable with the compared teams; the logistic curve must be monotone and stable; the transition matrix must have a unique stationary distribution; and the bowl site must be sufficiently neutral. The paper addresses the last one explicitly: “We also make the assumption that the field is truly neutral, despite some bowl games’ locations being slightly favored towards one team” [PAGE 5].

The gray-zone audit is where the model is most vulnerable. Equation (3) can be undefined when the denominator is zero, which occurs when the pooled common-opponent set is empty for all games with a given \(x\). Even when nonzero, the estimator is a ratio of counts and becomes highly variable when \(|\mathcal C(g)|\) is small. The logistic regression is fit to aggregated estimates, not to independent Bernoulli trials, so the reported Wald-test significance for the score-differential slope does not by itself prove calibration. The paper reports that “The margin of victory was shown to be statistically significant as a predictor of \(r_x\) for all seasons \((p \lt 0.001, \text{Wald Test})\)” [PAGE 4], but significance of a monotone slope is weaker than out-of-sample calibration. The transition matrix can also fail to be irreducible if the schedule graph is disconnected, and it can be periodic if self-loops vanish. In that case the stationary distribution is not guaranteed to be unique, or convergence to it is not guaranteed. The four-season rolling window is another gray zone: “Because college football styles of play change over time, we use a rolling four-season window to compile data” [PAGE 4]. That helps with nonstationarity but creates a bias-variance tradeoff. The paper says “Multiple training set sizes were analyzed and the four-season window provided the strongest results” [PAGE 4]; if that choice was made after examining bowl accuracy, there is a retrospective selection risk. Finally, the bowl evaluation uses many models and many McNemar comparisons; the paper reports that Kambour and PerformanZ “fail to be statistically significant over the F-LRMC (with p-values of .1282 and .1933, respectively)” [PAGE 6], which is a useful caution against over-reading small rank differences.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source model has three formal layers: a common-opponent estimator for \(r_x\), a logistic smoother, and a Markov-chain ranking operator. The source states the common-opponent estimator as
\[
r_x \approx \frac{\sum_{g\in G_x}\left(\mu(G_j(g))+\nu(G_i(g))\right)}{2\sum_{g\in G_x}|\{C(g)\}|},
\]
where \(G_x\) is the set of games in a season in which the home team won by \(x\) points, \(C(g)\) is the set of common opponents who played both the home and away teams, \(\mu(G_j(g))\) is the number of games team \(j\) won against those common opponents, and \(\nu(G_i(g))\) is the number of games team \(i\) lost against those common opponents [PAGE 4]. The paper paraphrases this as: “In simple terms, given the winning percentages \(p_i\) and \(p_j\) of teams \(i\) and \(j\) against their common opponents,” the quantity
\[
\frac{p_j+(1-p_i)}{2}
\]
“is an estimate of the probability that \(i\) is better then \(j\)” [PAGE 4]. Units: \(x\) is in points, an ordinal score unit rather than an SI unit; \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are counts of games; \(C(g)\) is a set of teams; \(r_x\) is a dimensionless probability in \([0,1]\).

The logistic smoother is
\[
r_x=\frac{e^{a x+b}}{1+e^{a x+b}},
\]
or equivalently
\[
\operatorname{logit}(r_x)=a x+b.
\]
The source says “The logistic regression model finds values \((a,b)\) such that” this logistic form is “best fit to the data” [PAGE 4]. Units: \(a\) has units of \(\text{points}^{-1}\), \(b\) is dimensionless, and \(a x+b\) is a dimensionless logit measured in nats because the natural exponential is used. The increasing-function assumption requires \(a>0\). If \(a=0\), the curve is flat and score differential carries no ranking information. If \(a<0\), the monotonicity assumption is violated. If \(|a x+b|\) becomes very large, the logistic saturates and the model loses local resolution; extrapolation far beyond the observed score-differential range is not supported by the fitted curve.

The Markov transition probabilities are given as
\[
t_{ij}=\frac{1}{N_i}\left[\sum_{g=(i,j)}r_{x(g)}+\sum_{g=(j,i)}\left(1-r_{x(g)}\right)\right],\qquad j\ne i,
\]
and
\[
t_{ii}=\frac{1}{N_i}\left[\sum_{g=(\cdot,i)}r_{x(g)}+\sum_{g=(i,\cdot)}\left(1-r_{x(g)}\right)\right],
\]
where \(N_i\) is the number of games played by team \(i\) [PAGE 3]. Units: \(t_{ij}\) and \(t_{ii}\) are dimensionless probabilities; \(N_i\) is a game count. The stationary distribution satisfies
\[
\pi=\pi T,\qquad \sum_i \pi_i=1,\qquad \pi_i\ge 0.
\]
The ranking is obtained by ordering \(\pi\) in decreasing order [PAGE 3].

Exact singularity and breakdown regimes:
1. Denominator-zero regime: if \(\sum_{g\in G_x}|C(g)|=0\), equation (3) is undefined. This occurs when no game with an \(x\)-point home win has any common opponent.
2. Sparse-common-opponent regime: if \(|\mathcal C(g)|\) is small, the count-based estimator is high variance; if all common-opponent results are one-sided, \(r_x\) can hit exactly \(0\) or \(1\), creating quasi-separation in the logistic fit.
3. Monotonicity-violation regime: if the fitted \(a\le 0\), the source assumption that \(r_x\) increases with \(x\) fails.
4. Reducibility regime: if the schedule graph splits into disconnected components, \(T\) is reducible and the stationary distribution is not unique.
5. Periodicity regime: if all self-transitions vanish and the schedule graph is bipartite, the chain can be periodic, so convergence to stationarity is not guaranteed even if \(\pi\) exists.
6. Nonstationary-window regime: if the four-season training window no longer represents current play, the fitted \((a,b)\) are stale. Table 1 shows small year-to-year parameter movement, but that is not proof of stability under rule changes, conference realignment, or schedule changes [PAGE 6].
7. Neutral-site-violation regime: if a bowl site is effectively not neutral, the rank-order prediction rule is mis-specified [PAGE 5].
8. Score-differential-manipulation regime: if final score differential is inflated by late scoring after the outcome is decided, \(x\) is not a clean signal of team quality; the paper notes that “The F-LRMC relies heavily on margin of victory in its construction” [PAGE 10].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is explicit. The paper extends “Kvam and Sokol’s (2006)” LRMC model for college basketball, which used “home-and-home” games to estimate the logistic function directly [PAGE 2]. The football version replaces that direct comparison with common opponents because football rarely supplies home-and-home pairs; the paper says “we have found only about 40 instances in the 20 years from 1996-2015” [PAGE 3]. The lineage then branches: Brown and Sokol (2010) used an empirical Bayes approach; Maclay used natural logs of margin of victory; Knottenbelt et al. (2012) used common opponents for tennis; Hamdy et al. (2016) and Liu et al. (2015) applied LRMC-style ideas to urban sprawl and land-use dynamics [PAGE 2, PAGE 4]. This is a retrocausal genealogy in the sense that the football model is a later node that reinterpreted the basketball model’s missing home-and-home condition as a common-opponent identification problem.

The multi-week traps are historical and methodological. First, the model uses a rolling four-season window, so each season’s ranking is trained on prior seasons only; that is good for temporal ordering, but the choice of a four-season window was itself selected by performance, which can create a retrospective tuning trap [PAGE 4]. Second, the paper evaluates on bowl games from 2002–2016, while the training window moves; rule changes, conference membership changes, and schedule-strength changes can make older seasons poor proxies. Third, bowl selection is not random: bowl games pair teams by conference tie-ins and selection rules, so the evaluation sample is not a random sample of all possible matchups. Fourth, Massey’s composite includes only models that published rankings in the final pre-bowl week; missing-model estimates in Table 3 are imputed, which adds uncertainty [PAGE 9]. Fifth, multiple model comparisons are made without an explicit family-wise correction; the paper uses McNemar tests and reports individual p-values, but the number of comparisons is large [PAGE 8, PAGE 9]. Sixth, the historical context matters: the BCS existed from 1998–2013 and was replaced by the CFP in 2014; margin of victory was removed from BCS consideration in 2002, and the CFP also indicates it does not consider margin [PAGE 11]. Those governance changes alter the incentive environment that generates score differentials, which is a genealogical trap for any later model that treats historical margins as exchangeable with current margins.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The F-LRMC is a scoreboard-only model. The source says the basketball LRMC used only “the names of the winning and losing team, the margin of victory of the winning team, and whether the game was played on the winners home court, the losers court, or a neutral location” [PAGE 2], and the football version likewise relies on scoreboard data rather than play-by-play state [PAGE 7]. That creates a recursive theory-of-mind gap: the model does not represent in-game beliefs, clock state, possession, or coaching adjustments. Under crisis, teams may regress to habitual behavior—conservative play, repeated personnel packages, or clock-management habits—and that habit regression changes the distribution of final score differentials conditional on team strength. A counter-trap arises when opponents anticipate that habit and design a response; the final score differential then reflects the strategic interaction, not only the latent strength difference. The clock matters because a final margin can be produced by late scoring after the outcome is effectively decided; the model cannot distinguish a controlled win from a garbage-time inflation. The paper’s own governance discussion illustrates the recursive incentive: “the CFP have also indicated that they do not consider margin of victory in its rankings,” and “The motivation behind this non-inclusion is to prevent teams from running up the score during games” [PAGE 11]. If a ranking system rewards margin, teams and coaches may adapt by running up the score; that adaptation changes the data-generating process that the model uses. The source also notes that the F-LRMC has “the exclusive function as ranking systems” and that neutral-site games allow predictions to be inferred “directly from the rankings” [PAGE 5]. Thus the model’s recursive blind spot is that it treats the final score as a sufficient statistic while the clock, crisis habits, and counter-traps can make the final score a strategic artifact.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is physical, biomechanical, and weather-based. The source model has no covariates for weather, travel, rest, injury, field surface, or biomechanical fatigue. If those variables shift the score-differential distribution conditional on team strength, the common-opponent estimator is biased. Formally, let \(Y_g=x(g)\) be the final score differential, let \(\theta_i,\theta_j\) be latent team strengths, and let \(W_g\) be a weather or physical-context vector. The source model implicitly assumes
\[
P(Y_g=y\mid \theta_i,\theta_j,W_g)=P(Y_g=y\mid \theta_i,\theta_j),
\]
so that pooling across contexts is valid. The common-opponent estimator also assumes exchangeability:
\[
\mathbb E\left[\mu(G_j(g))+\nu(G_i(g))\mid C(g),x\right]=2|C(g)|r_x.
\]
If \(W_g\) affects either the mean or variance of \(Y_g\), then the pooled estimator targets a mixture
\[
r_x^{\text{pooled}}=\int r_{x,w}\,dF(w),
\]
not the neutral-context probability \(r_x\). A veto is justified when weather, travel, or injury is correlated with the common-opponent set or with the score differential. A non-veto is justified when \(W_g\) is independent of the comparison set and the score differential. The source itself flags one cross-layer assumption: “We also make the assumption that the field is truly neutral, despite some bowl games’ locations being slightly favored towards one team” [PAGE 5]. That is a physical-site veto trigger. The proof is conditional-independence failure: if the context variable is a confounder of the score differential and the common-opponent estimate, no aggregation over games can recover the intended \(r_x\) without stratifying or adjusting for that context.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Common-opponent coverage veto. Verification: compute the distribution of \(|\mathcal C(g)|\) for every \(G_x\). If a season has many games with \(|\mathcal C(g)|=0\), or if \(\sum_{g\in G_x}|C(g)|=0\) for an observed \(x\), equation (3) is undefined or unstable. Prediction: the fitted \(r_x\) curve will have wide resampling variance, and bowl-order accuracy will degrade when those sparse-\(x\) games are removed or when the common-opponent set is restricted. This is a verifiable veto because the denominator and coverage counts are directly computable from the schedule.

2. Neutral-site/travel asymmetry veto. Verification: compute stadium location, travel distance, and rest differential for each bowl game. The source already concedes that “some bowl games’ locations” may be “slightly favored towards one team” [PAGE 5]. Prediction: F-LRMC rank-order errors will be larger when travel-distance asymmetry is large, and the sign of the residual will correlate with the team that travels less. If the residual correlation is near zero, the veto is not supported.

3. Score-differential-manipulation veto. Verification: use play-by-play or scoring-summary data to separate pre-decision scoring from late scoring after the outcome is effectively settled. The paper says the model “relies heavily on margin of victory” [PAGE 10], and it notes that the CFP excludes margin “to prevent teams from running up the score during games” [PAGE 11]. Prediction: teams with large late-score margins will be ranked too highly relative to their pre-decision scoring margin, and their bowl-order accuracy will be worse than that of teams with similar pre-decision margins. This is verifiable because the late-score component can be recomputed and removed, then the model can be refit and compared out of sample.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
