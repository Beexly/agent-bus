The paper’s core causal chain is a clock-modeling chain, not a generic “add atomic clock, get better position” chain. In the observation model, the pseudorange contains geometric range, tropospheric and ionospheric delay, receiver and satellite clock terms, relativistic correction, and measurement noise [PAGE 4]. Without receiver clock modelling, the receiver clock bias is estimated epoch-by-epoch because the internal quartz oscillator has poor long-term frequency stability; this creates four unknowns at each epoch, weakens observation geometry, requires at least four satellites, and correlates the clock time bias with the up coordinate and tropospheric delay [PAGE 1]. With RCM, the receiver clock is represented as a physically constrained stochastic process over a finite modeling interval, the clock process-noise matrix is populated from oscillator stability coefficients, and the geometry is strengthened; in principle, kinematic positioning can then be computed with three satellites per epoch under clock coasting [PAGE 2]. The paper states the necessary physical hierarchy explicitly: “In RCM, it is mandatory that the oscillator noise is less than the GNSS receiver noise” [PAGE 3]. That condition is the first gray-zone audit: the MAC SA.35m oscillator is better than the GPS L1 C/A code and Doppler observation noise but not better than the carrier-phase noise, so “RCM cannot be applied with phase observations using this oscillator” [PAGE 3].

The second hop is the Kalman filter. The same code and Doppler observations are processed, but with or without RCM the clock process-noise matrix changes. The paper says “different results of the KF with and without applying RCM are exclusively due to the differences in the clock process noise models” [PAGE 6]. The oscillator’s Allan deviation is transformed into spectral-density coefficients \(h_\alpha\), and these enter \(Q_{\omega\omega-\mathrm{clk},k}\) via white frequency noise, flicker frequency noise, and random-walk frequency noise terms [PAGE 6]. Thus the improvement is not a measurement-domain correction; it is a prior on clock dynamics. The prior is valid only when the external oscillator’s fractional-frequency noise is below the observation noise at the relevant averaging time and over the RCM modeling duration. If the prior is false, the filter becomes overconfident in a physically wrong clock trajectory. The paper proves this on the JAVAD 0081 internal-TCXO dataset: introducing atomic-clock stability into the process noise matrix when the receiver is driven by its own internal TCXO “is not valid” [PAGE 12], and a drift appears in coordinates in segment B–C, with velocity drift more pronounced in the vertical component, while the integrated clock offset after a straight-line fit remains almost the same with and without RCM [PAGE 13]. Hence “one cannot just use better clock stability characteristics to process the datasets and improve systems accuracy, precision and overall robustness” [PAGE 13].

The third hop is the empirical result and its scope. The abstract claims about 70% precision improvement in height and vertical velocity with RCM for both receivers [PAGE 1]. In the body, the height precision improvement is 73% for JAVAD 0082 and 68% for u-blox 1771, and the vertical-velocity precision improvement is 68% and 72%, respectively [PAGE 14]. Accuracy improves by at least 48% and 62% in the up coordinate and velocity components with both receivers [PAGE 14]. So the correct inference is narrow: external-clock RCM primarily strengthens the vertical/time-correlated part of code-and-Doppler PVT under the tested urban conditions, while high-sensitivity hardware improves continuity but carries higher code and Doppler noise.

Second, wrong-clock prior failure: atomic-clock process noise applied to an internal TCXO causes drift. RCM reduces the vertical error but does not turn the HS receiver into a geodetic receiver, does not eliminate multipath, and does not create observations where none exist. That is the multi-hop conclusion: clock modeling is a conditional physical prior, not a universal accuracy multiplier.

+T_A^i+I_A^i+c(\delta t_A-\delta t^i)+\delta t_{A,rel}^i+\epsilon_A^i
\]
[PAGE 4]. Here \(\bar{l}_k^i\) is the computed pseudorange in metres; \(X,Y,Z\) are geocentric coordinates in metres; \(T_A^i\) and \(I_A^i\) are tropospheric and ionospheric delays in metres; \(c\) is the speed of light in m/s; \(\delta t_A\) and \(\delta t^i\) are receiver and satellite clock errors in seconds; \(\delta t_{A,rel}^i\) is the relativistic time offset; and \(\epsilon_A^i\) is measurement noise in metres. The paper accounts for ionospheric delay with IONEX TEC maps and tropospheric delay with Vienna Mapping Function 3, and corrects satellite clock and relativistic offsets with IGS MGEX orbit/clock products [PAGE 4]. The up-coordinate/clock/troposphere correlation is the structural reason RCM can help the vertical component: the clock state and vertical state are not statistically orthogonal in the raw model [PAGE 1].

The observation variance model is the SIGMA-\(\varepsilon\) model, Eq. (2.4):
\[
\sigma_{i,\varepsilon}^2=C_v\,10^{-(C/N_0)/10},\qquad Q_{ll}=\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\sigma_{i,\varepsilon}^2\right)
\]
[PAGE 5]. \(C/N_0\) is measured in dB-Hz, \(C_v\) is a receiver–antenna and GNSS-system-dependent variance factor in m\(^2\)-Hz, and \(\sigma_{i,\varepsilon}^2\) is in m\(^2\). The paper tabulates code variance factors for GPS/GLONASS/Galileo: JAVAD Delta TRE G3 T–JAVAD GRANT G3T is \(0.41\times10^5\), \(0.44\times10^5\), \(0.08\times10^5\); u-blox NEO M8 T–JAVAD GRANT G3T is \(0.38\times10^5\), \(0.695\times10^5\), \(0.165\times10^5\), all in m\(^2\)-Hz [PAGE 5]. This matters because the HS receiver does not receive the same effective weights: its hardware and \(C/N_0\) distribution produce different variance factors and larger residual noise.

The linearized Kalman filter uses the error-state update:
\[
\Delta\hat{x}_k^+=\Delta\hat{x}_k^-+K_k\!\left(\Delta l_k-A_k\Delta\hat{x}_k^-\right),
\]
\[
Q_{xx,k}^+=(I-K_kA_k)Q_{xx,k}^-(I-K_kA_k)^T+K_kQ_{ll,k}K_k^T,
\]
\[
K_k=Q_{xx,k}^-A_k^T\!\left(Q_{ll,k}+A_kQ_{xx,k}^-A_k^T\right)^{-1},
\]
and the prediction:
\[
\Delta x_{k+1}^-=\Phi_k\Delta x_k^+,\qquad
Q_{xx,k+1}^-=\Phi_kQ_{xx,k}^-\Phi_k^T+Q_{\omega\omega,k}
\]
[PAGE 5][PAGE 6]. The transition matrix \(\Phi_k\) is based on a constant-velocity model; position process noise is modeled as a random ramp and velocity process noise as a random walk [PAGE 6]. The Kalman-gain inverse is the algebraic singularity point: if \(Q_{ll,k}+A_kQ_{xx,k}^-A_k^T\) is rank-deficient or the observation set is absent/degenerate, no usable update exists. Empirically, the paper reports 39 missing solution epochs for JAVAD 0082, including 8 epochs with no data under underpasses [PAGE 17].

\[
Q_{\omega\omega-\mathrm{clk},k}=
\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{h_0\Delta t}{2}+2h_{-1}\Delta t^2+\frac{2\pi^2h_{-2}\Delta t^3}{3}
&
h_{-1}\Delta t+\pi^2h_{-2}\Delta t^2
\\
h_{-1}\Delta t+\pi^2h_{-2}\Delta t^2
&
\frac{h_0}{2\Delta t}+4h_{-1}+\frac{8\pi^2h_{-2}\Delta t}{3}
\end{bmatrix}
\]
[PAGE 6]. The applied coefficients are \(h_0=8\times10^{-22}\), \(h_{-1}=7.2\times10^{-25}\), and \(h_{-2}=3\times10^{-29}\), corresponding to white frequency noise, flicker frequency noise, and random-walk frequency noise [PAGE 6]. The units are chosen so that the matrix is dimensionally consistent with the clock time-offset/frequency-offset state pair; the important physical point is that these coefficients encode oscillator stability, not receiver geometry. Without RCM, the clock time offset is modeled as a random ramp and the clock frequency offset as a random walk [PAGE 6].

The underlying oscillator condition is expressed through Allan variance. The paper states that oscillator characterization is typically done with the time-dependent Allan variance \(\sigma_y^2\) and that this determines the time prediction errors and the RCM modeling duration over which oscillator noise is less than GNSS receiver noise [PAGE 3]. In standard form, for fractional-frequency averages \(\bar y_k\) over averaging time \(\tau\),
\[
\sigma_y^2(\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\bar y_{k+1}-\bar y_k)^2\right],
\qquad
\sigma_y(\tau)=\sqrt{\sigma_y^2(\tau)},
\]
where \(\sigma_y\) is dimensionless fractional-frequency stability. The admissibility condition is
\[
\sigma_{\mathrm{clk}}(\tau)\ll\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}(\tau)
\quad\text{or, spectrally,}\quad
S_{\mathrm{clk}}(f)\ll S_{\mathrm{obs}}(f).
\]
The paper gives practical observation-noise scales of about 1% of the signal wavelength: 3 m for L1 C/A code, 0.002 m for L1 carrier phase, and about 0.05 m/s for L1 Doppler [PAGE 3]. Because the MAC SA.35m noise is below code and Doppler noise but above carrier-phase noise, “RCM cannot be applied with phase observations using this oscillator” [PAGE 3]. Breakdown regimes are therefore explicit: (i) oscillator noise not below observation noise, especially phase; (ii) wrong clock prior, such as atomic-clock process noise on an internal TCXO; (iii) degenerate geometry or no observations, such as underpasses; and (iv) unmodeled multipath/NLOS causing outliers and vertical spikes. The paper’s outlier table reinforces the last point: with RCM, detected code and Doppler outliers are always higher than without RCM, and u-blox Doppler outliers are 34–44% of total observations versus 12–18% for JAVAD [PAGE 17].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage runs from frequency-stability metrology to GNSS clock coasting to low-cost urban navigation. The references include Barnes et al. (1971) for characterization of frequency stability and Allan (1987) for time-domain Allan-variance characterization [PAGE 18]. Those are the metrological ancestors of the \(h_\alpha\) coefficients and the idea that an oscillator has a measurable stability hierarchy as a function of averaging time. The navigation ancestor is Sturza (1983), cited for GPS navigation with three satellites and a precise clock, and Knable and Kalafus (1984), cited for clock coasting and altimeter error analysis [PAGE 18]. The paper itself restates that “For kinematic applications, positioning can be computed with just three satellites at each epoch with clock coasting” [PAGE 2]. The direct precursor line continues through Weinbach and Schön (2011) on high-precision oscillators and PPP, Krawinkel and Schön (2015, 2016) on chip-scale atomic clocks and code-based RCM, Bochkati and Schön (2018) on steering a low-cost GNSS receiver with a chip-scale atomic clock, Jain and Schön (2020) on flight navigation, and Jain et al. (2020) on miniaturized atomic clocks in static laboratory and dynamic flight environments [PAGE 18][PAGE 19]. This 2021 paper adds the urban kinematic case with a low-cost HS u-blox receiver and a geodetic JAVAD receiver both driven by the same Microsemi MAC SA.35m.

A retrocausal reading shows why the paper spends so much space on laboratory characterization at PTB and on common-clock verification. The RCM prior is only as good as the oscillator model. The paper compares manufacturer data with PTB laboratory-derived ADEV over averaging times from one second to about 17 minutes and finds agreement [PAGE 3]. That long averaging window is where multi-week or long-campaign traps emerge: a short open-sky test can make an oscillator look adequate, but an urban campaign with repeated loops, static stops, underpasses, and building-lined streets tests whether the clock model remains valid over the actual process-noise time constants. The experiment lasts about two hours and includes a repeated loop driven six times, with the first loop slightly longer [PAGE 7]. The repeated B–C route is precisely where JAVAD 0082 shows lower \(C/N_0\) and where invalid RCM on JAVAD 0081 produces coordinate and velocity drift [PAGE 9][PAGE 13]. The multi-week trap is not that the paper ran for weeks; it is that a single short demonstration can hide segment-dependent multipath, oscillator-model mismatch, and receiver-specific outlier behavior. The paper’s common-clock check is also genealogically important: both receivers driven by the same MAC SA.35m show clock offsets in the range of about \(\pm100\) ns and frequency offsets of about \(\pm10\) ns/s, verifying the common-clock mode [PAGE 14]. If a later campaign assumes common-clock behavior without this verification, the RCM prior may be internally inconsistent.

Another lineage trap is the confusion between “better clock” and “better navigation.” The paper explicitly contrasts its valid external-clock RCM with the invalid case of applying atomic-clock stability to an internal TCXO: the latter produces drift and does not reproduce the same clock-offset behavior after integration [PAGE 13]. The historical chain therefore has a counter-branch: Sturza-style clock coasting is powerful, but it depends on a physically matched oscillator. The paper also inherits the SIGMA-\(\varepsilon\) variance-model line from Hartinger and Brunner (1999) and Wieser et al. (2005), which is why the HS receiver’s \(C/N_0\)-dependent weighting is not optional bookkeeping [PAGE 18]. The retrocausal lesson is that the modern low-cost HS receiver does not invalidate RCM; it changes the noise budget, the outlier rate, and the availability profile. The paper’s cited prior results and its own experiment form a chain: metrology gives \(h_\alpha\), GNSS theory gives clock-augmented geometry, low-cost hardware gives availability but higher noise, and urban multipath gives the adversarial test.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis-habit regression in this domain is the reflex to treat an atomic clock as a universal fix. When urban GNSS degrades, a common operator habit is to add sensors, add a 3D city model, or inject “better clock stability” into the filter without checking the physical noise hierarchy. The paper directly counters the first habit by noting that multisensor systems improve performance but increase cost and limit low-cost applicability, and that aided techniques such as 3D city models and shadow matching are computationally expensive [PAGE 2]. It counters the clock reflex more sharply: applying atomic-clock process noise to a receiver driven by its own internal TCXO “is not valid” [PAGE 12], and “one cannot just use better clock stability characteristics to process the datasets and improve systems accuracy, precision and overall robustness” [PAGE 13]. The recursive-theory-of-mind point is that the Kalman filter does not know whether the clock prior is true; it will recursively propagate the prior into position, velocity, covariance, and outlier tests. A false clock prior therefore becomes a self-consistent but biased belief system.

The counter-trap is a verification sequence. First, characterize the oscillator and establish the Allan deviation over the intended averaging times; the paper uses laboratory PTB data and manufacturer data from 1 s to about 17 min [PAGE 3]. Second, check whether oscillator noise is below the observation noise; for this MAC SA.35m, code and Doppler pass, phase fails [PAGE 3]. Third, propagate the correct \(h_\alpha\) coefficients into the clock process-noise matrix rather than a generic “atomic clock” variance [PAGE 6]. Fourth, verify common-clock behavior if two receivers share an oscillator; the paper checks offsets near \(\pm100\) ns and \(\pm10\) ns/s [PAGE 14]. Fifth, use \(C/N_0\)-dependent variance factors and outlier detection, because urban observations are not homogeneous; the paper applies a local-slippage test and down-weights outliers [PAGE 12]. Sixth, compare with and without RCM by segment, because aggregate statistics can hide segment-specific failures. The paper’s Table 4 shows that the up-coordinate and vertical-velocity precision improvements occur across kinematic segments, while horizontal components do not show the same consistent gain [PAGE 15]. The recursive mental model must therefore be segment-aware, receiver-aware, and observation-type-aware.

“Clock” functions here as an epistemic referee rather than a magic state. The clock model tells the filter how much the receiver time and frequency offsets may wander between updates; if the referee is calibrated, it stabilizes the vertical/time-correlated subspace and reduces spikes. If the referee is mis-calibrated, it suppresses the observations’ ability to correct the drift. The paper’s outlier result is a useful counter-signal: “The number of code and Doppler outliers detected with RCM applied is always higher than without RCM; hence it increases the overall robustness of the system” [PAGE 17]. That sounds paradoxical only if one treats more outliers as failure. In this context, a tighter clock model changes residuals and exposes inconsistent observations that would otherwise be absorbed into a loose clock state. The recursive theory-of-mind counter-trap is to ask: is the filter rejecting more observations because the model is better, or because the model is overconfident? The paper’s control case answers this: with the internal TCXO and invalid RCM, the result is drift, not improved robustness [PAGE 13]. Thus the proper habit is not “trust the clock” but “audit the clock’s noise hierarchy, then trust the model only within its validated averaging time and observation type.”
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source does not contain a literal biomechanical layer; the closest admissible layer is vehicle kinematics and motion-model validity. Under that translation, the cross-layer veto is still strong. At the physical signal layer, the urban environment produces obstruction, multipath, NLOS, diffraction, building heights of about 20–30 m, narrow streets of about 4–6 m, underpasses, and an overhead bridge [PAGE 2][PAGE 7]. These are not nuisance labels; they appear as measured \(C/N_0\) reductions, code-noise spikes, Doppler-noise spread, and missing epochs. JAVAD 0082 records \(C/N_0\) mostly from about 20 to 55 dB-Hz, while u-blox 1771 records mostly from about 15 to 52 dB-Hz, and the HS receiver tracks more low-\(C/N_0\) observations in segment B–C [PAGE 9]. Doppler noise in static phases is about \(\pm1.5\) cm/s for JAVAD 0082 and \(\pm3.5\) cm/s for u-blox 1771, with full-series standard deviations of about 25 cm/s and 55 cm/s [PAGE 12]. These physical and hardware facts veto any claim that RCM makes the HS receiver equivalent to the geodetic receiver.

At the kinematic layer, the filter uses a constant-velocity model with position process noise as a random ramp and velocity process noise as a random walk [PAGE 6]. That model is valid only within its tuning. The paper notes that during static phases the velocity deviations are much smaller than during dynamic phases, and that the considerable difference between static and moving Doppler noise must be accounted for with adequate velocity variances [PAGE 11][PAGE 12]. RCM improves the vertical velocity precision, but it does not remove the need for motion-model tuning. The cross-layer veto is that a clock prior cannot compensate for a wrong motion model; it can only redistribute uncertainty within the clock-related subspace.

At the atmospheric/weather layer, the paper does not run a weather-sensitivity experiment, but it does model the relevant atmospheric delays: ionospheric delay with IONEX TEC maps and tropospheric delay with VMF3 [PAGE 4]. Because the clock time bias, up coordinate, and tropospheric delay are highly correlated [PAGE 1], any unmodeled or mismodeled atmospheric delay can project into the vertical/clock subspace and mimic or mask RCM benefit. The weather veto is therefore conditional: if atmospheric delays are not adequately modeled, a vertical improvement attributed to RCM may be partly a delay-partitioning effect. The source’s own result is more conservative than a universal claim: RCM improves height and vertical velocity precision by roughly 70%, but horizontal precision shows no significant gain, and the conclusions state that horizontal standard deviations with RCM are about 1.5 m and 2.5 m for JAVAD 0082 and u-blox 1771 [PAGE 1][PAGE 17]. The up-coordinate spikes are reduced, but the paper explicitly attributes large spikes without RCM to multipath along Marienstraße and recommends 3D city models or shadow matching for further reduction [PAGE 17]. Thus the veto is: RCM cannot be adjudicated as a general urban-positioning cure; it passes only for the vertical/time-correlated code-and-Doppler case with a validated external oscillator, and it fails for phase, internal TCXO, horizontal precision, no-observation epochs, and severe multipath unless additional layers correct those effects.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

1. **Invalid-clock counterfactual: apply RCM to JAVAD 0081 internal TCXO.** Scenario: take the exact \(h_\alpha\)-based atomic-clock process-noise matrix used for MAC SA.35m and apply it to the JAVAD 0081 dataset driven by its own internal TCXO. Verifiable prediction from the source: coordinates drift in segment B–C, velocity drift is more pronounced in the vertical component, and the clock-frequency offset becomes smoother under RCM while the linearly detrended clock-time offset after a straight-line fit is almost the same with and without RCM [PAGE 12][PAGE 13]. Veto condition: if a replication reports that this invalid RCM yields the same vertical precision gain as the true external-clock case, the physical-condition claim is falsified. If it reports drift and no true sense of RCM, the source’s veto is confirmed.

2. **Observation-type counterfactual: apply RCM to L1 carrier phase with MAC SA.35m.** Scenario: use the same external oscillator and attempt RCM with carrier-phase observations. Verifiable prediction: the admissibility condition fails because the MAC SA.35m noise is smaller than GPS L1 C/A code and Doppler noise but higher than carrier-phase noise; the paper explicitly states “RCM cannot be applied with phase observations using this oscillator” [PAGE 3]. Veto condition: if a phase-RCM run claims a clean improvement using this oscillator without a lower-noise phase-capable clock, the noise-hierarchy claim is violated. If the phase run shows no valid RCM benefit or instability, the veto is confirmed.

3. **No-observation counterfactual: underpass and missing-epoch recovery.** Scenario: evaluate availability in the underpass/overhead-bridge segment and claim that RCM alone recovers all epochs. Verifiable prediction: the u-blox 1771 has a solution at all epochs, while JAVAD 0082 misses 39 epochs, and 8 of those are epochs with no data recorded as the vehicle crossed multiple underpasses [PAGE 17]. Veto condition: if a counterfactual claims RCM created a valid solution during the 8 no-data epochs without additional aiding, the availability claim is falsified. If the replication shows missing epochs for JAVAD and full availability for u-blox 1771, the source’s distinction between clock modeling and observation availability is confirmed. A secondary verifiable marker is the outlier structure: with RCM applied, detected code and Doppler outliers are always higher than without RCM, and u-blox Doppler outliers are 34–44% of total observations versus 12–18% for JAVAD [PAGE 17]. A counterfactual that reports fewer outliers under RCM without changing weighting, elevation mask, or data would therefore be suspect.
