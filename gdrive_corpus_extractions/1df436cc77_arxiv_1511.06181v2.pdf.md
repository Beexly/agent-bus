The source’s inference chain is a multi-hop reduction from image evidence to a globally optimal, physically constrained state trajectory. First, the authors define a factor graph over the 3D ball location \(X_t\), ball state \(S_t\), and image evidence \(I_t\), and they state that “we can formulate our tracking problem as one of maximizing the energy function” \(\Psi(X,S,I)\) [PAGE 3]. The posterior is factorized into image-evidence, state-transition, and location-change potentials, Eq. (1) [PAGE 3]. Taking logarithms and dropping \(\log Z\) yields a maximum a posteriori sequence over feasible \((X,S)\in\mathcal{F}\), Eq. (2) [PAGE 3]. The next hop converts this into an integer program over a ball graph \(G_b=(V_b,E_b)\): nodes carry \(x_i,s_i,t_i\), edges connect consecutive-time nodes, and binary flow variables \(f_i^j\) select one path. The objective is \(\max \sum_{(i,j)\in E_b} f_i^j c_{bi}^j\), with \(c_{bi}^j=\log\Psi_X+\log\Psi_S+\log\Psi_I\), subject to Eqs. 3(a–f) [PAGE 3]. Constraints 3(b–c) enforce one ball at each frame and flow conservation; 3(d–e) recover \(X_t,S_t\) from selected flows; 3(f) restricts to physically feasible sequences [PAGE 3–4].

The third hop adds continuous true positions \(P^t=(P_x^t,P_y^t,P_z^t)\), with \(\|P^t-X_t\|\le D_l\), Eq. (4) [PAGE 4]. This decouples the discrete candidate grid from exact physical motion. The fourth hop is the second-order constraint, Eq. (5): \(A_{s,c}(P_c^t-2P_c^{t-1}+P_c^{t-2})+B_{s,c}(P_c^t-P_c^{t-1})+C_{s,c}P_c^t-F^{s,c}\le K(3-M_{s,c}^t-M_{s,c}^{t-1}-M_{s,c}^{t-2})\) [PAGE 4]. The indicator \(M_{s,c}^t\) is one when the selected node is in state \(s\), coordinate \(c\), and not in the collision set \(O_{s,c}\). The right-hand side is zero only when all three indicators are one; otherwise it is \(K\) or \(2K\), so the physical constraint is inactive. This is the key gray-zone: the paper calls \(K\) a “large positive constant” but gives no numerical scaling, no units, and no solver-tolerance analysis [PAGE 4]. If \(K\) is too small, inactive constraints leak; if \(K\) is too large, numerical conditioning and integrality tolerances can dominate. The constraint activation is also a piecewise discontinuity at the triple \(\{M^t,M^{t-1},M^{t-2}\}=(1,1,1)\). The source explicitly says “we turn off these constraints altogether at locations in \(O_{s,c}\)” [PAGE 4], so an incorrectly specified collision set can silently accept nonphysical motion.

The fifth hop adds possession. The paper says “Possession implies that the ball must be near the player” [PAGE 4], and Eq. (7) requires player-flow mass within \(D_p\) of a possession-state ball node to be at least the incoming ball flow [PAGE 5]. \(D_p\) is sport-specific and is not numerically specified in the main text. The final MIP combines ball flows and player flows, Eq. (8), and is solved with Gurobi [PAGE 5]. The gray-zone audit must flag several source-level breaks. The player detector used in the motivating example “only ﬁnds people standing up” [PAGE 2], so a defending player on the ground is missed and possession evidence can disappear. The physical model neglects friction and has no wind or air-drag term; the source says “In both cases, we neglect the effect of friction” [PAGE 4]. Also, the written physical description and the displayed coefficient choices are not perfectly aligned: the text says “zero acceleration” and “constant speed motion,” but the inequality with \(A=1,B=0,C=0,F=0\) gives a one-sided second-difference constraint, not equality [PAGE 4]. The event metric tolerates “not more than 5 frames” of mismatch [PAGE 7], which can hide brief state errors. Finally, the global MIP can use future frames to revise earlier labels, which is powerful but makes error attribution nonlocal: a bad future detection can change a past state through the state-transition and second-order terms [PAGE 2–4].
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines a factor-graph posterior and then compiles it into a Mixed Integer Program. The core formal objects are as follows, with page citations and stated assumptions.

1. Posterior energy, Eq. (1) [PAGE 3]:
\[
\Psi(X,S,I)=\frac{1}{Z}\Psi_I(X_1,S_1,I_1)\prod_{t=2}^{T}\Big[\Psi_X(X_{t-1},S_{t-1},X_t)\Psi_S(S_{t-1},S_t)\Psi_I(X_t,S_t,I_t)\Big].
\]
The source says “we can formulate our tracking problem as one of maximizing the energy function” [PAGE 3]. \(X_t\in\mathbb{R}^3\) is in metres, \(S_t\) is a discrete state label, \(I_t\) is image evidence, and \(\Psi\) terms are potentials proportional to probabilities. The normalization \(Z\) is dropped after taking logs. If natural logarithms are used, the additive costs are in nats; if base-2 logarithms are used, they are in bits. The paper does not state the logarithm base, so the information unit is convention-dependent; probabilities themselves are dimensionless.

2. Log-MAP objective, Eq. (2) [PAGE 3]:
\[
(X^*,S^*)=\arg\max_{(X,S)\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{t=2}^{T}\Big[\log\Psi_X(X_{t-1},S_{t-1},S_t)+\log\Psi_S(S_{t-1},S_t)+\log\Psi_I(X_t,S_t,I_t)\Big]+\log\Psi_I(X_1,S_1,I_1).
\]
The feasible set \(\mathcal{F}\) is the set of admissible ball-location and state sequences. The source later expands \(\mathcal{F}\) with physics and possession constraints [PAGE 3–5].

3. Ball-graph integer program, Eq. (3) [PAGE 3–4]:
\[
\max \sum_{(i,j)\in E_b} f_i^j c_{bi}^j,
\quad
c_{bi}^j=\log\Psi_X(x_i,s_i,x_j)+\log\Psi_S(s_i,s_j)+\log\Psi_I(x_j,s_j,I_{t_j}),
\]
subject to \(f_i^j\in\{0,1\}\), one incoming flow at \(t_j=1\), flow conservation for \(0\le t_j\le T\), and \(X_t=\sum f_i^j x_j\), \(S_t=\sum f_i^j s_j\). The \(f_i^j\) are dimensionless binary flow variables; \(c_{bi}^j\) are log-potential costs in nats or bits; \(x_j\) is in metres. The paper states “We optimize with respect to the \(f_i^j\), which can be considered as flow variables” [PAGE 4].

4. Continuous-position relaxation, Eq. (4) [PAGE 4]:
\[
\|P^t-X_t\|\le D_l.
\]
\(P^t\) is the true 3D ball position in metres, \(X_t\) is the discrete candidate location in metres, and \(D_l\) is a maximum permissible distance in metres. \(D_l\) “depends on the expected accuracy of the \(x_i\)” [PAGE 4], but no numerical value is given in the main text. Breakdown regime: if \(D_l\) is too small, Eq. (4) can make the MIP infeasible; if \(D_l\) is too large, the continuous physics variable is only weakly tied to the discrete detections.

5. Second-order physical constraint, Eq. (5) [PAGE 4]:
\[
A_{s,c}(P_c^t-2P_c^{t-1}+P_c^{t-2})+B_{s,c}(P_c^t-P_c^{t-1})+C_{s,c}P_c^t-F^{s,c}\le K(3-M_{s,c}^t-M_{s,c}^{t-1}-M_{s,c}^{t-2}),
\]
with
\[
M_{s,c}^t=\sum_{(i,j)\in E_b,\,t_j=t,\,s_j=s,\,x_j\notin O_{s,c}} f_i^j.
\]
Here \(M_{s,c}^t\) is dimensionless and binary under Eq. (3). \(K\) is “a large positive constant” [PAGE 4] and has whatever units are needed to dominate the left side; the source does not state those units. \(O_{s,c}\) is the set of collision-allowed locations such as “near the basketball hoops or close to the ground” [PAGE 4]. The source gives the flying-ball z-coordinate tuple as \((1,0,0,-g/\mathrm{fps}^2)\) “to model the parabolic motion of an object subject to the sole force of gravity whose intensity is \(g\)” [PAGE 4]. With \(g\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) and fps in \(\mathrm{Hz}\), the discrete second difference has units of \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) after multiplication by \(\mathrm{fps}^2\), so the tuple is dimensionally coherent only if \(F^{s,c}\) carries acceleration units and \(A,B,C\) absorb the necessary frame-rate powers. The source does not provide a full dimensional table. For rolling x and y it gives \((1,0,0,0)\) “to denote a constant speed motion in the xy plane” and says “In both cases, we neglect the effect of friction” [PAGE 4]. Gray-zone breakdown: the inequality is one-sided; with \(A=1,B=0,C=0,F=0\), it enforces a second difference \(\le 0\), i.e. a nonpositive acceleration constraint, not exact constant speed. The text’s “zero acceleration” and “constant speed” language is therefore stronger than the displayed inequality. The constraint is active only when \(M_{s,c}^t=M_{s,c}^{t-1}=M_{s,c}^{t-2}=1\); if any indicator is zero, the right side is \(K\) or \(2K\), and the physical constraint is inactive. If \(K\) is too small relative to the residual, inactive constraints bind; if \(K\) is too large, solver conditioning and feasibility tolerances become the effective model. At \(x_j\in O_{s,c}\), the constraint is deliberately disabled, so collision-set errors are a physical-layer breakdown.

6. Possession constraint, Eq. (7) [PAGE 5]:
\[
\sum_{(k,l)\in E_p,\,t_l=t_j,\,\|x_j-x_l\|_2\le D_p} p_k^l \ge \sum_{i:(i,j)\in E_b} f_i^j
\quad \forall j: s_j\equiv \text{in possession}.
\]
\(p_k^l\) is a dimensionless player-flow variable, \(x_j,x_l\) are in metres, and \(D_p\) is “the maximum possible distance between the player and the ball location when the player is in control of it, which is sport-specific” [PAGE 5]. Breakdown: if \(D_p\) is too small, a true possession can be infeasible; if too large, a nearby but non-possessing player can satisfy the constraint. The source’s motivating example shows the detector “only ﬁnds people standing up,” so a grounded player can be missed [PAGE 2].

7. Combined MIP, Eq. (8) [PAGE 5]:
\[
\max \sum_{(i,j)\in E_b} f_i^j c_{bi}^j+\sum_{(i,j)\in E_p} p_i^j c_{pi}^j
\]
subject to Eqs. 3(a–e), 4, 5, 6(a–c), and 7. The source says “In practice, we use the Gurobi [12] solver to perform the optimization” [PAGE 5]. The player-flow cost is \(c_{pi}^j=\log P_p(x_i|I_{t_i})/(1-\log P_p(x_i|I_{t_i}))\) as printed in the source [PAGE 4], which is not a standard log-odds form because the denominator contains \(1-\log P\), not \(1-P\). That is a formal gray-zone in the printed equation; it should be treated as source text rather than silently corrected.

8. Potentials, Eqs. (9–11) [PAGE 5]:
\[
\Psi_I(x_i,s_i,I)=\psi(x_i,s_i,I)\prod_{j\in V_b:t_j=t,\,(x_j,s_j)\ne(x_i,s_i)}(1-\psi(x_j,s_j,I)),
\]
\[
\psi(x,s,I)=\sigma_s(P_b(x|I)P_c(s|x,I)),\qquad
\sigma_s(y)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\theta_{s0}-\theta_{s1}y}},
\]
\[
\Psi_S(s_i,s_j)=P(S_t=s_i|S_{t-1}=s_j),
\qquad
\Psi_X(x_i,s_i,x_j)=\mathbf{1}(\|x_i-x_j\|_2\le D_{s_i}).
\]
\(P_b\) and \(P_c\) are dimensionless probabilities; \(\theta_{s0},\theta_{s1}\) are learned logistic parameters; \(D_{s_i}\) is a maximum speed-distance in metres per frame step under state \(s_i\). Eq. (11) is a hard indicator, so it is a discontinuous feasibility gate: a candidate edge is either allowed or forbidden. The paper says the \(\Psi\) functions “are learned from training data” [PAGE 3] and that the state transition is learned as \(P(S_t=s_i|S_{t-1}=s_j)\) [PAGE 5]. Breakdown: the state set and transition prior are sport-specific; “strike” for volleyball and “pass” for basketball are named in the source [PAGE 2], so cross-sport transfer without retraining is not justified.

9. Metrics. Tracking accuracy is “the percent of frames in which the location of the tracked ball is closer than \(d\) to the ground truth location” [PAGE 7]. Event accuracy matches maximal state sequences if “there are not more than 5 frames during which one occurs and not the other” [PAGE 7]. The 5-frame tolerance is a verification gray-zone: brief state inversions can be counted as correct.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage is a fusion of two older families. The first is trajectory-segment growing: the source cites soccer, basketball, and volleyball systems that start from consecutive detections and greedily extend physical segments, often with sport-specific heuristics such as audio cues or adapted motion parameters [PAGE 1–2]. The second is global energy minimization, including K-shortest-path tracking [2], probabilistic occupancy maps [9], and integer-programming interaction tracking [28,29] [PAGE 2, 5]. The paper’s “retrocausal” behavior is not a physical time-reversal claim; it is the global MIP’s ability to use later state transitions and second-order constraints to revise earlier labels. Figure 1 makes this explicit: “Transitions between these states can only result from interacting with a player, which encourages the optimizer to find one in spite of the weak evidence” [PAGE 2]. In other words, a future “flying” state can retroactively support a past “strike” state, and a later ballistic segment can veto an earlier possession label. This is global inference, not causal reversal.

The genealogy also includes the solver and detector stack: probabilistic occupancy maps and K-shortest paths for people [2,9], SVM/HOG/HSV/motion-histogram ball detection, Random Forest state classification, and Gurobi MIP optimization [PAGE 5]. The historical trap is that each component was developed on a particular sport, camera layout, and annotation density. The source reports manual annotation of every 10th frame in Basket-1, 500 consecutive frames in Basket-2, every third frame in 1500-frame volleyball segments, and 1000-frame soccer splits [PAGE 6]. Multi-week deployment traps follow directly: camera calibration drift, lighting changes, and background motion change the SVM and occupancy-map inputs; the paper already notes that Basket-APIDIS is “challenging because the camera locations are not good for ball tracking and lighting conditions are difficult” [PAGE 6]. A second multi-week trap is state-prior drift: \(P(S_t|S_{t-1})\) and the logistic parameters \(\theta_{s0},\theta_{s1}\) are learned, so a new sport or a new officiating style can shift the state distribution without changing the geometric detections. A third trap is the collision set \(O_{s,c}\): it is manually or structurally defined around hoops and ground, and if the scene changes, the physics constraints are either wrongly disabled or wrongly enforced [PAGE 4]. A fourth trap is the player detector’s standing-person bias, which the authors themselves identify in the motivating sequence [PAGE 2]. Thus the paper’s lineage is strong for controlled multi-camera team-sport sequences, but its assumptions are not a general physical theory of all ball motion.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The paper’s “theory of mind” is narrow and operational: it does not model player intentions, deception, or biomechanics. It models possession as a proximity-and-flow condition, Eq. (7), and it models ball state transitions as a learned Markov prior, Eq. (10) [PAGE 4–5]. The recursive part is that ball tracking and player tracking can be optimized jointly: the MIP objective in Eq. (8) contains both ball flows and player flows, and the authors report that joint optimization is “only slightly more expensive but yields improvements” in cases such as Figure 1 [PAGE 5]. The crisis habit regression appears when the player detector fails. In the motivating volleyball sequence, the defending player is on the ground and “was not detected by the person detector we use [9] because it only finds people standing up” [PAGE 2]. In that regime, the model can regress to a physics-only or state-prior-only habit: it may label the ball as flying because no possession flow is available, even when a human would infer possession from the visible interaction. The counter-trap is the future-state constraint: a later flying segment, plus the requirement that state transitions be plausible, can push the optimizer to infer an interacting player despite weak evidence [PAGE 2]. The clock is the frame index and sequence length. The source reports that “As the number of frames decreases, the people tracker becomes less reliable but performing the tracking simultaneously yields a small but noticeable improvement both for the ball and the players” [PAGE 8]. Table 2’s caption states the three numbers are “simultaneous tracking of the players and ball / sequential tracking of the players and then the ball / improvement, as function of the lengths of the sequences” [PAGE 8]. The practical clock trap is that live or short-window use reduces the reliability of the people tracker, so the recursive correction is real but small. The model also has a habit-regression risk in event labeling: the event metric allows a 5-frame mismatch [PAGE 7], so a brief possession-to-flight inversion can be absorbed by the metric and become a habitual false positive. The correct counter-trap is to inspect the actual \(M_{s,c}^t\) activation and Eq. (7) flow slack, not just the aggregate event score.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto. For the flying state, with the source’s z-coordinate tuple \((1,0,0,-g/\mathrm{fps}^2)\), the active constraint is
\[
R_z^t=(P_z^t-2P_z^{t-1}+P_z^{t-2})+\frac{g}{\mathrm{fps}^2}\le 0
\]
when \(M_{z,\text{flying}}^t=M_{z,\text{flying}}^{t-1}=M_{z,\text{flying}}^{t-2}=1\) and \(x_j\notin O_{z,\text{flying}}\) [PAGE 4]. If \(R_z^t\) exceeds the solver feasibility tolerance, the candidate trajectory is physically vetoed unless the optimizer changes state, changes discrete location, or enters a collision set. The constraint is one-sided, so it permits downward acceleration stronger than \(g\); it is not an equality to free-fall. For rolling x and y, the displayed \((1,0,0,0)\) tuple gives a second-difference upper bound, not exact constant speed [PAGE 4]. Therefore a “constant-speed” claim cannot be used as a strict cross-layer veto without adding an equality or a two-sided bound. The source also says “we neglect the effect of friction” [PAGE 4], so any real rolling deceleration is outside the model.

Biomechanical-layer veto. The only biomechanical bridge is Eq. (7): a possession-state ball must have a player flow within \(D_p\) [PAGE 5]. This is a proximity veto, not a limb-contact or force veto. If a player is within \(D_p\) but not actually controlling the ball, Eq. (7) can pass; if a player is controlling the ball but not detected, Eq. (7) can fail. The source’s own example shows the detector missing a grounded player [PAGE 2]. Thus the biomechanical layer can veto a possession label only when the player-flow field is trustworthy; otherwise the veto is incomplete.

Weather-layer veto. There is no weather state variable in Eqs. (1–11), no wind vector, no air-density term, and no drag term. The source explicitly neglects friction and models flying z as “the sole force of gravity” [PAGE 4]. Therefore the model cannot adjudicate wind or weather. Operationally, if a wind-induced acceleration \(a_w\) is present, the active flying residual becomes approximately \((a_z-a_w)/\mathrm{fps}^2+g/\mathrm{fps}^2\); the MIP can only respond by changing state or entering \(O_{s,c}\), not by estimating weather. A weather veto is therefore external to the source: it must be supplied by an additional physical model, and without it the paper’s physical constraints are not valid under significant wind.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following three verifiable-reward veto scenarios use only the source’s own constraints. They are counterfactual checks: if the condition fails, the candidate output is rejected or penalized; if it passes, the output remains admissible.

1. Active second-order residual veto. Condition: for a candidate node triple at times \(t-2,t-1,t\), the selected state and coordinate satisfy \(M_{s,c}^t=M_{s,c}^{t-1}=M_{s,c}^{t-2}=1\), and none of the selected locations is in \(O_{s,c}\). Verifier: compute
\[
R=A_{s,c}(P_c^t-2P_c^{t-1}+P_c^{t-2})+B_{s,c}(P_c^t-P_c^{t-1})+C_{s,c}P_c^t-F^{s,c}.
\]
If \(R\) exceeds the numerical tolerance for the active constraint in Eq. (5), veto. Prediction: the MIP must either change the state sequence, change the discrete location, or enter a collision set; if all candidate detections are fixed and no such alternative exists, the instance is infeasible. This veto directly tests whether the “physics” claim is real rather than a disabled \(K\)-slack [PAGE 4].

2. Possession-proximity veto. Condition: a selected ball node \(j\) has \(s_j\equiv\) in possession. Verifier: evaluate Eq. (7), i.e. check whether the sum of player-flow mass within \(D_p\) of \(x_j\) is at least the incoming ball flow. If the sum is strictly smaller, veto. Prediction: the optimizer will either relabel the ball to flying or rolling, move the ball node, or, in joint optimization, increase a nearby player flow. If the player detector has missed a grounded player, the veto can be a false negative; the verifier should then flag “detector-limited” rather than “physically impossible” [PAGE 2, 5].

3. Flow-conservation and single-ball veto. Condition: a candidate output assigns two distinct ball locations or two distinct states at the same time \(t\), or violates conservation between consecutive frames. Verifier: check Eq. (3)(b–c) and the definitions \(X_t=\sum f_i^j x_j\), \(S_t=\sum f_i^j s_j\). If more than one unit of flow enters time \(t\), or if incoming and outgoing flows differ at an intermediate node, veto. Prediction: the solver must re-route the ball path; if the detections force a gap, the correct output is the “not present” state or an infeasible instance, not a duplicated ball. This veto is purely combinatorial and therefore highly verifiable; it prevents a high image-evidence score from overriding the one-ball-per-frame structure [PAGE 3–4].
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
