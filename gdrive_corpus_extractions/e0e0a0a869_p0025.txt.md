<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The disclosed architecture sits inside a well-established lineage of spatial sports analytics, but extends it with explicit monotonic geometric conditioning. The intellectual ancestor is the expected-goals (xG) tradition, wherein shot-location features map to scoring probability; early forms used logistic regression on distance and angle, later generalized to gradient-boosted trees and deep networks. The patent's two-stage structure—initial probability followed by feature-based modification—mirrors the residual correction pattern common in modern xG models that add defensive pressure and goalkeeper positioning as post-hoc modifiers. The use of a virtual line between shooter and goal echoes the "goal-line angle" and "shooting angle" geometry found in public tracking literature, while the defender-to-line distance d⊥ resembles the vertical distance-to-line metrics used in pitch-control and passing-risk models (e.g., Voronoi-based defensive coverage). The clarity value C is conceptually adjacent to "intervening player" counts in passing models and to shot-blockade features in possession-value frameworks.

The GAN component traces to generative adversarial networks (Goodfellow et al., 2014), though applying GANs to probability regression is unusual; most production sports probability models use discriminative supervised losses rather than adversarial objectives. The monotonic-constraint clause invokes shape-constrained neural networks (e.g., monotonic multi-layer perceptrons), where partial derivatives are constrained by sign so that, for instance, increasing defender proximity cannot increase scoring probability. Those constraints require a specified direction per feature; the patent leaves σᵢ and wᵢ unspecified, which is a departure from rigorous shape-constrained practice.

Multi-week traps are present throughout. Goalkeeper save-probability permutations π_g are likely trained on historical match data, but goalkeeper form, tactical instructions (sweeper-keeper vs. stay-on-line), and fatigue evolve week-to-week, so π_g learned from matches three weeks ago may misrepresent current save likelihood. The distance feature d⊥ depends on team tactical shape: a high defensive line compresses d⊥ systematically across a season, while a low block inflates it; a model that does not re-estimate the d⊥ coefficient after a managerial change will condition on stale geometry. Shot archetype distributions also drift: set-piece frequency changes with fixture congestion, and central-vs-lateral shot mix shifts as opponents adjust their defensive compactness. Weather-driven surface changes alter the effective ε for the clarity value because wet conditions reduce sliding tackle range, changing how many players realistically occupy the line's tubular neighborhood. Without a rolling re-validation window (e.g., last six matches), the feature set is exposed to distributional shift that the static claim language does not address.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The model operates under a recursive theory-of-mind structure in which the shooter, the defending goalkeeper, and the prediction model each maintain beliefs about the others' intentions. Crisis habit regression describes the tendency of athletes under high-stakes pressure to revert to historically dominant motor patterns: a shooter may favor their stronger foot or a preferred sector of the goal, and a goalkeeper may pre-commit to a habitual dive direction. If the feature set includes previous action type and shot archetype, the model can detect when an action deviates from the subject's crisis baseline; however, if the model over-weights the archetype prior, it will underestimate the probability of an atypical but high-value action.

The counter-trap is the adversarial layer: because the model's updated probability is a deterministic function of observable spatial features, an opposing coach who knows the weighting can manipulate those features to induce a misprediction. For example, if the model assigns high scoring probability when d⊥ is large (defender far from the virtual line), the defending team may deliberately station a player off the line to inflate d⊥ and thereby cause the broadcast or analytics feed to overstate the shooting threat, potentially influencing tactical substitutions or press narratives. Conversely, if the model penalizes low d⊥, the attacking team may run a decoy toward the line to suppress the model's scoring probability estimate and create space elsewhere. The model must therefore treat d⊥ and C as potentially strategic rather than purely physical.

The clock imposes non-stationarity on top of these psychological layers. Match time τ ∈ [0, 90] minutes changes the joint distribution of (d⊥, C, A): late-game fatigue increases player spacing (larger d⊥ on average) but reduces sprint recovery (higher effective C during transitions), while trailing teams shoot from wider angles (larger θ) and leading teams protect the central corridor. The crisis habit regression is therefore time-dependent: the same feature vector at τ = 15 min implies a different behavioral distribution than at τ = 85 min. A complete recursion must condition the update operator on τ and on recent crisis context (e.g., the last five actions of the same player), otherwise the model confuses a late-game structural shift with a crisis-driven habit change. The counter-trap is also clock-sensitive: tactical manipulation of d⊥ is cheaper early in the match when defensive discipline is high, and riskier late when fatigue forces compactness. Without modeling the interaction of crisis regression, counter-trap incentive, and clock, the probability update P¹_score = ℳ(ℐ⁰, 𝓕) is only a partial equilibrium, not a fixed point of the strategic game.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The output probability must be vetoed whenever any physical, biomechanical, or weather layer contradicts the spatial feature assumptions, because the model conditions on player positions as if they are causally reachable states. The veto stack is adjudicated in order: physical layer first, biomechanical second, weather third.

Physical veto: if the event timestamp is flagged as out of play, offside, or the ball is not in the field of play, the spatial configuration ℐ⁰ is not a live game state and all spatial features are void. For soccer specifically, [PAGE 2] notes "the first action includes a shot on the goal...the defending player is a goalkeeper," but if the offensive player is in an offside position at the moment of the shot, the goal is disallowed and the scoring probability for that event must be set to zero regardless of d⊥ or θ. Similarly, if p₁ or p_o lies outside the boundary lines of S, the feature vector is geometrically invalid.

Biomechanical veto: human motion imposes hard bounds on position sequences. Given sampling interval Δt and maximum muscular acceleration a_max (≈ 6 m/s² for elite sprint acceleration, lower on grass), any observed jump ∥p(t+Δt) − p(t)∥₂ > a_max·Δt²/2 is kinematically impossible and indicates tracking corruption. If such a jump occurs for p_o or p_d within the feature-extraction window, the derived virtual line L and distance d⊥ are built on impossible coordinates and must be vetoed. Additionally, goalkeeper reaction time τ_r (≈ 0.2–0.3 s) and maximum diving velocity impose a physical upper bound on save probability for close-range shots; if the model's P¹_save exceeds the physical bound derived from ball travel time and τ_r, the output is clipped at that bound rather than trusted.

Weather veto: precipitation reduces the effective coefficient of friction μ between boot and surface, which changes the feasible curvature of a shot and the maximal lateral acceleration of a defender. If a weather feed reports μ < 0.4 (wet grass) during the action window, static position features sampled at standard tracking rates may misrepresent post-contact ball trajectory; the angular permutation π_g(θ), which assumes a dry-surface shot dispersion model, becomes unreliable. Under a weather veto, the model must either (a) fall back to non-spatial features only (previous action type, shot archetype) or (b) inflate the epistemic uncertainty around P¹_score rather than issuing a point estimate.

Adjudication result: if any veto fires, the system returns a null or prior-valued output instead of P¹_score. The veto stack is composable; a single weather veto does not override a physical offside veto, but both are independently sufficient to suppress the spatial update.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
