Change of coordinates \(\zeta=z+\xi\) can remove it (Page 180, Example 5.7), but nonresonance \(\lambda_i(A)\neq\lambda_j(F)\) is required for the Sylvester equation \(FN-NA=M\) (Page 181-182).

14. Differentiability of cross-term (Page 187-194, Theorem 5.11, Cor. 5.12, Thm. 5.14):
\(\Psi\in C^1\) under Assumptions 5.1, 5.2, 5.10 (restriction on \(f(z)\) form, Page 188). \(\Psi\in C^r\) if limit sets of \(\dot z=f(z)\) are equilibria and eigenvalues at equilibria have real parts \(<1/(r\alpha)\) (Assumption 5.13, Page 192; Theorem 5.14). Breakdown Page 193-194, Example 5.15: multiple equilibria with different stability boundaries make \(\Psi\) non-differentiable (integral diverges).

15. Fast unmodeled dynamics and singular perturbations (Page 85-86, Eq. 3.2.5; Page 90, Theorem 3.18):
\(\dot x=f_c(x,z,u),\ \mu\dot z=q_c(x,z,u)\). Nominal \(\mu=0\) gives slow model \(\dot x=f_c(x,h(x,u),u)\). Theorem 3.18: if reduced model GAS+LES and fast subsystem GAS+LES uniformly in \(x\), then for every compact \(C_x\times C_z\) there exists \(\mu^*>0\) such that for \(0<\mu\le\mu^\star\), equilibrium \((0,0)\) is asymptotically stable with region of attraction containing \(C_x\times C_z\). Called “semiglobal in \(\mu\)” (Page 90). Breakdown Page 85-86, Example 3.17: single-link manipulator joint flexibility yields relative-degree-three perturbation block, non-passive, cannot be handled by disk margins; requires time-scale separation.

16. Peaking and nonpeaking systems (Page 154, Def. 4.30, Eq. 4.5.6):
\((A,B,C), y=C\xi\) is nonpeaking if \(\exists\) bounded \(u\) such that \(\|y(t)\|\le\gamma\|\xi(0)\|(e^{-\sigma at}+1/a)\), \(\gamma,\sigma\) independent of \(a\). Theorem 4.40 (Page 162-163): nonpeaking iff stabilizable, right-invertible, no zeros in open RHP. Prop. 4.37 (Page 160-161): for chain of integrators with output \(y=\xi_i\), state \(\xi_{i+k}\) peaks with exponent \(\pi=k\) and \(\xi_{i-k}\) with \(\pi=k-1\). Breakdown Page 167-168, Theorem 4.43: if \((A,B,C)\) peaking, \(\exists f,\tilde\psi\) such that cascade is not semiglobally stabilizable; strictly nonminimum-phase zero \(\nu>0\) forces output “energy” that perturbs \(z\) to finite-time escape (Eq. 4.5.40, Page 168).

17. Backstepping recursion (Page 235-238, Prop. 6.1, Eq. 6.1.24):
Strict-feedback \(\dot z=f+g\xi_1,\ \dot\xi_i=a_i+b_i\xi_{i+1},\ \dot\xi_n=a_n+b_nu\). Outputs \(y_i=\xi_i-\alpha_{i-1}\). Exact backstepping \(b_i^{-1}(-a_i-y_{i-1}+\dot\alpha_{i-1}-y_i)\). Breakdown: analytical complexity of \(\dot\alpha_i\) grows; high-gain replacement \(\dot\alpha_{i-1}\approx -ky_i\) can shrink region of attraction (Page 244-245, Example 6.4: Van der Pol reversed-time limit cycle, condition \(k\xi_1^2+k^{-1}\xi_2^2>3\) yields unboundedness).

18. Forwarding recursion (Page 250-253):
Strict-feedforward \(\dot x_1=x_2+x_2^3+x_2u,\ \dot x_2=x_3-x_2^3u,\ \dot x_3=u\) (Eq. 6.2.1). Step 1: stabilize \(\dot x_3=u\) with \(V_3=\tfrac12x_3^2,\ \alpha_3=-x_3\). Step 2: cross-term \(V_2=V_3+\tfrac12(x_2+x_3+x_3^3/3)^2\) (Eq. 6.2.4). Optimality: \(\alpha_2=-L_gV_2\), OFP\((-1/2)\), disk margin \(D(1/2)\) propagated (Eq. 6.2.9-6.2.12, Page 252). Complexity: integrals like \(\phi(x_2,x_3)=\int_0^\infty(\tilde x_2+\tilde x_2^3+\tilde x_2u_2)ds\) generally require numerical evaluation (Page 253).

19. Adaptive extension (Page 217-220, Eq. 5.5.1-5.5.20):
Unknown \(\theta\), overparameterization \(\Theta=[\theta^\top\ \vartheta^\top]\), certainty-equivalence \(u=-y-w^\top(z,\xi,y)\hat\Theta\) (Eq. 5.5.18), update \(\dot{\hat\Theta}=w(z,\xi,y)y\) (Eq. 5.5.21). Augmented Lyapunov \(V_e=V_0+\tfrac12y^2+\tfrac12\tilde\Theta^\top\tilde\Theta\) (Eq. 5.5.20), \(\dot V_e=-z^\top Q_Fz-\xi^\top Q_A\xi-y^2\le0\) (Eq. 5.5.22). Projection to keep \(\hat\theta\in\Pi\subset\mathcal P\) (Eq. 5.5.24, Page 223-224). Breakdown Page 228-229, Example 5.35: \(\dot z=\xi+\theta_1y,\ \dot\xi=-\xi+\theta_2y,\ \dot y=u\) not stabilizable for \(\theta_1+\theta_2=0\); admissible set \(\mathcal P=\{(\theta_1,\theta_2):\theta_1+\theta_2\neq0\}\).

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage of the source, with multi-week cognitive traps embedded in the literature.

The book’s intellectual ancestry runs from Lurie’s absolute stability problem [70] and Popov’s late-1950s breakthroughs [87, 88], through Yakubovich [121], Kalman [51, 52], Zames [123], and Willems’ 1972 state-space dissipativity formalism [120], to Hill and Moylan’s excess/shortage-of-passivity framework [37, 38] and the Kalman-Yakubovich-Popov Lemma (Page 2-4, 66-67, 78-79). The specific contributions of Sepulchre, Janković, and Kokotović extend Byrnes-Isidori-Willems feedback passivity [15] and Kokotović-Sussmann [59], remove the relative-degree-one and weak-minimum-phase restrictions via backstepping [61, Krstić-Kanellakopoulos-Kokotović] and forwarding [46, 95, authors’ own], and integrate Mazenc-Praly composite constructions [75], Teel saturation designs [109], Sontag ISS [103] and CLF [4, 98, 101], and Artstein-Sontag explicit formulae [100].

Multi-week traps in this lineage:
Trap 1 (sector vs. disk): The Aizerman conjecture that gain margin implies sector margin was disproved [86, 119]; Example 3.3 (Page 75-76) shows \(H(s)=(s+1)/s^2\) has gain margin \((0,\infty)\) yet loses asymptotic stability under a bounded static nonlinearity. A designer reasoning “gain margin is enough” is trapped one week into believing static sector robustness implies dynamic disk robustness. The source corrects this on Page 77-78: sector margin does not imply disk margin, demonstrated by \((s+1)^{-2}\) which has sector margin \((0,\infty)\) but no \(D(\alpha,\infty)\) for any \(\alpha\).

Trap 2 (optimal margin universality): Kalman’s linear result that LQR gives \(D(1/2)\) is often over-generalized to nonlinear costs with \(R(x)\neq I\). Example 3.33 (Page 101) is a counterexample: a smooth positive definite \(R(x)\) can destroy passivity entirely. A designer assuming “optimal always means disk margin” will over-specify robustness for multivariable non-quadratic control penalties. Page 103 explicitly states that for general \(R(x)\) no stability margin is guaranteed.

Trap 3 (nonpeaking output vs. nonpeaking state): Definition 4.30 (Page 154) requires only the output \(y=C\xi\) to satisfy the nonpeaking bound; states may peak. Proposition 4.37 (Page 160-161) shows that in a chain of integrators, nonpeaking the output \(y=\xi_i\) forces \(\xi_{i+k}\) to peak with exponent \(\pi=k\) and \(\xi_{i-k}\) with \(\pi=k-1\). A controller that claims “nonpeaking stabilization” while allowing peaking states inside the interconnection \(\tilde\psi(z,\xi)y\) violates Theorem 4.41 (Page 163-165) and loses semiglobal stability.

Trap 4 (high-gain monotonicity): Proposition 6.3 (Page 242-243) gives semiglobal stabilization with high gain \(k\) for any compact region, but Example 6.4 (Page 244-245) shows the actual region of attraction can shrink along \(\xi_2=0\) as \(k\to\infty\) because peaking derivatives excite the nonlinear interconnection \(z_2\xi_1z_2\). The trap is assuming “larger \(k\) always enlarges the domain”; the source shows peaking creates a non-monotonic trade-off.

Trap 5 (singular perturbation complacency): Theorem 3.18 (Page 90) guarantees semiglobal stability only for \(\mu\le\mu^\star\). Example 3.16 (Page 86-87) shows that increasing nominal gain widens the disk margin for relative-degree-preserving uncertainties but destabilizes the loop under a relative-degree-two unmodeled dynamics \(\Delta(s)=100/(s+10)^2\) for \(k>16.6\). The trap is treating disk margin as sufficient for all dynamic uncertainty; it applies only to uncertainties that do not change relative degree (Page 83-84).

These traps are “multi-week” because they arise after initial success: a designer implements backstepping, observes local stability, then deploys higher gains and encounters finite-time escape or limit-cycle-induced attraction collapse weeks later. The source’s formal conditions (relative degree one, weak minimum phase, nonresonance, radial unboundedness, \(\mu^\star\) bounds) are precisely the early-warning signatures that must be audited before the failure mode manifests.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Modeling the designer’s recursive crisis-habit regression, the counter-trap, and the clock that arbitrates between them.

Crisis habit regression: When a nonlinear plant resists stabilization, the designer’s default regression is feedback linearization and cancellation, exemplified by \(u_l(x)=-x^2-x\) for \(\dot x=x^2+u\) (Page 108, Example 3.38). This regress produces an apparently ideal closed loop \(\dot x=-x\) but zero stability margin: any perturbation \((1+\epsilon)u_l\) yields \(\dot x=-(1+\epsilon)x-\epsilon x^2\) with finite-time escape for any \(\epsilon\neq0\) (Eq. 3.5.8, Page 108). The habit is to cancel “unwanted” nonlinearities, treating them as disturbances rather than potential sources of damping. The regression is recursive because each cancellation at step \(i\) requires the derivative \(\dot\alpha_{i-1}\) at step \(i+1\), cascading analytic complexity until the control law becomes unimplementable (Page 240-241, Sec. 6.1.3).

Counter-trap: The inverse-optimal counter-habit is to avoid cancellation and instead dominate destabilizing terms. For the scalar example, \(u^*(x)=-x^2-x\sqrt{x^2+1}\) (Page 100-101, Example 3.20) never wastes effort canceling beneficial \(x^2\) for \(x<0\) and dominates rather than cancels destabilizing \(x^2\) for \(x>0\), yielding disk margin \((1/2,\infty)\). Recursively, backstepping implements this via robust “control laws” \(\alpha_i\) constructed to dominate uncertainties (Page 235, Sec. 6.1.1), and forwarding propagates the OFP\((-1/2)\) storage function through nested cross-terms (Page 252, Eq. 6.2.9-6.2.11). The counter-trap is verified by Sontag’s CLF formula (Page 113-114) and the domination redesign \( \tilde u=-\tfrac12\gamma(V)(L_gV)^\top \) which recovers \(D(1/2)\) from any optimal stabilizing seed (Page 104-105, Prop. 3.35).

Clock: The arbitrating variable is time-scale separation. In singular perturbations, \(\mu\) is the clock: Theorem 3.18 (Page 90) requires \(\mu\le\mu^\star\) for the compact set \(C_x\times C_z\); smaller \(\mu\) means wider separation between slow nominal dynamics and fast unmodeled dynamics. In backstepping, the clock is the nested-manifold gain hierarchy \(k_1,\dots,k_n\) (Eq. 6.1.45, Page 245): high gains enforce fast invariant manifolds \(x_2=\alpha_1(x_1)\), but excessive gain causes peaking and attraction collapse (Example 6.4, Page 244-245). In forwarding, the clock runs in reverse: low-gain saturation \(\sigma_\epsilon\) (Page 21-22) enforces slow manifolds \(x_2=x_3=0\) and convergence \(\dot\zeta=-\sigma_\epsilon(\zeta)+O(\epsilon^2)\) (Eq. 1.5.17, Page 22). The designer must choose the clock direction: backstepping speeds up inner loops (risking high-frequency unmodeled-dynamics instability, Page 20-21), forwarding slows down outer loops (risking disturbance sensitivity \(>\epsilon\), Page 22).

The recursion closes on the passivating output: backstepping starts from the integrator farthest from \(u\) and reaches \(u\) at the last step (Page 239-240), while forwarding starts at \(u\) and moves forward (Page 250-251). Both construct \(V\) as a CLF, but the crisis habit regresses to cancellation unless the designer explicitly invokes the domination counter-trap and checks the clock condition (\(\mu^\star\), gain separation, peaking exponents \(\pi=k-1\)).
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and environmental veto proof: conditions under which candidate control designs are vetoed by layer-specific constraints, with exact singularity/breakdown regimes.

Layer 1 — Mechanical plant (TORA, mass-spring-damper, ball-and-beam, single-link manipulator):
- TORA platform mass \(M\), rotor mass \(m\), eccentricity \(e\), spring constant \(k\), dimensionless parameter \(\epsilon\) (Page 145-146). The nominal model (4.4.1) has control torque \(u\) entering nonlinearly through \(1-\epsilon^2\cos^2 x_3\). Veto: if \(\epsilon\to1\), the input coefficient degenerates (\(1-\epsilon^2\cos^2x_3\to0\)), the feedback transformation (4.4.3) becomes singular, and passivation from \(u\) to \(y=x_4\) fails.
- Mass-spring-damper (Page 31, Example 2.8): with force input and position output, transfer function \(1/(ms^2+bs+k)\) has relative degree two; not passive. Veto: output-feedback passivity \(u=-y\) cannot stabilize; relative degree \(>1\) is a hard structural veto (Prop. 2.44, Page 63).
- Ball-and-beam (Page 214-215, Eq. 5.4.21-5.4.23): viscous friction \(\beta=0.1\) creates a slow mode at \(-\beta\); damping control achieves GAS but convergence of ball position is slow because Jacobian linearization dominates near origin (Page 216). Veto: cannot assign arbitrary fast rate without modifying storage to penalize \(z\)-variables (Page 151-152, PR-controller redesign).
- Single-link manipulator joint flexibility (Page 88-89, Example 3.17): fast states \(z_1=\zeta, z_2=\zeta^\dagger/\sqrt{k}, z_3=I\) with singular perturbation \(\mu\). Veto: if \(\mu>\mu^\star\) for the compact set of link angles and currents, the equilibrium loses asymptotic stability; the relative-degree-three actuator-current dynamics cannot be certified by disk margin (Page 85-86).

Layer 2 — Electrical and actuation:
- RLC circuit (Page 29-30, Example 2.7): passive with storage energy \(E=\tfrac12Li_L^2+\tfrac12Cv_C^2\). Veto: passivity holds only for the voltage-input/current-output pair; reversing input-output preserves passivity here, but general multi-port transformations require the pre/post-multiplication condition \(u=M(x)\bar u,\ \bar y=M^\top(x)y\) (Page 33-34, Prop. 2.11). If \(M\) depends on the other subsystem’s state and loses rank, the interconnection is ill-posed (Page 32-33, Example 2.9: static loop \(d_1=-1,d_2=1\) imposes \(x_1(t)\equiv r(t)\), violating the state equation).

Layer 3 — Informational and uncertainty constraints:
- Parametric uncertainty changing relative degree (Page 84, Example 3.12): in \(\dot x_1=f(x_1,x_2)+q_3u,\ \dot x_2=u,\ y=x_1\), the value \(q_3=0\) drops relative degree from one to two. Veto: any nominal model using \(q_3=0\) is invalid; disk/sector margins assume fixed relative degree (Page 83-84).
- Fast unmodeled dynamics changing relative degree (Page 85-87, Example 3.16): \(\Delta(s)=100/(s+10)^2\) destabilizes \(H(s)=1/(s-1)\) with gain \(k>16.6\). Veto: disk margin \(D(\alpha)\) protects only against uncertainties that preserve relative degree; relative-degree-changing dynamics require singular-period analysis with explicit \(\mu^\star\) (Page 90).
- Actuator saturation (Page 149): saturated P-controller \(u=-k_1 x_3/\sqrt{1+x_3^2} -(\delta-k_1)\arctan x_4\) with bound \(\delta\). Veto: if required control magnitude exceeds \(\delta\), the GAS proof via \(\dot V_\delta=-(\delta-k_1)x_4\arctan x_4\le0\) still holds locally but performance degrades; saturation level \(\epsilon\) too small causes large overshoot \(x_1\) before the manifold is reached (Page 22).

Adjudication rule: A design survives cross-layer veto iff (i) relative degree of the physical input-output pair is one (or is rendered one by non-singular feedback), (ii) zero dynamics are weakly minimum phase or are recursively stabilized by forwarding, (iii) the interconnection does not contain peaking states multiplied by superlinear functions of \(z\) (Theorem 4.43, Page 167-168), (iv) the singular perturbation parameter satisfies \(\mu\le\mu^\star\) for the prescribed compact region (Page 90), and (v) control authority \(\delta\) or saturation \(\epsilon\) exceeds the worst-case peaking transient \(\gamma(a)\) (Eq. 4.5.1, Page 153). Violation of any condition constitutes a veto; the failure mode is finite-time escape, attraction shrinkage, or loss of exponential stability, not merely performance degradation.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR (reasoning-layer verification and rejection) counterfactual veto scenarios. Each scenario specifies a precondition, the vetoed design, and a falsifiable prediction drawn from the source text.

Scenario A — Strictly nonminimum-phase cascade veto.
Precondition: The linear subsystem \((A,B,C)\) in the cascade \(\dot z=f(z)+\tilde\psi(z,\xi)y,\ \dot\xi=A\xi+Bu,\ y=C\xi\) has a zero \(\nu\in\mathbb R_{>0}\) (open right-half plane), and the interconnection is \(\psi=\tilde\psi(z)z^{q+1}y^{2s}\) with positive integers \(q,s\) and positive \(\alpha,\beta\) (Page 167-168, Theorem 4.43 proof, Eq. 4.5.40). Vetoed design: any partial- or full-state feedback controller claiming semiglobal asymptotic stability of \((z,\xi)=(0,0)\). Verifiable prediction: There exists an initial condition \((z(0),\xi(0))\) such that \(z(t)\) escapes to infinity in finite time \(t_f\), regardless of the input \(u(t)\). In the scalar counterexample \(\dot z=(-\alpha z+\beta z^{q+1}y^2s)\), the unstable zero forces the output to act as stabilizing control for the zero dynamics; its irreducible “energy” perturbs the \(z\)-subsystem and produces finite-time escape (Page 168). Rejection criterion: simulate with any stabilizing \(u\); if \(\|z(t)\|\to\infty\) before \(t=1\) for the constructed initial condition, the veto is confirmed.

Scenario B — Non-diagonal control penalty veto.
Precondition: The optimal cost is \(J=\int_0^\infty(l(x)+u^\top R(x)u)\,dt\) with \(R(x)\) positive definite but non-diagonal, as in Example 3.33 (Page 101): \(R(x)=[1\ -1; -1\ 2\sigma(\cdot)]^{-1}>0\) with saturation \(\sigma\), \(a>2+\sqrt2\). Vetoed design: the claim that the optimal stabilizing control \(u(x)=-\tfrac12R^{-1}(x)(x_1+x_2)\) achieves a disk margin \(D(1/2)\) or even passivity of the output system \(y=\tfrac12R^{-1}(x)(x_1+x_2)\). Verifiable prediction: Along the trajectory \(x_1(t)=\frac{1}{1+a^2}\sin t+\frac{a}{1+a^2}\cos t,\ x_2(t)=\sin t,\ u(t)=\cos t\), the accumulated supply \(\int_0^{2n\pi}u(t)y(t)dt\) diverges to \(-\infty\) as \(n\to\infty\) (Page 101-102, Eq. 3.4.8). Hence the system is not passive and no disk margin exists. Rejection criterion: compute the integral over periods; if it trends to \(-\infty\), the OFP\((-1/2)\) certificate is void and the disk-margin claim is rejected.

Scenario C — Singular perturbation clock veto.
Precondition: The plant is in standard singular perturbation form \(\dot x=f(x,z),\ \mu\dot z=q(x,z)\) (Page 90, Eq. 3.2.13-3.2.14), with reduced model GAS+LES and fast subsystem GAS+LES uniformly in \(x\). Let \(C_x\times C_z\) be a prescribed compact region of attraction. Vetoed design: the claim that the nominal slow-model controller guarantees asymptotic stability of \((0,0)\) with region containing \(C_x\times C_z\) for any \(\mu>0\). Verifiable prediction: Theorem 3.18 (Page 90) asserts existence of \(\mu^\star>0\) such that for \(0<\mu\le\mu^\star\) the region contains \(C_x\times C_z\). Counterfactually, if \(\mu>\mu^\star\), the equilibrium may lose asymptotic stability or the region of attraction excludes part of \(C_x\times C_z\). In the single-link manipulator (Page 88-89), the armature inductance \(L/R\) and flexibility \(1/\sqrt{k}\) are both scaled by \(\mu\); if the electrical time constant is not sufficiently smaller than the mechanical mode, the fast current dynamics destabilize the rigid-joint nominal design. Rejection criterion: reduce \(\mu\) until stability is recovered; if stability is lost for all \(\mu\) down to machine precision, the reduced-model assumption is structurally invalid (e.g., fast subsystem not uniformly GAS).
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
