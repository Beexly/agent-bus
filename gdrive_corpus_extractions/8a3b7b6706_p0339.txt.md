<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage of Knight's distinction between risk and uncertainty originates in the late 19th-century critique of neoclassical equilibrium. Alfred Marshall's partial equilibrium relied on ceteris paribus stability, while Léon Walras attempted to formalize general equilibrium through tâtonnement. Both implicitly assumed that agents possessed calculable probabilities. Frank Knight's *Risk, Uncertainty and Profit* (1921) dismantled this by separating 'risk' (insurable, quantifiable via law of large numbers) from 'uncertainty' (uniqueness requiring judgment). This genealogical break birthed modern corporate finance: the firm exists because entrepreneurs assume uninsurable uncertainty, earning profit as a residual claim. The 'machinery of the market' described on page 328 evolves directly from this: if profit stems from unique judgment, then capital markets must price heterogeneous expectations rather than homogeneous goods. Later developments—Friedman-Savage portfolio theory, Fama's EMH, Akerlof's lemons—all inherit Knight's core insight: prices adjust where beliefs collide, but they also expose the fragility when 'judgment' becomes correlated across agents. Multi-week traps emerge when narratives of 'permanent innovation' (e.g., dot-com, crypto winters) cause the market to re-evaluate entire classes of securities as pure uncertainty rather than risk, triggering cascading reassessments of supply and demand.
