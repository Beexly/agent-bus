\[
\mathrm{EPA}_{i,t}=\alpha+\beta X_{i,t-1}+\gamma' C_{i,t}+\varepsilon_{i,t},

E(p)=p\,e_P(p)+(1-p)e_R.

\[
\frac{dE}{dp}=e_P(p)+p\,e_P'(p)-e_R=0,

The source does not provide the diagnostics needed to rule out these regimes.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper sits in a lineage that moves from raw box-score efficiency to success rate, then to expected points, then to win-probability and dynamic play-calling models. The source’s data and background reading point to this genealogy: “https://github.com/ryurko/nflscrapR-data”, “https://www.footballoutsiders.com/”, “https://www.sharpfootballstats.com/rushing-success-rate-over-average--sroa-.html”, FiveThirtyEight’s passing-league arguments, Pro Football Reference, and ESPN’s expected-points explainer [PAGE 11]. Retrocausally, later EPA and success-rate tools reinterpret earlier run-heavy decisions as potentially conservative: if passing has higher measured value, then historical “establish the run” logic looks like a habit inherited from a lower-information era. The source’s own summary chain is “Tested for diminishing returns” and “EPA of a play was not impacted by proportion of previous play-calls” [PAGE 9]. But the genealogy also warns against a simple inversion. The same analytics lineage that favors passing also reveals opponent adjustment, game script, and selection effects. Multi-week traps are therefore serious: a team that passes more may do so because it has a better quarterback, a worse defense, or a trailing game script; a defense that sees more passing may invest in pass defense; a public-data sample may mix regular season, playoffs, weather, and injuries. The source’s conclusion “More evenly distributed run/pass play-calling does not lead to increased efficiency” [PAGE 10] is a short-horizon empirical result, not a multi-week strategic law. A multi-week counter-trap is that if passing efficiency is high league-wide, defensive adaptation can compress it over subsequent seasons, producing a lagged diminishing return that single-season or short-window tests miss. The source’s cautious final claim, “We cannot calculate an optimal run/pass ratio until there is data showing that there is such a thing as too much passing” [PAGE 10], is exactly the correct retrocausal brake: the absence of an observed plateau in the available sample is not evidence that no plateau can exist after strategic adjustment.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive layer is coach-versus-coach belief modeling. An offensive coordinator chooses \(p\) based on a model of the defensive coordinator’s model of the offense. If the offense passes more, the defense may expect pass and allocate coverage; that can raise rush EPA as a counter-trap. The source tests only prior play proportions and finds “no effect” on subsequent EPA in four cross-cases [PAGE 7] [PAGE 8], which suggests that short-horizon surprise or balance effects are not detectable in that specification. But recursive theory of mind operates on longer timescales: film study, tendency breakers, third-down tendencies, and red-zone tendencies. A null within-game prior-mix effect does not rule out a multi-week tendency effect. Crisis habit regression is the second layer: under pressure, coaches may regress to conservative rushing because it avoids interceptions, protects a lead, and burns clock, even when the source says “Passing is far more valuable than rushing” [PAGE 10]. Counter-trap logic says that if every team follows the pass-heavy analytics, defensive investment and coverage shells may eventually punish it; the source has not yet observed that plateau, but it also says “there’s no indication yet” [PAGE 10], not “never.” Clock logic is the third layer: passing stops the clock on incompletions and increases sack risk, while rushing can consume time when leading. Therefore the recursive optimum is state-dependent: score differential, time remaining, timeouts, down and distance, and opponent expectations all belong in \(C_{i,t}\). The source’s own question, “Does the value of passing diminish as you throw more often?” [PAGE 6], is a recursive strategic question, not merely a curve-fitting question. The correct theory-of-mind conclusion is that the observed data support a pass-value advantage and fail to locate a short-horizon balance penalty, but they do not resolve the longer recursive game against adaptive defenses.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A universal “pass more” prescription is vetoed by physical, biomechanical, and weather layers unless those layers are explicitly conditioned. The source includes “Adjusted Sack Rate on passing plays” [PAGE 5] as a partial proxy for protection and pressure, but it does not report weather, quarterback injury, receiver separation, or offensive-line health interactions. Formally, let \(W_t\) be weather state and \(H_t\) be health and protection state. The relevant comparison is
\[
\Delta(p,W,H)=e_P(p,W,H)-e_R(p,W,H),
\]
with units points per play. The source’s average claim “Passing is far more valuable than rushing” [PAGE 10] is conditional on the observed distribution of \(W\) and \(H\). In high wind, heavy rain, extreme cold, or poor grip conditions, ball-flight variance and catch probability can fall; in quarterback or offensive-line injury states, sack rate and pressure rate can rise. Those regimes can make \(\Delta(p,W,H)\lt 0\), which vetoes a context-free pass-heavy conclusion. The physical veto is not a refutation of the source’s average result; it is a scope condition. The source’s data-source list [PAGE 11] does not show weather or injury controls, so the slides cannot adjudicate the extreme-regime veto. The correct adjudication is: retain the source’s average finding, but mark it as non-identifying for extreme weather, severe injury, and protection-collapse regimes. In those regimes the estimate is unstable or singular because the observed support is thin, and extrapolating the average pass-value advantage beyond that support is invalid.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Diminishing-returns veto. Pre-register a within-team, within-season model \(\mathrm{EPA}^{\mathrm{pass}}_{i,t}=\alpha+\beta_p p_{i,t}+\gamma'C_{i,t}+\varepsilon_{i,t}\), with \(p\) the team’s pass proportion and \(C\) controlling score, clock, down, distance, and opponent. If \(\beta_p\) is negative with a 95% interval excluding zero at high observed pass rates, then the source’s statement “there’s no indication yet of a frequency that leads to diminishing returns” [PAGE 10] is vetoed. Prediction: the estimated interior optimum \(p^*\) from \(e_P(p)+p e_P'(p)-e_R=0\) will be below the observed maximum pass rate, and the pass-rate interaction in a win model will be negative. Verification is direct from play-by-play data with a pre-specified threshold and uncertainty interval.

2. Prior-mix invariance veto. Use exogenous or quasi-exogenous variation in prior play mix, such as scripted opening drives, turnovers, or sudden score changes, to test the four null claims: “The percentage of prior rushing plays called has no effect on the EPA of rushing plays”; “The percentage of prior passing plays called has no effect on the EPA of passing plays” [PAGE 7]; and the two cross-case claims [PAGE 8]. If any \(\beta\) in the prior-mix EPA model is nonzero with a confidence interval excluding zero, the strong “no effect” reading is vetoed. Prediction: if surprise matters, prior pass proportion should negatively predict pass EPA when defenses expect pass, and prior run proportion should positively predict rush EPA when defenses expect run. Verification requires the same four models with reported coefficients and clustered standard errors.

3. Weather-biomechanical interaction veto. Pre-register a model with \(\mathrm{EPA}^{\mathrm{pass}}_{i,t}-\mathrm{EPA}^{\mathrm{rush}}_{i,t}=\delta_0+\delta_1 W_{i,t}+\delta_2 H_{i,t}+\delta_3 p_{i,t}+\delta_4(p_{i,t}\times W_{i,t})+\gamma'C_{i,t}+\varepsilon_{i,t}\). If \(\delta_4\) is negative and large enough that \(\Delta(p,W,H)\lt 0\) in high-wind, heavy-rain, extreme-cold, or protection-collapse games, then the universal reading of “Passing is far more valuable than rushing” [PAGE 10] is vetoed for those regimes. Prediction: the optimal pass proportion will fall in those games, and the pass-value advantage will shrink or reverse. Verification requires joining play-by-play data to weather and injury or pressure data; the source’s listed data sources [PAGE 11] do not by themselves provide that join, so this is a genuine counterfactual audit rather than a claim already established by the paper.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
