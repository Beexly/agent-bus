Riemann’s original formulation (1854) relied on partitioning the *domain* of the function and taking suprema/infima over intervals. This worked beautifully for continuous functions on compact intervals but failed for highly discontinuous functions (like the Dirichlet function). Lebesgue’s revolutionary insight (1901-1902) was to partition the *range* of the function and measure the size of the pre-images. Halmos’s `s(C)` operator is a hybrid: it uses a partition of the domain, but the coefficients are the infima of `f` on those sets, effectively mimicking the range-partitioning logic through the lens of simple functions. This approach democratizes the construction of the integral, making it accessible without explicit reference to range partitions initially.

Exercise (7b) is a direct descendant of the Hardy-Littlewood maximal inequality and the theory of symmetric decreasing rearrangements developed in the early 20th century. The identity `∫f dμ = ∫f^* dy` is the cornerstone of rearrangement inequalities, which state that among all functions with a given distribution, the radially symmetric decreasing rearrangement maximizes certain norms or minimizes energy functionals. This principle permeates modern analysis, appearing in the proof of the Sobolev inequalities, the study of nonlinear PDEs (e.g., Navier-Stokes regularity via concentration-compactness), and even in quantum chemistry (Thomas-Fermi theory). The historical trap lies in assuming this invariance is obvious; it is actually a profound statement about the irrelevance of spatial arrangement for `L^p` norms when only magnitude matters.
