That earlier work is the classic latent-learning demonstration: three groups of rats ran a maze for many days, one rewarded every day, one never rewarded, and one rewarded only after an initial unrewarded period. The delayed-reward group rapidly caught up once reward was introduced, which Tolman interpreted as evidence that a “cognitive map” had been formed during the unrewarded phase and was merely latent until incentive made performance evident. The present page 54 manipulates the reverse contingency: remove a high-pulling-power goal object after learning and observe degeneration.

The retro-causal lineage continues through mid-century learning theory. Hull’s drive-reduction hierarchy predicted performance proportional to \(D \times H\), where drive \(D\) and habit strength \(H\) multiply. Tolman’s result—hungry rats whose performance collapses when reward is withdrawn—undermines a pure drive-habit product because \(D\) remains high while performance falls. The modern formal descendant is the temporal-difference prediction error \(\delta_t = r_{t+1} + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)\). Omission of an expected reward generates \(\delta_t < 0\), driving \(V(s)\) downward across successive trials; this is the computational mechanism behind the observed degeneration. Rescorla-Wagner and later outcome-devaluation literature (Balleine & Dickinson) reframe the same phenomenon: the action “run to exit” was instrumental because it was contingent on food; devaluing the outcome degrades the action even when the habitual response topography remains physically available.
