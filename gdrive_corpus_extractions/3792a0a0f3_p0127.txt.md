\subsection{The Joint Product Critique and Its Demolition}
\subsubsection{Mechanical vs. Economic Necessity}

The core flaw identified by Knight concerns the equivocation between two distinct notions of necessity: \textit{mechanical necessity} and \textit{economic necessity}. Mechanical necessity holds that, physically and logically, every productive factor must be present for an output to exist; land enables spatial utility, capital provides tools, and labor applies effort. However, this observation pertains solely to the ontology of goods, not to the distribution of income.

Economic necessity, by contrast, is a relational concept tied to market processes. A factor is economically necessary to the extent that its exclusion from a production function results in an irreversible loss of total output. If the withdrawn factor is perfectly substitutable elsewhere without diminishing aggregate production, then it is economically surplus. Land, on the margin, possesses this characteristic due to its theoretical infinite replicability; each plot can theoretically be duplicated, ensuring that no individual parcel is indispensable for maintaining the status quo. Thus, although no house exists without lumber and nails, no farmer exists without soil, and no factory operates without space, the specific plots or units in use do not individually satisfy economic necessity.

Mathematically, this distinction manifests in the behavior of marginal rates of technical substitution (\(\text{MRTS}\)). When a factor exhibits perfect substitutability, its \(\text{MRTS}\) approaches infinity; the factor can be infinitely scaled without altering the composite factor ratio. In such cases, the partial derivative of production with respect to that factor (\(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial K}\)) remains finite while the corresponding factor price (\(r\)) may approach zero, violating the equality \(P_{m} \cdot MP_{K} = r\) upon which marginal product theory relies.

\subsubsection{Path Dependence and Reductive Fallacy}

The second layer of Knight's refutation targets the implicit mathematical structure of marginalist summation. Marginal productivity theory claims that:
\[
\begin{equation*}
Q = f(L, K) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial L} \cdot \Delta L + \frac{\partial f}{\partial K} \cdot \Delta K = \Delta Q
\end{equation*}
\]
However, the joint product argument demonstrates that this additive decomposition is logically untenable. The marginal product of labor (\(MP_L\)) is defined as:
\[
\begin{equation*}
MP_{L} = \lim_{\Delta L \to 0} \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta L} \bigg|_{K=\text{constant}}
\end{equation*}
\]

This partial derivative presumes that capital stock \(K\) is held constant during the marginal adjustment. Yet, in practical production, changing the labor force (\(\Delta L\)) necessarily alters the intensity of capital utilized per worker, thereby shifting the effective capital-to-labor ratio (\(K/L\)). Consequently, the marginal product of labor at one scale (\(L_1\)) differs from that at another scale (\(L_2\)), even within the same industry, introducing path dependence that contradicts the additive assumption.

Equivalently, consider the total derivative of output with respect to capital:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dQ}{dK} &= \frac{\partial f}{\partial L} \cdot \frac{dL}{dK} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial K} \cdot \frac{dK}{dK} \\
&= \frac{\partial f}{\partial L} \cdot \text{RTSLK} + MP_{K}
\end{aligned}
\]
If factors are genuinely joint, then changes in labor input (\(L\)) induce a proportional shift in capital complementarity, maintaining \(MP_L\) while reducing \(\text{RTSLK}\); the total effect remains undecomposable into autonomous factor contributions.

Formally, let the production function be expressed in extensive form as \(F(K, L, H)\), where \(H\) represents human capital or quality. The joint product thesis asserts that:
\[
\begin{equation}
\forall (K_0, L_0, H_0), \; \frac{\partial F}{\partial K} \neq g(K, L, H) \land \frac{\partial F}{\partial L} \neq h(K, L, H)
\end{equation}
\]
for some functions \(g\) and \(h\). That is, partial derivatives do not exist independently of the other arguments. Consequently, the fundamental premise underlying the distribution of national income—that each factor earns the present value of its marginal product—cannot be satisfied, as the marginal products themselves lack stable, autonomous definitions.

\subsubsection{The Measurement Paradox: Circular Reference and Apriori Determination}

Even assuming away the analytical issues of non-separability, the joint product argument engenders a severe measurement paradox. To observe the price of an input factor (say, wage rate \(w\)), one must already know the market-clearing quantity demanded of that factor, which is derived from firms' profit-maximizing decisions under given output prices. Conversely, the value of the marginal product depends on the same output price, which in turn reflects the weighted average of factor costs. Hence, the system:
\[
\begin{cases}
MP_L \cdot P = w \\
MRP_L = f'(L, K) \cdot P \\
\text{market clearing: } w = f(L^*, K^*)
\end{cases}
\]
constitutes a closed loop of simultaneous determination, rendering the marginal revenue product (\(MRP_L\)) itself unobservably dependent on the wage rate.

To illustrate this formally, consider the expenditure minimization problem faced by a representative firm:
\[
\begin{equation}
\min_{L, K} \; wL + rK \quad \text{s.t.} \quad F(K, L) \geq Q
\end{equation}
\]
Using Lagrange multipliers, the first-order conditions yield:
\[
\begin{cases}
\lambda = \frac{w}{MP_L} \\
\lambda = \frac{r}{MP_K}
\end{cases}
\]
If \(MP_L\) and \(MP_K\) are endogenously determined by Equation (1) as functions of \(L\) and \(K\), then \(\lambda\) becomes a function of itself, creating a vicious circle. No data-generating process exists within standard microfoundations that could break this feedback loop without invoking external shocks or structural breaks. This logical tightness mirrors the Sraffian critique of value theory, where prices appear to determine values, which simultaneously determine prices—a tautology unless resolved through exogenous technological progress or institutional rule-breaking.

\subsubsection{Normative Collapse: The Problem of Apportionment and Collective Agency}

Finally, the most profound consequence of accepting the joint product doctrine is normative dissolution. If all factors are collectively indispensable, then no individual input can lay exclusive claim to the generated surplus. Wages, profits, and rent are no longer rewards for factor services but merely symptomatic payments reflecting the group's preference ordering over historical contingencies. The entrepreneurial role—the bearing of risk, the recognition of temporal disequilibria, and the orchestration of dispersed knowledge—becomes subsumed under a collective bargaining mechanism.

Formally, let the social accounting identity define national income as:
\[
\begin{equation}
Y = C + I + G + NX
\end{equation}
\]
where \(Y\) is GDP, \(C\) consumption, \(I\) investment, \(G\) government spending, and \(NX\) net exports. Within \(Y\), the components \(C\) and \(I\) incorporate factor payments:
\[
\begin{aligned}
C &= \int_0^{T_c} w(t) dt + \int_0^{T_i} r(t) dt + \int_0^{T_p} \pi(t) dt \\
I &= \int_0^{T_i} r(t) dt + \int_0^{T_p} \pi(t) dt
\end{aligned}
\]
Here, integration represents the accumulation of flows over time intervals \(T_c\) and \(T_i\), and superscripts denote labor, capital, and profit respectively. Under joint productism, each integral cannot be further disaggregated into independent addends because each flow is entangled with others. For example, wages cannot be isolated from the demand for consumer goods, which in turn depends on the availability of durable capital equipment, which again hinges on the anticipation of future profitability by profit-earners.

Thus, the question of factor shares becomes a pure apportionment problem devoid of objective resolution. Philosophically, this resembles the Tragedy of the Commons: no one owns the fish in the sea, yet everyone contributes to its extinction. Individual incentives contradict collective outcomes, leading to inefficient resource allocation. The market mechanism, stripped of its coordinating efficiency, reduces to mere coordination failure.

In conclusion, Knight’s critique demonstrates that the joint product argument not only challenges marginal productivity theory mathematically but also dismantles the logical foundations required for both positive science and normative policy prescriptions. Only by reintroducing the entrepreneur as a separating agent capable of rendering inputs partially independent through innovative contracts and risk-bearing can the fragmentation of production be managed. Otherwise, the economy collapses into perpetual renegotiation cycles, undermining any possibility of sustainable growth.

Counterfactual Scenario C: Emergence of Non-Democratic Central Planning Without Market Exchange
Envision a society where a benevolent dictator commands all production decisions without relying on market prices or factor markets. Resources are allocated according to a centrally prescribed social welfare function.
</VETO_DESCRIPTION>
<PREDICTION>
Under this autarchy, the concept of factor prices would be obsolete, but the intrinsic jointness of production would persist. The planner would face the same fundamental agglomeration problem, resulting in the same measurement indeterminacy. However, the critique's edge—that markets provide a solution via negotiation—would be defanged because markets are outlawed.
</PREDICTION>
<FAIL_STATE>
Despite the absence of markets, the joint product critique would still apply. The planner would encounter identical aggregation issues when attempting to assign inputs to outputs using sectoral accounting identities. The measurement paradox of producing income from joint causes would remain, as the social product is a seamless whole. Therefore, the planner's mandate to apportion output would fail analytically, mirroring Knight's conclusion that even command economies struggle with distributional logic.
</FAIL_STATE>
