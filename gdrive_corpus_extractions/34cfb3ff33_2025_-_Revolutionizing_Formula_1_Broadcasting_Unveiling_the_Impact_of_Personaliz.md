<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s causal chain is implicit rather than formally identified. It moves from geometric exposure \(V\) to attentional capture \(T^{(1)}\), dwell \(S\), revisits \(R\), and finally brand recall and lasting impression. The first link is defined operationally: “Visibility Percentage = (Signage Size / Image Size) * 100” [PAGE 7]. The result is \(\bar V=7.63\%\) across 22 frames, compared with race standings at \(10.81\%\) [PAGE 8]. The second link is eye-tracking: personalized virtual advertising billboards, PVABs, produced “the shortest Average Time to First Fixation at 0.79 seconds” in Survey B [PAGE 9], while Table III reports standard virtual advertising billboards at \(1.04\) s and PVABs at \(0.79\) s [PAGE 10]. The third link is recall: Qatar Airways had “the highest average unaided recall rate at 17.88% with stronger recall in Survey A (21.88%) than in Survey B (13.89%)” [PAGE 11], while Lenovo rose “from 6.25% in Survey A to 8.33% in Survey B” and Pirelli “from 9.38% to 11.11%” [PAGE 11]. The fourth link is lasting impression: “DHL and Qatar Airways were the top performers, each scoring 25%” [PAGE 12], but “Liqui Moly’s failure to generating lasting impressions (0%) despite high visibility” appears on the same page [PAGE 12]. This immediately falsifies any simple monotonic claim that visibility alone causes recall.

The main gray-zone is inferential overreach from a preliminary design. The source states: “The participants were divided into two groups of 10” [PAGE 7], and the experiment used “20 frames from the 2023 Formula 1 season, each featuring a virtual advertisement displayed for 3 seconds, amounting to a total exposure time of 60 seconds” [PAGE 8]. Between-group comparison with \(n=10\) per cell, static frames, and no repeated-measures randomization cannot support strong causal language. The paper repeatedly uses “significantly” without reporting a test statistic, confidence interval, or effect size; for example, “personalized ads significantly reduced Time to First Fixation to 0.79 seconds compared to 1.35 seconds for non-personalized ads” [PAGE 14]. That comparison is internally suspicious because Table III reports standard virtual advertising billboard TTF at \(1.04\) s, PVAB at \(0.79\) s, and \(1.35\) s for the Left Hand Side Standing Graph in Survey B [PAGE 10]. Thus the “1.35 s non-personalized” comparator is either a transcription error or an unreported subset; either way, it is a gray-zone that must be audited before treating the \(0.79\) s result as a robust personalization effect.

A second gray-zone is recall denominator provenance. The study has \(n=20\) participants, but reported rates such as \(21.88\%\), \(13.89\%\), \(6.25\%\), \(8.33\%\), \(9.38\%\), and \(11.11\%\) [PAGE 11] are not simple fractions of 20. They imply either multiple observations per participant, frame-level aggregation, or an unstated denominator. The paper does not formalize the recall operator. A third gray-zone is construct validity: “the personalized messages used in this study did not include individual participants’ names” [PAGE 17]. Therefore the paper tests generic personalization, not individual-level personalization; the title’s “personalized virtual advertising” is broader than the manipulation. A fourth gray-zone is external validity: “static frame analysis” [PAGE 16] cannot fully capture camera cuts, motion blur, weather, and live race dynamics on “5 to 7-kilometer tracks” [PAGE 4]. A fifth is the control set: “three logos from actual Formula E sponsors—JULIUS BÄR, ABB, and PANASONIC—were included among 13 logos” [PAGE 8], which is a reasonable false-recall control but not an F1-brand-familiarity control. These issues do not invalidate the descriptive eye-tracking report; they veto strong causal and generalized claims unless matched-visibility, within-subject, live-broadcast, and denominator-corrected replications are run.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(A_{\text{signage},i}\) be the detected signage area in pixels\(^2\) and \(A_{\text{frame},i}\) the frame area in pixels\(^2\). The source operator is
\[
V_i = 100\cdot \frac{A_{\text{signage},i}}{A_{\text{frame},i}},
\]
with units of percent. The source states: “Visibility Percentage = (Signage Size / Image Size) * 100” [PAGE 7]. The sample mean is
\[
\bar V = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} V_i,\qquad N=22,
\]
and the paper reports \(\bar V=7.63\%\) and race standings at \(10.81\%\) [PAGE 8]. Assumptions: frames are standardized to \(1440\times900\) pixels [PAGE 7], the signage polygon is unoccluded, and the overlay is fully inside the frame. Breakdown regimes: cropping or camera cuts change \(A_{\text{frame}}\); partial occlusion changes \(A_{\text{signage}}\); a visually large but low-contrast board can have high \(V_i\) and low attention, as implied by Liqui Moly’s \(23.64\%\) visibility and \(0\%\) lasting impression [PAGE 12]. Therefore \(V_i\) is a geometric exposure variable, not a perceptual salience variable.

For fixation timing, define \(g_j(t)\in\mathbb{R}^2\) as participant \(j\)’s gaze point at time \(t\) in seconds and \(AOI_a\) as the area of interest. The first-fixation time is
\[
T^{(1)}_{j,a}=\inf\{t\ge 0: g_j(t)\in AOI_a\},
\]
with unit seconds. The source reports PVAB average TTF at \(0.79\) s [PAGE 9] and Table III values for standard virtual advertising billboards [PAGE 10]. Assumption: a trial lasts 3 s because “each featuring a virtual advertisement displayed for 3 seconds” [PAGE 8]. Breakdown: if no fixation occurs before 3 s, \(T^{(1)}_{j,a}\) is right-censored, and a naive arithmetic mean of observed TTF is downward-biased. A survival or hierarchical model is required; the source reports means but not censoring rules.

Dwell time is
\[
S_{j,a}=\sum_{f=1}^{F_{j,a}} d_{j,a,f},
\]
in seconds, where \(d_{j,a,f}\) is the duration of fixation \(f\). The source states: “The average Time Spent on PVABs was 0.69 seconds, comparable to the Time Spent on racing cars, which was 0.69 seconds in Survey A and 0.68 seconds in Survey B” [PAGE 9]. Revisits are a count,
\[
R_{j,a}=\left|\{k: \text{return to } AOI_a \text{ after leaving}\}\right|,
\]
dimensionless; Survey B reports PVAB average revisits \(0.40\) [PAGE 10]. Breakdown: fixations and revisits are clustered within participants and frames, so naive standard errors are invalid; a mixed model with participant and frame random effects is required.

Brand recall is an indicator proportion. For brand \(b\),
\[
U_b = 100\cdot \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{\text{participant }j\text{ names }b\text{ unaided}\},
\]
\[
A_b = 100\cdot \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{\text{participant }j\text{ recognizes }b\text{ when aided}\},
\]
\[
L_b = 100\cdot \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{\text{participant }j\text{ selects }b\text{ as most lasting impression}\}.
\]
These are probability-scale percentages, hence dimensionless. The source reports Qatar Airways \(U_b=17.88\%\), Survey A \(21.88\%\), Survey B \(13.89\%\); Lenovo \(6.25\%\to8.33\%\); Pirelli \(9.38\%\to11.11\%\) [PAGE 11], and DHL/Qatar Airways \(L_b=25\%\) [PAGE 12]. Assumption: denominators are the intended participant or observation set. Breakdown: the paper does not specify whether \(n=10\) per survey, \(n=20\) pooled, or multiple frame-level responses; values such as \(21.88\%=7/32\) and \(6.25\%=2/32\) are inconsistent with a simple \(n=20\) denominator, so the recall rates are not fully identified from the text alone.

The personalization contrast can be written as
\[
\Delta_{\text{TTFF}} = \overline{T}^{(1)}_{\text{non-personalized}}-\overline{T}^{(1)}_{\text{personalized}},
\]
in seconds. Table III implies \(1.04-0.79=0.25\) s [PAGE 10], while the conclusion states “0.79 seconds compared to 1.35 seconds” [PAGE 14], implying \(0.56\) s. This discrepancy is a formal breakdown in the operator: the comparator is not consistently defined. A visibility-controlled recall model would require at least
\[
\operatorname{logit}\Pr(U_b=1)=\alpha+\beta V_b+\gamma P_b+\delta(V_b\times P_b)+\eta\,\text{BrandFamiliarity}_b+\epsilon_b,
\]
where \(P_b\) is a personalization indicator and \(\epsilon_b\) is a brand-level error. The source does not estimate this model; it reports correlation heatmaps [PAGE 14] without numeric coefficients in the extracted text. Therefore any claim that \(V\) alone drives \(U_b\) is vetoed by the Liqui Moly counterexample and by the Qatar Airways low-visibility personalized placement: “positioning it in a low-visibility size” [PAGE 14]. Information-theoretic units, such as bits or entropy, are not reported anywhere in the source; inventing them would be an external operator, so they are excluded.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage begins with virtual advertising in the mid-1990s: “Virtual Advertising, first introduced in the mid-1990s in the USA” [PAGE 1]. The 2006 FIFA World Cup is presented as the scaling event: “The 2006 FIFA World Cup in Germany marked a pivotal moment in the evolution of virtual advertising, enabling advertisers to deliver region-specific advertisements tailored to diverse global audiences” [PAGE 1]. The paper then connects this to F1’s own streaming platform: “F1 TV Pro, launched in 2018” [PAGE 1], and later to “F1 TV, its official OTT platform” [PAGE 6]. This genealogy matters because virtual advertising is not a new salience mechanism; it is a region-specific insertion technology whose commercial logic is repeated exposure, localization, and inventory expansion. The paper’s eye-tracking test is therefore a late-stage test of an older media-economics proposition, not a wholly novel attention theory.

The scientific lineage runs through brand recall and recognition theory. The source distinguishes recall from recognition: “Brand recall is defined as the ability of consumers to accurately retrieve the brand from memory when prompted with a relevant cue” [PAGE 3], while recognition is “the consumers’ ability to confirm prior exposure to the brand when presented with it as a cue” [PAGE 3]. It also imports the sleeper effect: “it is critical to account for the sleeper effect, which proposes that an advertisement may not immediately impact consumer behavior after the exposure, but forces its influence over time” [PAGE 3]. That creates the first multi-week trap: a 60-second static-frame experiment [PAGE 8] measures immediate recall, not multi-week retention or delayed attitude change. The second multi-week trap is repetition and fatigue: embedded ads can be repeated, but repeated exposure may decay or irritate; the source notes that embedded advertising “allows the advertisements to be repeated within the content” and that repetition enhances retention [PAGE 3], while also warning that multiple sponsors reduce individual message effectiveness [PAGE 3]. The third trap is novelty decay: a personalized overlay may capture attention in a first exposure but lose advantage after repeated weeks of exposure.

Eye-tracking enters as a corrective to self-report. The source notes that “traditional self-reporting methods often fail to accurately capture viewer attention and cognitive processing of online sports ads” [PAGE 6], and cites Porter (2022): “while virtual ads capture more visual attention than traditional in-stadium ads during NBA broadcasts, they tend to be less memorable” [PAGE 6]. This is the crucial retrodiction: gaze does not equal memory. The paper’s own data reproduce that tension, since high visibility and attention can coexist with zero lasting impression for Liqui Moly [PAGE 12]. The historical lineage also includes game-dynamics effects: “attention to sponsor brands is higher in the first half of the game than in the second half” [PAGE 4], and attention rises when a favored team is likely to lose [PAGE 4]. In F1 terms, safety cars, red flags, overtakes, and championship stakes are uncontrolled state variables. A multi-week study that ignores race drama, camera cut rate, and repeated-exposure novelty will trap itself into interpreting transient salience as durable brand equity.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when stakeholders face ambiguous results and revert to familiar shortcuts: “visibility equals value,” “gaze equals memory,” and “personalization equals engagement.” The paper partially invites this by emphasizing that PVABs achieved “the shortest Average Time to First Fixation at 0.79 seconds” [PAGE 9] and that virtual ads occupied \(7.63\%\) of the frame [PAGE 8]. But the same data contain a counterexample: Qatar Airways’ personalized frame was “positioning it in a low-visibility size” and its unaided recall “declined from 21.88% to 13.89%” [PAGE 11], while Lenovo and Pirelli improved [PAGE 11]. The correct recursive inference is not “personalization works” or “personalization fails”; it is that personalization is an interaction between self-reference, visibility, brand familiarity, and creative execution. The paper itself admits the limitation: “the personalized messages used in this study did not include individual participants’ names” [PAGE 17]. That means the manipulation could not trigger strong self-reference, so the observed \(0.79\) s TTF is a lower-bound or generic-personalization effect, not an individual-name effect.

The counter-trap is overcorrection: after seeing Qatar Airways decline, an analyst may conclude that personalization backfires. That is also too simple. Lenovo and Pirelli increased unaided recall [PAGE 11], and PVABs matched racing cars on average time spent, \(0.69\) s [PAGE 9]. A better recursive model is that viewers infer persuasive intent. When the personalized message is obviously targeted but poorly integrated or low-visibility, it can trigger reactance or be discounted; when it is integrated into a high-visibility track infrastructure placement, it can exploit self-reference and fluency. The source’s discussion of “overload theory” and clutter [PAGE 15] supports this interaction: personalization reduces clutter only if it replaces, rather than adds to, competing messages.

The clock is decisive. The experiment uses 3 s per frame and 60 s total [PAGE 8]; TTF is measured in seconds; recall is measured after a short exposure. The source also acknowledges the sleeper effect over time [PAGE 3] and first-half versus second-half attention differences [PAGE 4]. A recursive theory of mind must therefore separate three clocks: the fixation clock, \(0.79\) s; the trial clock, 3 s; and the memory clock, days to weeks. The paper measures the first two and infers the third weakly. The correct counter-trap is to require a delayed recall test, a repeated-exposure schedule, and a manipulation check on perceived personalization. Otherwise the analyst regresses to the habit of treating immediate eye movement as durable brand memory, which the Porter (2022) citation explicitly warns against [PAGE 6].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto: the visibility operator is valid only when the virtual overlay is unoccluded and the camera geometry is stable. F1 is a moving-camera sport on “5 to 7-kilometer tracks” [PAGE 4], and the paper uses static frames [PAGE 16]. A physical veto applies if a signage area is partially hidden by a car, barrier, bridge, or tunnel edge; if motion blur reduces the effective contrast; or if a camera cut changes the frame between signage insertion and gaze measurement. The Monaco tunnel result, where Qatar Airways reached \(20.31\%\) visibility [PAGE 8], is a high-visibility exception that cannot be generalized to open-track corners without matching camera distance, angle, and occlusion. Formally, the claim \(V_i\to\text{attention}\) is vetoed when
\[
\Pr(\text{signage unoccluded}\mid \text{frame }i)<\tau_{\text{phys}}
\]
or when the overlay is composited with non-physical luminance. The paper does not report such a mask, so the geometric \(V_i\) is an upper bound on visible exposure.

Biomechanical layer veto: webcam eye-tracking depends on calibration, head pose, blink, and fixation classification. The source says the procedure “commenced with webcam calibration” [PAGE 7] and that Realeye.io “assigns a grade to assess the quality of eye-tracking data” [PAGE 7], but the extracted text does not report grade distributions. A biomechanical veto applies if calibration grade is low, if missingness is non-random across AOIs, or if fixations near the frame boundary are systematically lost. With two groups of 10 [PAGE 7], a single poor calibration can materially shift a group mean. The claim that PVABs produce \(0.79\) s TTF is therefore not transportable unless the analysis conditions on calibration grade and uses participant-level random effects.

Weather and lighting layer veto: the paper does not stratify by wet/dry, day/night, glare, spray, or contrast. Virtual overlays can be more or less visually coherent under different luminance and weather conditions. If rain or spray reduces contrast in the physical scene, a bright virtual board may become more salient; if sunlight or glare washes out the overlay, it may become less salient. The source’s static frames cannot adjudicate this. A weather veto applies when
\[
\text{Contrast}_{\text{overlay}}-\text{Contrast}_{\text{background}}<\tau_{\text{contrast}}
\]
or when the compositing boundary is visible. Under those conditions, the measured \(V_i\) and TTF do not identify the personalization effect; they identify a rendering artifact. Cross-layer proof therefore requires at least three controls: camera-geometry matching, calibration-grade filtering, and weather/lighting stratification. Without them, the paper’s strongest claim is descriptive: in its 22 selected frames and 20-participant preliminary sample, PVABs had a lower observed TTF than the chosen standard billboard comparator. The causal and broadcast-wide claims remain vetoed at the physical, biomechanical, and weather layers.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto scenario 1: matched-visibility within-subject PVAB test. The source plans an “ongoing larger experiment” targeting “100 participants” [PAGE 17]. Counterfactual: run a within-subject randomized trial where each participant sees the same frame with a generic PVAB and a standard VAB, matched on \(A_{\text{signage}}\), location, brand, and frame duration. Prediction if the RQ2 claim is real: \(\Delta_{\text{TTFF}}=\overline{T}^{(1)}_{\text{non-personalized}}-\overline{T}^{(1)}_{\text{personalized}}\ge 0.15\) s with a 95% confidence interval excluding zero. If the observed \(\Delta_{\text{TTFF}}\) is less than \(0.05\) s or the interval includes zero, the source’s \(0.79\) s advantage is vetoed as a comparator artifact. This is verifiable because the source’s own Table III gives \(1.04-0.79=0.25\) s, while the conclusion’s \(1.35-0.79=0.56\) s comparator is inconsistent [PAGE 10, PAGE 14].

Veto scenario 2: visibility-controlled recall model. Counterfactual: estimate a mixed logistic model with \(U_b\) as the outcome and fixed effects for \(V_b\), personalization \(P_b\), brand familiarity, and \(V_b\times P_b\). Prediction if personalization has an independent recall effect: \(\beta_{P}>0\) and the interaction \(\delta>0\) with 95% intervals excluding zero after controlling for \(V_b\). If \(\beta_{P}\) collapses toward zero once \(V_b\) and familiarity are included, the claim that personalization drives recall is vetoed. This is directly testable against the source’s own pattern: Liqui Moly had \(23.64\%\) visibility but \(0\%\) lasting impression [PAGE 12], and Qatar Airways’ personalized frame was placed in “a low-visibility size” yet still produced \(25\%\) lasting impression [PAGE 12, PAGE 14]. The model must explain both, or the causal story is underspecified.

Veto scenario 3: live dynamic broadcast replication. Counterfactual: collect live or near-live eye-tracking during actual race cuts, with camera-cut rate, weather, lighting, and occlusion coded. Prediction if the static-frame result transports: PVABs retain at least \(0.05\) s lower TTF and at least a \(5\) percentage-point higher unaided recall than matched VABs. If the live study shows no TTF advantage, or if the advantage reverses under wet/dark/high-cut-rate conditions, the static-frame generalization is vetoed. This counterfactual is especially important because the paper itself lists “static frame analysis” as a limitation [PAGE 16] and because F1 camera dynamics on “5 to 7-kilometer tracks” [PAGE 4] are not represented by 3-second static frames [PAGE 8]. A verifiable RLVR reward here would be the sign and confidence interval of the live-versus-static interaction; a negative interaction would be a clean veto, not a rhetorical objection.
