Third, information-retrieval vector-space models (Salton cosine similarity; Jaccard index) supplied the mathematical vocabulary for scoring term-item or item-item overlap, though the patent claims only raw counts and co-occurrence frequency rather than any logarithmic or cosine transform.

Multi-week traps emerge from the temporal structure of the offline/online split. Because the similar-items lists are generated in an off-line mode [PAGE 19] and stored for “subsequent look-up,” any shift in user co-occurrence behavior—holiday purchasing, fashion cycles, or sudden viral adoption—is invisible until the next batch rebuild. If the rebuild interval stretches across multiple weeks, the offline matrix \(S(i,j)\) systematically under-represents emergent item relationships and over-represents decaying ones, producing relevance decay that compounds week over week. Claim 36 [PAGE 20] exacerbates this by using popularity levels to select which products receive stored similar-item sets; head items dominate the offline structure while long-tail items are starved of representation, creating a multi-week rich-get-richer trap in which new or niche products cannot penetrate the top-K truncation until a rebuild admits them. Claim 40’s cross-machine replication [PAGE 20] adds a second temporal trap: propagation lag across replicas means that even after a rebuild, different users may be served from stale partitions for days or weeks, fragmenting the personalization experience and violating read-your-writes consistency. Finally, claim 28(c)’s truncation [PAGE 19] ossifies the candidate set; because truncation happens offline, an item relationship that strengthens in week three cannot influence rankings in weeks one and two, imposing a hard multi-week barrier to discovery.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression occurs when the system falls back from personalized union to a non-personalized heuristic. Under load spikes, replica lag (claim 40 [PAGE 20]), or offline rebuild failure, the real-time composition \(R_u(c)\) becomes unreliable or unavailable. The system then regresses to a habit of serving popularity-ranked items—essentially using the claim 36 popularity levels [PAGE 20] as a proxy for individual taste. This is a first-order theory-of-mind failure: the system models the user as “the average user” rather than maintaining a model of \(I_u\).

The counter-trap is the patent’s core personalization design. Claims 25 and 28 [PAGE 19] explicitly remove the requirement for explicit user ratings and instead build recommendations from implicit interest sets known to the system. By combining similar-items lists derived from multiple products known to be of interest to the user [PAGE 19], the architecture constructs a user-specific hypothesis about taste without asking the user to articulate it. This counters the popularity regression by preserving the recursive structure: recommendations depend on the user’s own item set, not on global aggregates alone.

The clock is the hard real-time constraint of claim 33 [PAGE 19-20]: “immediately generating and displaying personal product recommendations.” This imposes a latency ceiling on the query-time union. If the B-tree is undergoing rebalancing (claim 39 [PAGE 20]) or replicas are synchronizing after an offline rebuild (claim 40 [PAGE 20]), the clock forces a choice: serve a stale read, degrade to a cached fallback, or miss the display deadline. The recursive loop closes through user behavior: recommendations influence purchases; purchases alter \(C(i,j)\) in the next offline batch; the altered matrix changes future recommendations; users, anticipating the recommender, adjust their browsing and cart behavior (claim 24 multiple shopping carts [PAGE 19]), which in turn reshapes the co-occurrence counts. This is a second-order fixed point in which the system’s model of the user and the user’s response to the model co-evolve across rebuild cycles.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: The data-structure replication required by claim 40 [PAGE 20] depends on data-center power, cooling, and inter-node network integrity. A veto condition at this layer is: if replica consistency lag \(\tau_{\text{rep}}\) exceeds a bound \(\tau_{\max}\), suppress personalized lookup and serve a static, edge-cached list. This is a physical-layer veto because it is triggered by infrastructure failure, not by recommendation quality.

Biomechanical / HCI layer: Claim 24 [PAGE 19] supports multiple shopping carts within a single account, with cart-specific recommendations tied to “a role of a particular shopping cart.” A veto arises when the user’s interaction tempo—cart switching, item addition, or session timeout—exceeds the human working-memory buffer for processing recommendations. If the system cannot render cart-specific recommendations within the user’s decision window, the recommendation is behaviorally vetoed by abandonment regardless of its score.

Weather / environmental layer: Regional weather events that disrupt edge connectivity or mobile-user uptime veto real-time personalization when the round-trip time to a replicated node violates the claim 33 immediacy requirement [PAGE 19-20]. The system must then fall back to locally cached non-personalized content.

Decidable logical vetoes are explicitly present in the claims. Claim 38 [PAGE 20] verbatim provides “inhibiting selection in (c) of products already purchased by the user,” and claim 26 [PAGE 19] provides filtering “out items already purchased by the user.” Formally:

\[
c \in \text{Recommended}(u) \;\Longrightarrow\; c \notin \text{Purchased}(u) \;\land\; c \in \text{AllowedCategories}(u),
\]

where the category constraint follows from claim 27 [PAGE 19]. These predicates are independent of \(S(i,j)\); they veto candidates before any similarity score is consulted. The veto proof is therefore complete: physical infra failure, HCI latency breach, weather-induced connectivity loss, and explicit ownership/category exclusion each independently remove candidates from the ranked set without requiring recalibration of the similarity operator.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR denotes a rule-based verification layer over recommendation outputs. Three verifiable veto scenarios:

Scenario 1 — Cold-start item veto. For a newly ingested item \(i_{\text{new}}\) with \(N(i_{\text{new}})=0\), claim 28(d) [PAGE 19] implies no offline list is stored because no users are yet interested in it. Veto: suppress \(i_{\text{new}}\) from all recommendations until \(C(i_{\text{new}},j)>\theta_c\) for some \(j\), e.g., \(\theta_c=5\) co-occurrences. Prediction: Precision@K for \(i_{\text{new}}\) is exactly 0 pre-threshold; after threshold crossing, precision rises as \(S(i_{\text{new}},j)\) estimates stabilize; system-wide recall remains unchanged because cold-start items contribute zero mass until admitted.

Scenario 2 — Already-purchased veto. Per claim 26/38 [PAGE 19-20], if candidate \(c\) belongs to \(\text{Purchased}(u)\), it is inhibited from the combined list regardless of \(S(i,c)\). Verification: instrument recommendation impressions and record click-through rate on vetoed items. Prediction: CTR on vetoed items equals 0; impression waste is eliminated; cart-add rate increases because users are not re-shown owned items; no degradation in NDCG@K for non-owned items is expected because the veto removes only zero-utility candidates.

Scenario 3 — Stale-offline veto. If the offline rebuild interval \(\Delta t\) exceeds a staleness bound \(\tau_{\text{stale}}\) (e.g., 14 days), and the co-occurrence drift metric

\[
D(\Delta t) \;=\; \frac{1}{M^2}\sum_{i,j} \bigl| S_t(i,j) - S_{t-\Delta t}(i,j) \bigr|
\]

exceeds \(\delta\), veto personalized ranking and serve a global-popularity fallback. Prediction: NDCG@K decays monotonically with \(\Delta t\) beyond \(\tau_{\text{stale}}\), approximately linearly for small drift and exponentially for large drift; switching to the fallback arrests further decay but sacrifices the personalization lift derived from \(I_u\)-specific union [PAGE 19]. Each scenario is verifiable through logged counters (co-occurrence thresholds, purchase-membership flags, and drift metrics) without reliance on any betting line or simulated odds.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
