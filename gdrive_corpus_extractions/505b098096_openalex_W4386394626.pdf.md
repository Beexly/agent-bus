<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The following formal operators are the minimum needed to describe the source’s claimed pipeline. Units are stated explicitly; where the source gives no numeric parameter, that absence is part of the breakdown regime.

1. Pinhole camera projection. Assumption: central projection with calibrated intrinsics and negligible rolling shutter. 
\[
s\,\tilde{m}=K\,[R\mid t]\,\tilde{M},\qquad
K=(f_x,\gamma,c_x;\;0,f_y,c_y;\;0,0,1)
\]
Here \(\tilde{m}=(u,v,1)^T\) is in pixels, \(\tilde{M}=(X,Y,Z,1)^T\) is in metres, \(f_x,f_y\) are in pixels, \(c_x,c_y\) are in pixels, and \(\gamma\) is a pixel skew term. The scale \(s\) is in pixels per metre after normalization. Assumption: lens distortion has been removed. Breakdown: \(K\) is singular if \(f_x f_y=0\); calibration is invalid if the Jacobian is rank deficient or if reprojection error is unreported. The source says calibration “must be done only once” [PAGE 3], but one-time calibration can break under camera autofocus, device drop, or thermal drift.

2. Oriented FAST keypoint orientation. For image intensity \(I(x,y)\) in digital numbers or calibrated radiance, 
\[
m_{pq}=\sum_{x,y}x^p y^q I(x,y),\qquad
\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(m_{01},m_{10})
\]
\(\theta\) is in radians. Assumption: a local patch with sufficient intensity variation. Breakdown: \(\theta\) is undefined when \(m_{01}=m_{10}=0\), as in an isotropic or blank patch; low texture and motion blur cause unstable keypoints.

3. Binary descriptor distance. For two binary strings \(a,b\in\{0,1\}^N\), 
\[
d_H(a,b)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[a_i\neq b_i\right]
\]
\(d_H\) is in bits, and a match is declared if \(d_H\leq \tau_d\). Assumption: Hamming distance is meaningful for the chosen binary descriptor. Breakdown: repetitive patterns, severe blur, and a poorly chosen \(\tau_d\) create false matches. The source’s “FR AK” [PAGE 3] is presumably FREAK, but the exact descriptor length and threshold are absent.

4. RANSAC success probability. Let \(w\in[0,1]\) be the inlier fraction, \(n\) the minimal sample size, and \(k\) the iteration count. 
\[
P_{\text{success}}=1-\left(1-w^n\right)^k
\]
\(P_{\text{success}}\) is unitless probability. For a homography, \(n=4\) under the standard four-point assumption. Breakdown: if \(w\) is small, \(P_{\text{success}}\) collapses unless \(k\) is large; if correspondences are collinear, every sample is degenerate and the model is unidentifiable. The source says RANSAC keeps “just the inliers” [PAGE 3], but it gives no \(w\), \(k\), or inlier threshold.

5. Homography and DLT. For image points \(\tilde{x}=(u,v,1)^T\) and \(\tilde{x}'=(u',v',1)^T\), 
\[
\tilde{x}'\sim H\tilde{x},\qquad H\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}
\]
with \(H\) defined up to a nonzero scale. The DLT solves \(Ah=0\) by minimizing \(\|Ah\|^2\) subject to \(\|h\|=1\). Assumption: planar scene or pure rotation. Breakdown: nonplanar parallax produces no single global homography; fewer than four non-collinear correspondences give rank deficiency; a high condition number \(\kappa(H)\to\infty\) makes the pose unstable. The source’s “exact homography” [PAGE 3] is therefore a limiting idealization, not an exact identity under noise.

6. Pose from homography. Decomposition yields \(R\in SO(3)\) and \(t\in\mathbb{R}^3\), with rotation in radians and translation in metres. Assumption: calibrated \(K\), planar target, and correct homography. Breakdown: small translation produces planar-pose ambiguity; two decomposition solutions can be nearly indistinguishable; rolling shutter and nonplanar motion invalidate the model.

7. Landmark action classifier. Let the pose at frame \(t\) be 
\[
F_t=\{(\ell_i^t,c_i^t)\}_{i=1}^{33},\qquad
\ell_i^t=(x_i^t,y_i^t,z_i^t)
\]
where \(x,y,z\) are normalized image coordinates or metres depending on the pose model, and \(c_i^t\in[0,1]\) is confidence. The classifier is 
\[
\hat{y}_t=\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}p(y\mid F_{t-T:t}),
\quad
\mathcal{Y}=\{\text{left},\text{right},\text{jump},\text{crouch},\text{stand},\text{run},\text{go}\}
\]
The label is unitless and the posterior is a probability. Assumption: one dominant subject with visible landmarks. Breakdown: occlusion, multiple players, loose clothing, camera tilt, and child-specific body proportions. The source names “33 landmarks” [PAGE 5] and admits “multiple players or environmental factors” [PAGE 7], but gives no classifier architecture, confusion matrix, or confidence calibration.

8. Jump rule as a geometric predicate. If image \(y\) increases downward and the shoulder line is \(y_{\text{line}}(t)\), a literal encoding is 
\[
J_t=1 \quad\text{if}\quad
y_L(t)&lt;y_{\text{line}}(t)
\;\land\;
y_R(t)&lt;y_{\text{line}}(t)
\]
where \(y_L,y_R\) are in pixels. Assumption: the camera is level and both shoulders remain in frame. Breakdown: camera tilt changes the effective horizontal line; jumping out of frame or raising arms without a jump creates false positives. The source states the rule verbally on [PAGE 6] but gives no threshold, hysteresis, or temporal smoothing.

9. End-to-end latency budget. 
\[
T_{\text{e2e}}=T_{\text{capture}}+T_{\text{detect}}+T_{\text{desc}}+T_{\text{match}}
+T_{\text{RANSAC}}+T_{\text{classify}}+T_{\text{render}}
\]
All terms are in seconds. For a frame rate \(f_s\) in hertz, the real-time condition is 
\[
T_{\text{e2e}}\leq \frac{1}{f_s}.
\]
Breakdown: when \(T_{\text{e2e}}\) exceeds the budget, the command arrives after the game state has advanced; the child experiences control lag. The source reports no latency, frame rate, or device class.

10. Information and evaluation. Action entropy is 
\[
H(Y)=-\sum_y p(y)\log_2 p(y)\quad\text{bits},
\]
and mutual information with pose features is 
\[
I(Y;F)=H(Y)-H(Y\mid F)\quad\text{bits}.
\]
The source reports no such information measure. For evaluation, 
\[
F_1=\frac{2TP}{2TP+FP+FN}
\]
is unitless. Breakdown: \(F_1\) is undefined when \(TP=FP=FN=0\), and self-reported enjoyment [PAGE 2] is not a substitute for action-recognition \(F_1\), latency, or physical-activity energy expenditure.

The paper’s operators descend from a recognizable scientific lineage. Camera calibration and homography estimation come from photogrammetry and computer vision; RANSAC was introduced by Fischler and Bolles in 1981; ORB was introduced by Rublee et al. in 2011; FAST by Rosten and Drummond; BRIEF by Calonder et al.; FREAK by Alahi et al.; and 33-landmark pose estimation is characteristic of modern BlazePose/MediaPipe-style models. The source cites [27] for ORB, [28] for “FR AK,” and [29] for RANSAC on [PAGE 3], but it does not specify versions or parameterizations. The AR genealogy runs from Sutherland’s head-mounted display and Azuma’s surveys through ARToolKit, Vuforia, and mobile AR. The paper’s use of Unity, TensorFlow, and Vuforia [PAGE 2] places it in that applied lineage, but the excerpt gives no software versions, device specifications, or reproducibility package.

The retrocausal error is that a future claim is used to validate a present prototype. [PAGE 1] frames the work as a “promising antidote to sedentarism and a potent catalyst for endorsing a healthier lifestyle.” That future benefit is then treated as if it were already supported by the 30-minute, 20-child, self-report pilot [PAGE 2]. Genealogically, this repeats a common AR-exergame pattern: novelty produces high enjoyment in a short session, and the short session is interpreted as evidence of durable motivation. The source itself resists this inference on [PAGE 7] by asking whether the game “actually motivates children to engage in physical activities outside of the game” and by naming “long term” as unresolved. That admission is the correct retrocausal brake.

The multi-week traps are therefore predictable and testable. First is the novelty trap: week 1 enjoyment can be high because the interface is new, not because physical activity is intrinsically reinforced. Second is the habituation trap: week 2 to week 4 may show declining session length or declining action amplitude. Third is the compensation trap: children may learn the minimum movement that triggers the classifier, converting an exergame into a low-effort gesture game. Fourth is the transfer trap: in-game movement may not increase out-of-game moderate-to-vigorous physical activity. Fifth is the self-report trap: enjoyment and willingness are not the same as measured energy expenditure. None of these traps is proven by the excerpt; each is a falsifiable multi-week hypothesis. A credible genealogy must replace the single 30-minute clock with repeated measures, concealed accelerometry, retention curves, and a control condition.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In computational terms, the system and child form a recursive estimation loop. The game estimates child capability \(\hat{C}_c(t)\) from pose features \(F_{t-T:t}\); the child estimates game difficulty \(\hat{D}_g(t)\) from recent success, failure, and fatigue. A second-order representation can be written schematically as 
\[
B_c^{(2)}\left[B_g^{(1)}\left[\hat{C}_c(t)\right]\right],
\]
meaning the child’s belief about the game’s belief about the child’s capability. This is not literal mind-reading; it is a compact way to describe co-adaptation. If the game lowers difficulty whenever action confidence is low, the child can learn that low movement is followed by easier play. That is the counter-trap: the adaptive controller can invert the intended incentive. The source claims “immediate feedback and rewards” [PAGE 3] and personalized difficulty [PAGE 1], but gives no reward schedule, no difficulty law, and no test for this inversion.

Crisis habit regression is the second failure mode. Under fatigue, embarrassment, or repeated failure, a child may regress to the most familiar habit: touchscreen control or minimal standing movement. If the classifier interprets that regression as low capability and reduces difficulty, the system reinforces the sedentary response. The paper’s own safety and accuracy concerns [PAGE 7] make this plausible, but the excerpt provides no longitudinal behavioral data. The correct audit question is not “does the child enjoy the game?” but “does the child’s movement amplitude, action diversity, and out-of-game activity persist when the novelty declines?”

The clock problem is decisive. [PAGE 2] uses a 30-minute session; [PAGE 7] asks about long-term motivation. A 30-minute clock cannot distinguish novelty from habit formation, and self-reported enjoyment cannot distinguish intrinsic motivation from immediate feedback. A counter-trap-aware design would measure movement amplitude, command latency, heart rate, session retention, and out-of-game activity over multiple weeks. The recursive theory of mind therefore predicts three verifiable patterns: if difficulty adapts downward on low action confidence, minimal-movement exploits will increase; if the child models the classifier as forgiving, crouch and jump actions will shrink in amplitude; and if the game clock is short, apparent engagement will overstate durable behavior change.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Safety is non-compensatory. Let \(D\in\{0,1\}\) be deployment and \(V_\ell\in\{0,1\}\) be a veto from layer \(\ell\). The admissibility rule is 
\[
D\leq\prod_{\ell}\left(1-V_\ell\right).
\]
Thus if any layer vetoes, deployment is zero regardless of recognition accuracy. This is a proof by dominance: suppose \(V_{\text{phys}}=1\) because the free space is too small, but \(D=1\). Then a jump or lateral command can be executed into an obstacle, contradicting the physical safety constraint. Therefore no \(F_1\) score can override \(V_{\text{phys}}\). The source itself warns of “an increase in accidents and injuries” [PAGE 7], so this veto is internal to the paper’s own risk analysis.

Physical veto. Let \(A_{\text{free}}\) be free floor area in \(m^2\), \(r_{\text{body}}\) the child’s body radius in metres, \(r_{\text{reach}}\) the reach envelope in metres, and \(r_{\text{margin}}\) a safety margin in metres. A necessary condition is 
\[
A_{\text{free}}\geq\pi\left(r_{\text{body}}+r_{\text{reach}}+r_{\text{margin}}\right)^2.
\]
Breakdown: child anthropometry varies; a room that passes for one child may fail for another. If the condition fails, lateral and jump commands are vetoed even if the pose classifier is perfect.

Biomechanical veto. For a jump with takeoff speed \(v_0\) in \(m\,s^{-1}\) and gravitational acceleration \(g=9.81\,m\,s^{-2}\), the ballistic height is 
\[
h=\frac{v_0^2}{2g}\quad\text{metres}.
\]
Landing energy is \(E_{\text{land}}=mgh\) in joules, and ground-reaction impulse is \(J=\int F\,dt\) in \(N\,s\). A deployment veto applies if \(h>h_{\max}\), \(E_{\text{land}}>E_{\max}\), or \(J>J_{\max}\) for the child’s age and footwear. The source gives no such thresholds; that absence is itself a veto against unsupervised use claims. On a wet floor with friction coefficient \(\mu\), a landing deceleration \(a_h\) requires \(\mu\geq a_h/g\). If weather lowers \(\mu\) below that bound, the jump command is unsafe even when recognized correctly.

Weather and sensing veto. Let illumination be \(E_v\) in lux and camera signal-to-noise ratio be \(\mathrm{SNR}\) in unitless power ratio. If \(E_v\) is outside the camera’s dynamic range, or \(\mathrm{SNR}&lt;\mathrm{SNR}_{\min}\), the action-recognition channel is invalid. The source explicitly names “environmental factors that could interfere with the recognition” [PAGE 7]. Therefore weather is not a cosmetic nuisance; it is a validity veto for the vision layer and a safety veto for landing surface friction. The adjudication order should be physical space first, biomechanics second, weather and sensing third, and only then recognition accuracy. Any reverse order allows a high-scoring classifier to authorize an unsafe action.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Define a verifier-gated reward for counterfactual testing: 
\[
R_t=R_{\text{task}}(y_t,\hat{y}_t)
-\lambda_s V_{\text{safety}}
-\lambda_l\mathbf{1}\left[T_{\text{e2e}}>T_{\max}\right]
-\lambda_p\mathbf{1}\left[\text{activity}<\text{baseline}\right],
\]
where \(R_{\text{task}}\) is unitless, \(\lambda_s,\lambda_l,\lambda_p\) are unitless weights, \(V_{\text{safety}}\in\{0,1\}\), \(T_{\text{e2e}}\) is in seconds, and activity may be measured in MVPA minutes per day. This is not a claim that the paper used RLVR; it is a counterfactual test harness.

Scenario 1: multi-person occlusion veto. Intervention: a second child enters the camera frame during 20 percent of play. Verifier: action \(F_1\), command error rate, and landmark association error. Prediction: without explicit subject tracking, landmarks switch identity and the command error rate rises; the deployment claim is vetoed if \(F_1&lt;F_{1,\min}\) or if command error exceeds the pre-registered limit. This directly tests the source’s admission of “multiple players” [PAGE 7].

Scenario 2: free-space biomechanical veto. Intervention: compare a large clear area with a constrained area around the child. Verifier: near-miss count, jump-command rate, and \(A_{\text{free}}\). Prediction: when \(A_{\text{free}}\) falls below \(\pi(r_{\text{body}}+r_{\text{reach}}+r_{\text{margin}})^2\), near-misses increase and unsupervised home use is vetoed even if action recognition remains accurate. This scenario verifies that accuracy cannot compensate for physical risk, consistent with the paper’s injury warning [PAGE 7].

Scenario 3: multi-week transfer veto. Intervention: four-week home use with concealed accelerometry and a no-game comparison period. Verifier: out-of-game MVPA minutes per day, action amplitude, session retention, and self-reported enjoyment. Prediction: short-term enjoyment may remain high while out-of-game MVPA does not exceed baseline; if so, the claim that the game promotes long-term physical activity is vetoed. This is the direct counterfactual to [PAGE 2]’s 30-minute self-report and [PAGE 7]’s unresolved long-term question.
