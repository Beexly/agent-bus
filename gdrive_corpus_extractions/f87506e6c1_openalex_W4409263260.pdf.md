The recursive risk is that frequent reinitialization can reduce temporal smoothness and hide the original cause, such as poor segmentation, a wrong \(T\), or an invalid \(k_1\).

The counter-trap is the decision not to trust optical flow when confidence is low. The paper explicitly says the score is used “when the optical flow correspondences are to be discarded to prevent them from damaging the optimization, as it relies on the previous camera parameters \(\kappa_{t-1}\)” [PAGE 5]. That is a theory-of-mind move inside the system: it models the previous camera estimate as a potentially corrupted prior and refuses to let self-generated correspondences confirm the corruption. A second counter-trap is player filtering with RTMDet [PAGE 5], which prevents human motion from being treated as rigid pitch-plane motion. The residual trap is threshold calibration: too high a threshold causes crisis habit regression; too low a threshold lets drift persist.

The clock layer is the temporal budget and horizon. The paper reports 16 fps for HD images on a server with two RTX 4090 GPUs, without optimization [PAGE 1]. At 16 fps, a 15-frame loss is about 0.94 s, and a 4-frame loss is about 0.25 s. The 30-second benchmark is 750 frames per sequence [PAGE 6], while the long clip is 60,000 frames [PAGE 7]. The clock therefore changes the meaning of a reinitialization: in a 30-second clip, 33 reinitializations over 49 sequences is frequent but survivable; over weeks, the same habit may be unacceptable. The “vertigo effect” is the clock’s optical counterpart: focal length and position can trade off over time, so a smooth-looking track can still have drifting physical parameters [PAGE 7]. The correct recursive policy is to monitor reinitialization rate, loss duration, focal-length jitter, and tripod residual together, not to optimize JaC alone.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The optical veto is proven in-source by ablation: removing radial distortion drops JaC5 from 54.1 to 42.09 and raises MRE from 5.04 to 5.74 [PAGE 7]. The paper states “the biggest improvement in performance comes from the inclusion of radial distortion in the camera model” [PAGE 7]. Therefore, any homography-only or zero-distortion claim is vetoed for this broadcast setting unless it reproduces those numbers. The optical veto also fails outside the first-order Brown-Conrady regime: if \(1+k_1r^2\le0\), the distortion map is non-invertible.

The mechanical veto is the fixed tripod geometry. The source assumes the pan and tilt axes intersect in a point \(T\) that “remains fixed during the whole game” [PAGE 4]. If \(T\) moves, or if \(\delta\) changes, \(L_T=\delta-\|O^*-T\|_2\) becomes nonzero. The paper’s qualitative evidence is that the focal point estimated by NBJW “can travel up to 20 meters along a single sequence, while our focal point remains in a close neighborhood of the estimated tripod position” [PAGE 8]. That is a mechanical plausibility veto against unconstrained per-frame calibration. However, because \(\omega\) is unspecified and \(L_T\) is soft, a large pixel residual can still outvote a small mechanical violation; the veto is probabilistic, not absolute.

The biomechanical veto has two parts. First, the human operator is modeled as producing smooth pan and tilt because “The pan-tilt head allows the camera to rotate smoothly while allowing the camera operators to make quick sweeps if they lose track of the action” [PAGE 3]. If the operator whip-pans faster than the optical-flow pyramid can track, or if the head vibrates, the smoothness prior is violated. Second, players are biomechanical non-plane objects. The paper removes optical-flow points around player bounding boxes using RTMDet [PAGE 5]. If player detection misses a limb, shadow, or referee, the ray-plane intersection \(X=L\cap\{Z=0\}\) is wrong and the camera estimate is vetoed by non-planar motion. This is a concrete cross-layer veto: human motion can corrupt a geometric constraint.

The weather veto is not proven in-source. The paper provides no rain, wind, snow, fog, lens-droplet, or thermal-expansion ablation. The 20-minute sequence is qualitative only [PAGE 7]. Therefore the correct adjudication is conservative: the system’s weather robustness is unproven; if wind moves \(T\), if rain changes lens distortion, or if droplets create false edges, the physical model can be violated. The source supports optical, mechanical, and limited biomechanical vetoes; it does not support an all-weather claim.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

1. Radial-distortion counterfactual. Re-run the no-distortion and with-distortion conditions under the same optical-flow and tripod settings. Prediction from the paper’s ablation: with \(k_1=0\), JaC5 should fall toward 42.09 and MRE should rise toward 5.74; with \(k_1\) enabled, JaC5 should rise toward 54.1 and MRE should fall toward 5.04 [PAGE 7]. If the gap disappears, the claim that radial distortion is the dominant physical correction is vetoed.

2. Confidence-threshold counterfactual. Change the reinitialization threshold from \(s=0.5\) to \(s=0.2\) on the 49 sn-gamestate sequences. Prediction: the reinitialization count should drop below the reported 33, and either average tracking-loss duration should rise above 15 frames or JaC5/MRE should degrade because drift is no longer corrected [PAGE 6]. If the count does not change, the confidence score is not causally controlling reinitialization.

3. Tripod-constraint counterfactual. Remove the tripod term \(L_T\) or fix the focal point as in the “Ours (fixed C)” row. Prediction from Table 1: JaC5 should fall from 56.88 to about 50.97 and MRE should rise from 5.02 to about 5.39 [PAGE 6]; qualitatively, the focal point in the XY plane should disperse more, since NBJW’s focal point “can travel up to 20 meters” [PAGE 8]. If removing the tripod constraint does not increase focal-point dispersion or degrade these metrics, the mechanical-model claim is vetoed.
