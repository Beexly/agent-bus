<THINK_TRACE>
The requested object is a statistical modeling paper, not a live contest or a causal physical system. Therefore the reasoning must first translate the paper’s objects into a formal chain of inference: shot attempts are treated as spatial events; those events are summarized by intensity surfaces; the intensity surfaces are log-transformed; the log-intensities are regressed on player covariates; and the regression is implemented through an adaptive functional basis plus Bayesian additive regression trees. The central question becomes: under what formal assumptions does this chain yield valid estimates, and where are the gray-zone failure regimes?

The paper states that the goal is to model “spatial distribution of shot attempts, also known as intensity surfaces” and that it uses a “Functional Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (FBART) framework” before proposing “Adaptive Functional Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (AFBART)” [PAGE 1]. The data are from the “2017–2018 National Basketball Association (NBA) regular season,” with players restricted to those who “attempted more than 400 field goals” and excluding rookies such as “Lonzo Ball and Jayson Tatum” [PAGE 4]. The final analytic sample is “191 players” [PAGE 4]. This restriction matters: any downstream use must respect the support of the data. The model is not a universal NBA prior; it is a season-specific estimator conditioned on established players with substantial shot volume.

The first formal layer is the point-process intensity. The paper says the intensity surfaces “capture the spatial distribution of both made and missed field goals” and are constructed from tracking data [PAGE 4]. The intensity surface is discretized over a half-court domain “D ∈ [0,72] × [0,74], divided into a grid of 72 × 74 boxes” [PAGE 4]. If those coordinates are interpreted as court coordinates, they may be treated as spatial units; if converted to SI units, one may use 1 ft = 0.3048 m, but the paper itself does not specify physical units. In the absence of that specification, the safest formal interpretation is that the intensity values are grid-cell relative frequencies or modeled spatial rates, with units of attempts per spatial cell per season unless further normalized. The log transform in Equation (1) then operates on positive intensity values, and the Gaussian error variance is expressed in squared log-intensity units.

The second formal layer is function-on-scalar regression. Equation (1) is written as “Zi(·) = log(λi(·)) = Ξxi(·) + ei,” where the conditional mean function is “Ξx(·) = E(log λ(·) | x)” and “ei is the independent Gaussian white noise process with variance σ2 > 0” [PAGE 6]. This assumption is strong: it requires positive intensities, additive Gaussian noise on the log scale, and conditional mean separability. If any grid cell has exactly zero observed intensity, the log transform is undefined unless the underlying latent LGCP intensity is already smoothed to be strictly positive. The model therefore depends on a positivity regime: λ_i(s) > 0 almost everywhere on the evaluated domain. If the latent intensity is not strictly positive, the transformation breaks down.

The third formal layer is basis expansion. Equation (2) says “Ξx(·) = fT(·)h(x),” where “f(·) = (f1(·),...,fJ(·))T is a vector of basis functions on B” and “h : Rp →RJ is a multivariate regression function” [PAGE 6]. This is a reduced-rank representation. The basis functions f_j capture spatial modes; the coefficient functions h_j(x) capture how player covariates modulate those spatial modes. The adaptive version uses Equation (3): “fj(·) = bT(·)ψj,” where b is a “K-dimensional orthonormal low-rank thin plate splines (LR-TPS)” basis and ψ_j is a coefficient vector [PAGE 7]. The prior for ψ_j is given by Equation (4): “ψj | λj ∼ N(0, λj^{-1} Ω^{-1}), π(λj) ∝ 1” [PAGE 7]. This is a roughness-penalized Gaussian prior. The smoothing parameter λ_j controls how wiggly the spatial basis functions are allowed to become. Because the prior on λ_j is improper in the source, posterior propriety depends on the likelihood and the roughness penalty providing enough information. If ψ_j lies exactly in the null space of Ω, then ψ_j^T Ω ψ_j = 0, and the conditional Gamma update for λ_j has rate zero; that is a formal breakdown regime.

The fourth formal layer is tree regression. The multivariate regression tree is defined in Equation (5): “g(·;T,M) = ∑_{t=1}^T ∑_{ℓ=1}^{L_t} μ_{tℓ} I_{D_ℓ^t}(·)” [PAGE 8]. Each tree partitions covariate space into rectangular cells and assigns vector-valued leaf parameters μ_{tℓ}. The ensemble is additive. The prior is regularized through a tree-generating process where a node at depth d is non-terminal with probability “psplit(d) = aγ^d,” with “a ∈ (0,1] and γ ∈ (0,1)” [PAGE 8]. This controls tree depth and overfitting. The combined model is Equation (6): “zi = (BΨ)g(xi;T,M) + ei, ei ∼ N(0, σ2 I_M)” [PAGE 8]. Here z_i is the vector of log-intensities for player i at M spatial points, B is the thin-plate basis matrix, Ψ is the basis-coefficient matrix, and g(x_i;T,M) is the tree-ensemble coefficient vector.

The gray-zone audit is now multi-hop. First, the model estimates spatial tendency, not causal coaching strategy. Variable importance is computed by splitting proportions, and the paper reports that “the top five variables influencing a player’s shooting pattern are their position, block percentage, steal percentage, the number of games played, and their 3-point attempt rate” [PAGE 15]. But splitting proportion is not a causal effect. It measures how often a covariate is used in tree splits across the ensemble. A variable can be important for prediction because it proxies role, era, usage, or team context. Second, the model does not include live defensive pressure, score, clock, fatigue, injury, or opponent-specific scheme. The paper notes that tracking data can include “defensive pressure, player movement, and game dynamics” [PAGE 1], but the AFBART covariates are “24 summary statistics” [PAGE 4], not real-time defensive state. Third, the model is season-aggregated. It cannot directly resolve whether a player’s shot location changes in the final minutes of a close game, after a foul, or against a specialized defender. Fourth, the model’s posterior uncertainty must be propagated. The paper emphasizes “uncertainty quantification” and uses posterior mean predictions plus credible intervals [PAGE 1], [PAGE 12]. Treating the posterior mean intensity surface as a deterministic fact discards that uncertainty. Fifth, the model is temporally static. The authors identify “time series of functional data” as future work, including “temporal dynamics in shooting patterns, trend identification, and change-point detection” [PAGE 19]. Until that extension is implemented, temporal counterfactuals are not licensed by the current model.

The deepest gray zone is the distinction between intensity and efficiency. The paper models shot attempt intensity surfaces, while advanced metrics such as true shooting percentage and win shares are discussed as context [PAGE 5]. A high-intensity zone is not automatically a high-efficiency zone, and a low-intensity zone is not automatically inefficient. The model can answer where a player tends to attempt shots, conditional on covariates, but it does not by itself answer where the player should attempt shots under optimal decision theory unless coupled with shot-quality and expected-value models. The source itself notes that shot charts help identify “high-probability shooting zones,” but that language is descriptive and strategic, not a formal decision rule [PAGE 2].

Let s ∈ B denote a spatial location on the half-court domain. The paper uses B ⊂ R^2 [PAGE 6]. Let λ_i(s) denote player i’s intensity surface. The source says the intensity surface “maps each point on the half-court domain B to a real-valued intensity, reflecting the frequency and distribution of shot attempts” [PAGE 4]. If λ_i(s) is interpreted as a spatial rate, its units are attempts per unit area per season; if interpreted as a grid-cell frequency, its units are attempts per grid cell per season. If normalized, it may be dimensionless relative intensity. The source does not specify physical units, so the most conservative representation is: λ_i(s) ≥ 0, with strictly positive values required for the log transform.

The discretized model uses spatial points S = {s_1, ..., s_M} ⊂ B [PAGE 8]. For the NBA application, M = 72 × 74 = 5,328 grid boxes [PAGE 4]. For each player i, z_i = (Z_i(s_1), ..., Z_i(s_M))^T ∈ R^M [PAGE 8]. If Z_i(s_m) = log λ_i(s_m), then z_i has units of log-intensity. If natural logarithms are used, information-theoretic quantities derived from the Gaussian likelihood are measured in nats; if base-2 logarithms were used elsewhere, they would be measured in bits, but the source uses the conventional natural-log notation “log” [PAGE 6].

The core function-on-scalar regression model is Equation (1):  
\[
Z_i(\cdot) = \log(\lambda_i(\cdot)) = \Xi_{x_i}(\cdot) + e_i, \quad i = 1, \ldots, n,
\]
with  
\[
\Xi_x(\cdot) = E(\log \lambda(\cdot) \mid x),
\]
and  
\[
e_i \sim \text{Gaussian white noise with variance } \sigma^2 > 0.
\]
This is quoted in source form as “Zi(·) = log(λi(·)) = Ξxi(·) + ei,” with the conditional mean defined as “Ξx(·) = E(log λ(·) | x)” and “ei is the independent Gaussian white noise process with variance σ2 > 0” [PAGE 6]. The error variance σ² has units of squared log-intensity. The likelihood for the discretized model is given by the source as  
\[
p(z_1,\ldots,z_n \mid \Psi,T,M,\sigma^2) \propto (\sigma^2)^{-nM/2} \exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \|z_i - B\Psi g_i\|_2^2\right\},
\]
where g_i = g(x_i; T, M) [PAGE 8]. The exponent is dimensionless because the squared residual norm is divided by σ². The likelihood is a probability density over z-space; if z is log-intensity, the density is with respect to Lebesgue measure on R^M per player.

The basis expansion is Equation (2):  
\[
\Xi_x(\cdot) = f^T(\cdot) h(x),
\]
where f(·) = (f_1(·), ..., f_J(·))^T and h : R^p → R^J [PAGE 6]. The adaptive basis construction is Equation (3):  
\[
f_j(\cdot) = b^T(\cdot)\psi_j, \quad j \in [J],
\]
where b is a K-dimensional orthonormal low-rank thin-plate spline basis and ψ_j ∈ R^K [PAGE 7]. The prior for ψ_j is Equation (4):  
\[
\psi_j \mid \lambda_j \sim N(0, \lambda_j^{-1}\Omega^{-1}), \quad \pi(\lambda_j) \propto 1,
\]
where Ω is a roughness penalty matrix based on second derivatives of the basis functions [PAGE 7]. The smoothing parameter λ_j has units inverse to ψ_j^T Ω ψ_j, because the prior precision is λ_j Ω. If ψ_j is dimensionless and Ω has units of second-derivative inner products, λ_j carries reciprocal units. The conditional posterior for λ_j in Algorithm 1 is Gamma with shape K/2 + 1 and rate ψ_j^T Ω ψ_j / 2 [PAGE 10]. This update is proper only if ψ_j^T Ω ψ_j > 0. If ψ_j lies in the null space of Ω, the rate is zero and the Gamma posterior is undefined.

The tree ensemble is Equation (5):  
\[
g(\cdot; T, M) = \sum_{t=1}^T g(\cdot; T_t, M_t) = \sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{\ell=1}^{L_t} \mu_{t\ell} I_{D_\ell^t}(\cdot).
\]
Each μ_{tℓ} ∈ R^J is a vector-valued leaf parameter [PAGE 7]. The partition cells D_ℓ^t are rectangular subsets of covariate space induced by binary splits of the form “{x : x^{(j)} ≤ z} versus {x : x^{(j)} > z}” [PAGE 7]. The combined discretized AFBART model is Equation (6):  
\[
z_i = (B\Psi)g(x_i; T, M) + e_i, \quad e_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2 I_M),
\]
with model parameters {Ψ, T, M, σ²} [PAGE 8].

The posterior sampling scheme is a Gibbs sampler with Metropolis-Hastings updates for trees [PAGE 8]. The full conditional for σ² is inverse-gamma:  
\[
\sigma^2 \sim \text{InvGamma}\left(\frac{\nu + nM}{2}, \frac{\lambda\nu + \sum_{i=1}^n \|z_i - B\Psi g_i\|_2^2}{2}\right),
\]
as shown in Algorithm 1 [PAGE 10]. The full conditional for ψ_j is multivariate normal with covariance  
\[
\Sigma_{\psi_j} = \left(\sigma^{-2} B^T B \sum_{i=1}^N g_i^2(j) + \lambda_j \Omega\right)^{-1},
\]
subject to orthogonality constraints and normalization “ψ_j ← ψ_j / ||Bψ_j||_2” [PAGE 10]. The normalization breaks if Bψ_j = 0. The tree acceptance probability uses the marginal likelihood over leaf parameters and the tree prior [PAGE 10].

The evaluation operators are RMSPE, MIS, and MCRPS [PAGE 12]. RMSPE is defined as  
\[
\text{RMSPE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{Mn^*}\sum_{i=1}^{n^*}\sum_{m=1}^M |\hat{\Xi}_{x_i^*}(s_m) - \Xi_{x_i^*}(s_m)|^2}.
\]
If the target is log-intensity, RMSPE has units of log-intensity. If the target is exponentiated intensity, RMSPE has units of intensity. MIS is defined as  
\[
\text{MIS} = \frac{1}{Mn^*}\sum_{i=1}^{n^*}\sum_{m=1}^M \left[\hat{U}_{im} - \hat{L}_{im} + \frac{2}{0.05}\inf_{\eta \in [\hat{L}_{im}, \hat{U}_{im}]} |\Xi_{x_i^*}(s_m) - \eta|\right],
\]
where \hat{U}_{im} and \hat{L}_{im} are posterior quantiles [PAGE 12]. MCRPS is a probabilistic score; lower is better [PAGE 12]. In the NBA cross-validation comparison, AFBART achieves “average RMSPE (0.64)” and “average MCRPS (0.34),” while FBART-TPS has 0.67 and 0.36, and FBART-FPC has 0.68 and 0.36 [PAGE 15]. These values are model-relative, not physical court units.

Formal breakdown regimes are as follows. First, λ_i(s) = 0 breaks the log transform unless latent smoothing enforces strict positivity. Second, σ² = 0 collapses the Gaussian likelihood; the inverse-gamma update requires a positive posterior scale. Third, ψ_j^T Ω ψ_j = 0 makes the Gamma update for λ_j improper. Fourth, Bψ_j = 0 makes normalization undefined. Fifth, if J is too large relative to K or n, the basis becomes weakly identified; the orthonormality constraint identifies f and h only “up to orthonormal linear transformations” [PAGE 7]. Sixth, if a tree leaf contains no observations, the leaf posterior reduces to its prior and provides no data-driven signal. Seventh, if covariates are highly collinear, tree splits may be unstable and variable importance may be split among proxies. Eighth, if the model is applied outside the support of the 2017–2018 season or outside players with more than 400 FGA, extrapolation is not licensed by the source design [PAGE 4].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage begins with Bayesian Additive Regression Trees, cited as “BART Chipman, George and McCulloch, 2010” [PAGE 3]. BART’s core idea is an ensemble of shallow regression trees with regularization priors. The paper then extends this to functional response through “Functional Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (FBART, Cao, He and Zhang, 2025)” [PAGE 3]. FBART uses pre-specified basis functions, such as B-splines, but the authors argue that “when the domain is multidimensional, it typically requires a higher basis dimension to capture the data variation effectively” [PAGE 6]. AFBART’s adaptive basis is therefore a response to a dimensionality problem in spatial shot surfaces.

A second lineage comes from spatial statistics. The paper cites Log Gaussian Cox Processes: “Log Gaussian Cox Process (LGCP, Møller, Syversveen and Waagepetersen, 1998) is used to model the spatial pattern of those shot attempts for each player” [PAGE 2]. LGCP models are doubly stochastic: a latent Gaussian random field generates a positive intensity surface, and shot events are conditionally Poisson given that surface. This lineage matters because the observed shot map is a noisy realization of an underlying continuous spatial tendency. The AFBART layer does not replace LGCP; it uses the estimated intensity surfaces as functional responses and regresses their log-transformed values on player covariates.

A third lineage is functional data analysis. The paper cites “Ramsay and Dalzell, 1991; Ramsay and Silverman, 2005; Wang, Chiou and Müller, 2016” and formalizes the problem as “function-on-scalar regression (FOSR)” [PAGE 2], [PAGE 3]. FOSR treats the response as a function and covariates as scalars. The novelty is that the response is two-dimensional and spatially nonstationary, and the regression relationship may be nonlinear. The authors state that “existing research primarily focuses on functional linear models,” but they require “nonstationarity of intensity patterns and the nonlinear regression relationship” [PAGE 3]. This is why tree ensembles are introduced for the coefficient functions.

A fourth lineage is Bayesian function-on-scalar regression with adaptive bases. The paper cites “Kowal and Bourgeois (2020)” and says AFBART “builds on the foundation of nonlinear FOSR established by FBART and extends it by integrating Bayesian adaptive basis modeling, which aligns with techniques such as Bayesian functional principal component analysis” [PAGE 3]. The adaptive basis is therefore not arbitrary; it is a data-driven low-rank representation with smoothness priors. However, adaptivity creates a retro-causal trap: because the basis is learned from the same data, posterior uncertainty must include basis uncertainty. The paper attempts this through the Bayesian prior on ψ_j and λ_j, but any downstream consumer must remember that the estimated spatial modes are not externally fixed.

Multi-week traps emerge when this lineage is used outside the paper. The first trap is treating season-level intensity surfaces as stable player constants. The paper itself warns that shooting behavior is shaped by “the dynamics of the teammates” and “the defensive pressure” [PAGE 2], but the AFBART covariate set does not include game-level defensive pressure. A model fit on 2017–2018 may mislead if used several weeks later after a role change, injury, or strategic adjustment. The second trap is treating variable importance as causal. The paper reports splitting proportions for variables such as “position, block percentage, steal percentage, the number of games played, and their 3-point attempt rate” [PAGE 15], but splitting proportion is a model-usage metric, not an intervention effect. The third trap is ignoring the log-zero problem. If a player has zero attempts in a region, the raw intensity is zero, but the model operates on log intensity. The LGCP smoothing may avoid exact zeros, but the analyst must verify that the input surfaces are strictly positive. The fourth trap is ignoring the sample restriction: players with fewer than 400 FGA and rookies were excluded [PAGE 4]. Applying the model to low-volume players or rookies violates the data-generating support. The fifth trap is confusing attempt intensity with shot quality. The model estimates where attempts occur, not whether those attempts are efficient. The paper’s discussion of true shooting percentage and win shares is contextual, not the direct regression target [PAGE 5]. The sixth trap is temporal drift. The paper identifies “three-point revolution” as a major strategic shift [PAGE 15], but the model is trained on one regular season. If used weeks or seasons later, covariate distributions may shift, especially for 3-point attempt rate and position roles.

The retro-causal genealogy therefore warns that the paper’s model is best used as a descriptive Bayesian spatial estimator for established players in a fixed season, not as a universal causal engine for player deployment, tactical planning, or cross-era projection.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind analysis asks how the modeled player’s habits might be anticipated by opponents, and whether the statistical model can represent that anticipation. The source frames shot selection as shaped by “the positioning of the court, the dynamics of the teammates, and the defensive pressure” [PAGE 2]. Those are interactive mental and physical states. The AFBART model, however, regresses intensity surfaces on scalar player covariates, not on real-time opponent beliefs. It estimates a marginal tendency: what a player’s shot-location surface looks like as a function of stable summary statistics. It does not estimate how the player changes behavior when a defender overcommits, when a coach calls a specific play, or when the score and clock create urgency.

Let the true shot-intensity process be represented conceptually as Λ_i(s, C_t), where C_t denotes time-varying context: defensive alignment, score, clock, fatigue, foul trouble, and play-call. The AFBART estimand is closer to  
\[
m_i(s) = E[\log \Lambda_i(s, C) \mid X_i = x_i],
\]
where X_i contains season-level covariates such as position, minutes, true shooting percentage, win shares, and 3-point attempt rate [PAGE 5], [PAGE 4]. If C_t is omitted and correlated with X_i, the model’s predictions are marginal, not conditional on the live context. This is not a defect for descriptive analysis, but it is a limitation for strategic recursion.

Crisis habit regression refers to the tendency of a player under pressure to revert to high-frequency habits captured by the model. If a player’s season surface shows heavy corner-three intensity, an opponent may design a defensive scheme to remove that habit. The AFBART model, if used by a coach without updating, would still predict the habitual surface. The paper acknowledges that shot charts help coaches “design defensive schemes to disrupt opponents’ preferred areas” [PAGE 2]. But the model itself does not simulate the opponent’s counter-scheme. Thus the model can identify a tendency, but it cannot certify that the tendency will persist under adversarial adjustment.

The counter-trap layer is strategic. If the model’s variable importance is public or scouting-visible, opponents can target the covariates. For example, the paper finds that 3-point attempt rate is among the top variables influencing shooting pattern [PAGE 15]. If an opposing team knows that a player’s predicted surface is strongly governed by 3PAr, it can alter closeout behavior. The model does not include closeout behavior, so its prediction may become stale. A rational adversary will try to push the player toward lower-predicted regions or force the player away from high-intensity regions. The model’s posterior uncertainty should widen when such strategic adjustment is suspected, but the source does not provide a dynamic strategic prior.

The clock layer is missing. The paper’s future-work section explicitly says that “extending AFBART to time series of functional data would allow for analyzing temporal dynamics in shooting patterns, enabling trend identification and change-point detection” [PAGE 19]. This confirms that the current model is not a clock-aware model. It cannot distinguish a player’s early-game distribution from a late-game distribution, nor can it detect whether a player becomes more three-point-heavy in the fourth quarter or after a substitution pattern. Any inference about clutch behavior, late-clock shot selection, or fatigue-induced regression is outside the model’s current formal scope.

A formal recursive theory-of-mind veto can be stated as follows. Suppose an opponent’s strategy Z_t changes the conditional distribution of shot locations. If the AFBART model conditions only on X_i and ignores Z_t, then the model’s predictive distribution is  
\[
p_{\text{AFBART}}(\Lambda \mid X_i),
\]
whereas the true strategic distribution is  
\[
p_{\text{true}}(\Lambda \mid X_i, Z_t).
\]
If Z_t has a nonzero effect on Λ, then the model is misspecified for adversarial prediction. The remedy is not to invent a hidden ratio, but to include explicit state variables: defensive pressure, score, time, fatigue, matchup, or play type. The source notes that tracking data can contain such information [PAGE 1], but the presented AFBART application does not use it. Therefore the model should be treated as a descriptive habit estimator, not a real-time adversarial predictor.

The recursive audit also exposes a habit-versus-countermeasure loop. If a player’s model-predicted intensity surface is used to design offensive plays, the opponent may adapt. If the opponent adapts, the player’s realized surface changes. If the model is not updated, its prediction becomes biased. The paper’s static season-level design does not close this loop. The safest inference is: AFBART reveals stable historical tendencies; it does not prove that those tendencies remain optimal or unchanged under strategic counterplay.
</RECURSIVE_THEOry_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto question is whether physical, biomechanical, or weather constraints must override the statistical model. The source does not include explicit biomechanical covariates, health status, injury risk, court condition, or weather. Its covariates are “24 summary statistics” such as player position, age, team affiliation, minutes played, player efficiency rating, and field goal attempt rate [PAGE 4]. Therefore any physical, biomechanical, or weather veto must come from an external layer, not from the AFBART likelihood itself.

A formal veto proof can be stated using a latent true intensity process. Let Λ_i(s) be the actual shot-attempt intensity for player i at location s. Let X_i be the observed AFBART covariate vector. Let Z_i be an external physical or biomechanical constraint, such as injury, restricted mobility, fatigue, court hazard, or environmental condition. The AFBART model estimates a function of X_i only:  
\[
\hat{\Xi}_{x_i}(s) \approx E[\log \Lambda_i(s) \mid X_i = x_i].
\]
If Z_i deterministically forces Λ_i(s) = 0 on some region, while the model assumes a log-normal or Gaussian log-intensity with positive support, then the model assigns no probability to the exact event Λ_i(s) = 0. Formally, if the model implies  
\[
P(\Lambda_i(s) > 0 \mid X_i) = 1,
\]
but the physical layer implies  
\[
P(\Lambda_i(s) = 0 \mid Z_i) = 1,
\]
then the statistical layer is incompatible with the physical layer in that regime. The veto is not a matter of preference; it is a contradiction between model support and physical reality.

A second veto proof follows from omitted-variable bias. Suppose the true log-intensity is  
\[
\log \Lambda_i(s) = \Xi_{x_i}(s) + \beta Z_i(s) + \varepsilon_i(s).
\]
If Z_i is omitted and correlated with X_i, then the estimated coefficient functions or tree partitions can absorb Z_i’s effect into X_i’s apparent effect. The model may then attribute a physical restriction to a player covariate. For example, reduced mobility could reduce paint intensity, but if mobility is not measured, the model might incorrectly attribute the reduction to age, position, or usage. The veto is: do not use the model for physical readiness or biomechanical restriction unless Z_i is measured and included.

A third veto proof concerns weather. The source does not model weather. If a shot-location model were applied to an outdoor context, or to a context where travel, altitude, humidity, or court condition affects performance, the model’s conditional expectation is not identified for those factors. In an indoor NBA setting, weather may be less direct, but travel fatigue and schedule context still matter. The paper does not include schedule density or rest days among the highlighted covariates, although minutes played and games played are included [PAGE 5], [PAGE 15]. Therefore weather or environmental constraints must be treated as external vetoes, not as model-derived signals.

The only source-internal veto is the sample-support veto. The paper excludes players who began their careers in 2017–2018 and focuses on players with more than 400 FGA [PAGE 4]. Therefore the model should veto predictions for rookies or low-volume players unless the analyst explicitly extends the model and validates it. This is a statistical support veto, not a physical one.

The adjudication is: AFBART can be used to describe historical spatial shot tendency for established players in the 2017–2018 dataset. It cannot override physical, biomechanical, or environmental constraints. If such constraints are known, they must veto or adjust the statistical prediction. If they are unknown, the model’s uncertainty is understated because the external layer is unmodeled.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following three RLVR-style counterfactual veto scenarios are verifiable without requiring wagering lines, outcome tables, or prohibited market quantities. Each scenario provides a veto condition and a prediction.

Scenario 1: Rookie or low-volume counterfactual.  
Veto condition: The player is a rookie or attempted fewer than 400 field goals in the relevant season. The source states that the authors “focused on players who attempted more than 400 field goals” and “excluded players who began their careers in the 2017–2018 season” [PAGE 4].  
Formal prediction: AFBART trained on the 191-player sample should not be used as a validated estimator for such players. If forced to predict, the covariate vector lies outside the training support, and posterior uncertainty should be treated as unreliable. Verification: check FGA count, rookie status, and covariate overlap. If the player’s 3PAr, minutes, position, or efficiency metrics are outside the training range, the veto is triggered.

Scenario 2: Temporal or strategic covariate-shift counterfactual.  
Veto condition: The model trained on the 2017–2018 regular season is applied to a later period where the distribution of shot selection has changed, especially through increased three-point attempt rates or role changes. The paper identifies the “three-point revolution” as a major shift and notes that “the league has seen a dramatic rise in three-point attempts per game” [PAGE 15].  
Formal prediction: If the test-period covariate distribution has materially more high 3PAr players or different positional roles than the 2017–2018 training sample, the model’s posterior mean intensity surfaces may be biased for players in the shifted region. The model’s tree partitions were induced by the old covariate distribution, so extrapolation beyond those partitions is not guaranteed. Verification: compare covariate distributions across seasons; check whether the target season’s 3PAr, position mix, and minutes distribution fall inside the training hull. If not, veto or require retraining.

Scenario 3: Zero-intensity log degeneracy counterfactual.  
Veto condition: The input intensity surface contains exact zeros in regions where the analyst requires log-intensity predictions. The model uses “Zi(·) = log(λi(·))” [PAGE 6].  
Formal prediction: If λ_i(s) = 0, the log transform is undefined. If the LGCP smoothing produces strictly positive latent intensities, the model may still operate, but the observed zero indicates no local support. Predictions in that region are driven by smoothing, prior, or basis extrapolation, not by local observed attempts. Verification: inspect the discretized intensity matrix for exact zeros; check the lower intensity legend in figures, where intensity bins begin near zero, for example “(0.00, 0.01]” [PAGE 6]. If zeros are present and no offset or positive latent intensity is supplied, veto the use of naive log predictions in those regions.

These three vetoes are verifiable because they rely on data checks: FGA threshold and rookie status, covariate distribution shift, and exact zero intensities. They do not require forecasting contest outcomes, and they do not rely on market quantities. They are model-validation vetoes: if any condition fails, the appropriate reward is to withhold the prediction, widen uncertainty, or rebuild the model with additional data.
