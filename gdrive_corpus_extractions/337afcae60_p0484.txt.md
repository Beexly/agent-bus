The index itself preserves the tension: Watson's circular maze [PAGE 1, p63] represents the behaviorist tradition of measuring habit strength through error counts, whereas Köhler's "Umteeg" set-ups [PAGE 1, pp219-232] (almost certainly Köhler's "Umweg" or detour problems) represent the Gestalt/insight tradition. Tolman's synthesis, indexed under "Purposive Behavior," treats the animal as an organism that forms expectancies about means-end relations rather than merely stamping in S-R bonds.

The genealogical line continues forward through Blodgett's T-maze work [PAGE 1, p48, Blodgett 7-unit T-maze], which underpinned latent-learning arguments: rats exposed to a maze without reward later perform as well as rewarded rats when reward is introduced, implying that a cognitive representation was built during non-rewarded exploration. This anticipates O'Keefe and Dostrovsky's place cells (1971) and the Morris water maze (1984), both of which retrospectively validate Tolman's claim that rats represent spatial topology. The Yoshioka mazes [PAGE 1, pp123,125; diamond-shaped orientation; equal-path; long-path short-path mushroom maze] prefigure modern manipulations of path geometry and viewpoint invariance in hippocampal research.

Multi-week traps: In longitudinal testing, an animal's performance in week 3 is not a pure function of week-3 cognition. Proactive interference from week-1 maze configurations can produce retroactive interference when the maze is altered; extinction of a reinforced path suppresses but does not erase the cognitive map, yielding renewal when context changes. A persistent trap is concluding "no learning" from poor late-session performance when the animal may be exhibiting latent inhibition, context-dependent retrieval failure, or simple satiation. Additionally, Richter's activity cage data [PAGE 1, p32] index circadian locomotor rhythms; if trials are run at inconsistent circadian phases across weeks, motivation and arousal drift independently of any learning curve, producing false plateaus or false gains. The retrocausal lesson is that modern place-cell findings read back into Tolman a representationalism he was gesturing toward, but the index alone cannot confirm the neural mechanism; it confirms only that the experimental apparatuses were built to discriminate between habit and purposive accounts.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression occurs when an animal under stress (e.g., blocked goal path in the Tolman and Honzik insight maze [PAGE 1, p167], or insoluble choice in Hamilton's box [PAGE 1, p172f.]) reverts to an overlearned S-R habit rather than flexibly deploying a cognitive map. In Hullian terms, the habit hierarchy's strongest habit wins under arousal. The counter-trap is the experimenter's interpretation: observing regression, the experimenter may conclude the animal "never had" a map or "lost" insight. That is a misattribution. The map may be intact but inaccessible under high anxiety; state-dependent retrieval failure mimics cognitive deficit.

The clock operates on two layers. The experimental clock—inter-trial interval, session start time, trial number—interacts with the biological circadian clock indexed by Richter's activity cage [PAGE 1, p32]. If an animal is tested at 09:00 on Monday and 16:00 on Wednesday, performance differences may reflect circadian phase shifts in motivation, temperature, or olfactory acuity rather than learning. A recursive theory of mind enters because the animal is also modeling the experimenter: it learns the contingency schedule (when reward appears, when the maze is reset). The experimenter, in turn, models the animal's learning curve. When the animal violates the experimenter's model—an Aha-Erlebnis [PAGE 1, pp198,216]—the experimenter may misattribute the sudden insight to random error or "latent" maturation rather than to the animal's detection of an expectancy violation.

The recursive loop is: stress → habit regression → experimenter infers cognitive absence → experimenter changes contingency → animal detects contingency shift → increased stress → deeper regression. The counter-trap requires yoked controls (same stimuli, no contingent reward), counterbalanced circadian scheduling, and probe trials that dissociate performance from competence. Only when regression is shown to be context- and state-dependent rather than representational can the crisis be adjudicated as a retrieval failure rather than a loss of knowledge.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto (morphological and topological). The index lists many mazes [PAGE 1] but provides no dimensions. For a laboratory rat (body length ≈ 0.20 m, shoulder width ≈ 0.06 m), any alley width w < 0.06 m constitutes a physical veto: the animal cannot traverse the apparatus, so choice behavior cannot be expressed. Similarly, if total path length L is shorter than one stride, the maze cannot support a discrimination among routes. Without dimensional data from the source pages referenced in the index, all spatial interpretations remain under a physical veto unless independent measurements confirm that the apparatus fits the subject.

Biomechanical veto (fatigue and motor execution). Repeated trials in problem-boxes [PAGE 1, e.g., Adams lever boxes p99; Gengerelli least-action box p104] induce fatigue. If trial duration T_trial exceeds the fatigue time constant τ_fatigue (seconds), or if the inter-trial interval ITI is shorter than the recovery time, running speed and response vigor decline independently of cognition. The veto condition is: T_trial > τ_fatigue OR ITI < recovery_time ⇒ performance decline is biomechanical, not cognitive. Verification requires measuring trial-by-trial latency or velocity; a monotonic velocity decay invalidates any learning curve derived from error rates alone.

Weather/environmental veto (sensory masking). Although the apparatuses are indoor, discrimination-boxes [PAGE 1, e.g., Sams and Tolman time discrimination-box p107; De Camp space discrimination-box p102; Robinson and Wever visual distance discrimination-box p128] depend on sensory cues that are vulnerable to ambient temperature, humidity, airflow, and olfactory contamination. For Rattus norvegicus, the thermoneutral zone is roughly 26–30 °C; outside this zone, metabolic stress alters motivation. Cleaning-solvent residues, lingering odor trails from prior subjects, or barometric-pressure changes can mask or enhance the very cues the task requires. The veto condition is: if performance collapses under a change in temperature, humidity, or odor-control protocol, the sensory layer has failed and no cognitive conclusion is licensed.

Adjudication order. A result may be attributed to purposive/cognitive processing only if it survives, in sequence: (1) physical veto (apparatus fits subject), (2) biomechanical veto (no fatigue confound), (3) environmental veto (sensory cues stable across conditions), and only then (4) cognitive interpretation. If any layer vetoes, the result is assigned to that lower layer and the higher-layer interpretation is suspended.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios. RLVR here denotes an intervention whose reward structure and measurement are operationally defined so the veto is pass/fail.

Scenario 1 — Time-discrimination jitter (Sams and Tolman time discrimination-box [PAGE 1, p107]). Intervention: train the animal on a fixed sample-test interval t*, then probe with intervals t* ± 50% in a jittered sequence. Observable: choice accuracy as a function of probe interval. Prediction under an interval-timing account: accuracy declines smoothly with probe deviation, consistent with scalar variability (constant Weber fraction). Veto condition: if accuracy drops to chance immediately for any probe t ≠ t*, the animal is not using an internal clock but is relying on sequential non-temporal cues; the timing-based purposive account is vetoed.

Scenario 2 — Insight-maze path blockage (Tolman and Honzik "insight" maze [PAGE 1, p167]). Intervention: after overlearning the reinforced path P1, block P1 and open a previously unrewarded shortcut P2. Observable: latency to first traverse P2. Prediction under a cognitive-map/latent-learning account: P2 is used within 1–2 trials, indicating the animal represented the maze topology during non-rewarded exposure. Veto condition: if the animal persists on the blocked path or searches at chance for more than 5 trials, the cognitive-map interpretation is vetoed in favor of a pure S-R habit account.

Scenario 3 — Least-action cost manipulation (Gengerelli "least action" box [PAGE 1, p104]). Intervention: independently vary physical cost (e.g., incline angle θ or door mass m) while holding reward magnitude constant. Observable: path-choice probability as a function of work W = mgh + friction (joules). Prediction under a least-action account: choice probability for the short/high-cost path decreases monotonically as W increases across a physically discriminable range. Veto condition: if choice probability is invariant to θ across that range, the least-action/economy principle is vetoed; behavior is driven by non-energetic cues (visual, olfactory, or idiosyncratic) rather than work minimization.
Each scenario is verifiable because the independent variable is physically manipulable, the dependent variable is a binary or continuous choice measure, and the veto condition is a falsifiable inequality (immediate chance drop, >5-trial persistence, or invariance to cost).
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
