The scientific lineage of this source runs through four historical layers.

First, classical single-camera tracking. [PAGE 2] says generative models include “Kalman filtering and mean-shift” and discriminative models include “Struck” and “TLD.” These establish the basic idea that tracking is either similarity search against a template or classification against background. The paper’s re-detection threshold also has an older signal-processing ancestor: “Inspired by the peak to sidelobe ration in MOSSE” [PAGE 6]. MOSSE is a correlation-filter lineage that uses response-map peaks and sidelobe statistics to detect target presence. ASNet inherits this by converting response-map peaks into tracking scores and failure triggers.

Second, deep siamese and correlation-filter tracking. [PAGE 2] traces deep trackers through “MDNet,” “Siam-FC,” “SiamRPN,” “DaSiamRPN,” “SiamRPN++,” and “C-RPN.” It also traces discriminative correlation filters: “Discriminative correlation filter (DCF) can be learned very efficiently in the frequency domain via fast Fourier transform” [PAGE 2]. ASNet’s base tracker, DSiam, belongs to this siamese/online transformation lineage. [PAGE 5] states: “Dynamic siamese network (DSiam) can enable effective online learning of target appearance variation and background suppression from previous frames.” Thus ASNet is not a new detector from scratch; it is a multi-agent wrapper around a single-camera online tracker.

Third, multi-camera tracking. [PAGE 2] says multi-camera tracking uses “information of different views by estimating a common axis or subspace, or fusing multi-view information.” However, most prior multi-camera work targeted multi-object tracking or person re-identification, often with static cameras. [PAGE 2] states: “As multiple cameras are static, the spatial relations between cameras are either explicitly mapped in 3D, learned by tracking known identities, or obtained by comparing entry/exit rates across pairs of cameras.” MDOT changes this by using moving drones, so the inter-view relation is not a fixed calibration. This creates a multi-week trap: methods that rely on static camera geometry cannot be directly transferred.

Fourth, drone dataset lineage. [PAGE 1] lists prior drone datasets: “UAV123,” “Campus,” “VisDrone-2018,” and “UAVDT.” [PAGE 3] adds detail: “UAV123 dataset contains a total of 123 video sequences and more than 110K frames,” while “UAVDT dataset consists of 80,000 representative frames.” MDOT distinguishes itself by using multiple drones observing the same target: “MDOT consists of 92 groups of video clips with 113,918 high resolution frames taken by two drones and 63 groups of video clips with 145,875 high resolution frames taken by three drones” [PAGE 1]. The historical trap is that single-drone datasets reward single-view robustness, while multi-drone datasets reward cross-view evidence management.

Multi-week traps embedded in the source’s construction include the following.

Temporal collection split. “Two-MDOT was collected in 2018 while Three-MDOT was collected in 2019. Hence, there is no overlap between Two-MDOT and Three-MDOT” [PAGE 4]. This prevents leakage across subsets but also means the two subsets may differ in scene distribution, target categories, or drone operating conditions.

Train-test split. “the dataset is divided into train set (37 groups in Two-MDOT and 28 groups of in Three-MDOT) and the test set (55 groups in Two-MDOT and 35 groups of in Three-MDOT)” [PAGE 4]. A model that memorizes group-level scene regularities can overfit if group identity is not respected during training.

Attribute ambiguity. The attribute table defines “Partial Occlusion,” “Full Occlusion,” “Out Of View,” “Similar Object,” “Viewpoint Change,” “Illumination Variation,” and “Low Resolution” [PAGE 5]. These labels are frame-level or sequence-level? The paper says “all the sequences are labeled by 10 attributes” [PAGE 4], but some attributes such as occlusion and out-of-view can vary frame by frame. A rigorous evaluation must distinguish sequence-level annotation from frame-level evidence.

Synchrony assumption. “we assume that videos of multiple drones are synchronous by starting tracking across drones simultaneously” [PAGE 8]. In deployment, drone clocks, frame exposure, and communication latency may break this assumption. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The system can be modeled as multiple tracking agents with recursive beliefs about target location and about each other’s reliability.

Each drone agent k maintains:
- a template O_1^k or shared template O_1^v,
- a search region Z_t^k,
- a response map S_t^k,
- a peak score g(s_t^k),
- a location p_t^k.

The target has latent state T_t: location, scale, appearance, and visibility. Each drone’s observation is a function of target state, drone pose, altitude, illumination, and occlusion. The recursive belief structure is:
\[
B_t^k = P(T_t \mid Z_t^k, O^k, \text{shared templates}, \text{past scores}).
\]
The paper does not write a probabilistic belief equation, but its operators implement an implicit belief update: templates encode appearance belief, search regions encode spatial belief, response maps encode evidence, and tracking scores encode confidence.

Crisis habit regression occurs when a drone agent encounters a severe distribution shift. The paper identifies hard attributes: “the performances on attributes CM, FOC, VC and IV are inferior than that on other attributes” [PAGE 7]. CM is camera motion, FOC is full occlusion, VC is viewpoint change, IV is illumination variation. Under these crises, the base tracker’s habit is to rely on the most recent template and local search. If the target appearance changes faster than the template adapts, the response peak falls. The re-detection threshold detects this crisis: “the target may be lost when the score is less than ωq t or the tracking score is the threshold Tscore” [PAGE 6]. If no counter-trap is active, the agent regresses to DSiam-like behavior and drifts.

Counter-trap is the multi-agent correction mechanism. There are three layers.

Layer 1: re-detection. The agent expands the search region when score confidence drops. The paper says: “we use the local-to-global strategy to expand the search region step by step to re-detect the target” [PAGE 6]. In recursive terms, the agent lowers its spatial prior certainty and searches a broader region. This prevents premature loss but increases false-positive risk.

Layer 2: template sharing. One drone’s appearance evidence can support another drone. The response map in Equation 5 uses drone v’s template with drone k’s search region [PAGE 6]. This is a theory-of-mind operation: drone k assumes that drone v may have seen the target more clearly. The self-supervised weights in Equation 6 estimate which prior tracked targets from other templates are most predictive. The paper says the weights “reﬂect the correlation between tracked target and target template of the (t −1)-th frame” [PAGE 6]. If drone v’s previous evidence was reliable, the weight increases; if it was corrupted, the regularized least-squares objective should reduce its influence, provided the regularization and previous-frame quality are adequate.

Layer 3: view-aware fusion. The system compares response-map peaks across drones and selects the best one: “we search the maximum value g(sk t) in the response map” and “the index of the best response map bt” is selected [PAGE 6]. This is a meta-agent adjudicator. It does not model full joint uncertainty; it uses a scalar confidence proxy. This is efficient but fragile if scores are not calibrated. A high peak can occur on a distractor, especially under “Similar Object” conditions [PAGE 5].

Clock is the temporal control layer. The paper’s discussion is explicit: “we assume that videos of multiple drones are synchronous by starting tracking across drones simultaneously” [PAGE 8]. The clock determines whether template sharing is valid. If template sharing is delayed, the shared evidence may refer to a past target state. The paper acknowledges: “the only component of ASNet, i.e., template Sharing, is affected by communication latency” [PAGE 8]. Therefore, ASNet has a clock-dependent decomposition:
\[
\text{ASNet}_{\text{full}}
=
\text{re-detection}
+
\text{template sharing}
+
\text{view-aware fusion}.
\]
If latency is high, the counter-trap should degrade to:
\[
\text{ASNet}_{\text{no TS}}
=
\text{re-detection}
+
\text{view-aware fusion}.
\]
The ablation supports this fallback: “ASNet w/o TS” achieves success 47.6 and precision 73.4 on Two-MDOT, and success 52.7 and precision 76.6 on Three-MDOT, while full ASNet achieves 48.2/74.3 and 53.3/78.2 [PAGE 9]. The gap between full ASNet and ASNet without template sharing quantifies the value of template sharing under the paper’s synchronous assumption.

Recursive failure mode: if previous tracked targets are wrong, the self-supervised weights can reinforce the wrong target. This is a closed-loop belief collapse. The paper’s regularization terms λ_m, λ_w, and λ_u are intended to stabilize the loop, but they do not guarantee correctness. The ablation shows that re-detection and view-aware fusion are stronger contributors than template sharing alone: “if we only apply the view-aware fusion in the DSiam tracker, the performance is greatly improved” [PAGE 8], and “Finally, we add the re-detection module, template sharing design and view-aware fusion scheme to the DSiam tracker (ASNet). Thus we can observe considerable improvement in precision score (14.9/14.1) and success score (10.6/10.4)” [PAGE 8].

Thus the recursive theory-of-mind control law is: when crisis score falls, expand search; when cross-view evidence is reliable and fresh, share templates; when multiple drones disagree, select the highest response peak; when latency or desynchronization is high, disable template sharing and rely on local re-detection plus view selection.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer veto adjudication tests whether algorithmic claims survive physical, biomechanical, and environmental constraints.

Physical layer veto 1: visible-light sensing only.
The dataset uses “DJI PHANTOM 4Pro drones” [PAGE 4] and does not collect lidar: “we do not collect the lidar data because several drones with lidar are much expensive compared with visible light camera equipped drones and the accurate sensing distance of lidar is about 200m” [PAGE 4]. Therefore, any claim that ASNet provides lidar-like depth robustness is vetoed. The system can fuse appearance evidence, but it cannot recover depth or shape information absent from visible-light frames.

Physical layer veto 2: altitude and pixel resolution.
The drones operate “from different view-angles and different altitudes, ranging from 20m to 100m” [PAGE 4]. Image resolution is “1280×720” [PAGE 4]. The low-resolution attribute defines tiny targets as “pixels are less than 400” [PAGE 5]. If a target occupies fewer than 400 pixels in a drone view, the available visual evidence is severely compressed. Formal veto: let A_t^v be the target pixel area in drone v. If A_t^v < 400 for all v, the attribute label indicates low resolution. Appearance variation transformations and correlation filters may still operate, but the information ceiling is lower. Fusion cannot create spatial detail that was never sampled.

Physical layer veto 3: full occlusion and out-of-view.
The attributes include “Full Occlusion” and “Out Of View” [PAGE 5]. If the target is fully occluded in all drone views at frame t, the observation likelihood is effectively null. In score terms:
\[
s(h_t^v, y_t^v) = 0
\quad \text{or undefined for all } v,
\]
so:
\[
\mathrm{IFS}_t = 0
\]
under a zero convention. No fusion policy can exceed this ceiling. If the target is out of view in all drones, the same veto applies. If at least one drone sees the target, IFS can be positive, and view-aware fusion becomes meaningful.

Physical layer veto 4: latency and synchrony.
The paper assumes synchronous videos: “we assume that videos of multiple drones are synchronous by starting tracking across drones simultaneously” [PAGE 8]. It also states: “the only component of ASNet, i.e., template Sharing, is affected by communication latency” [PAGE 8]. Let τ be template-sharing latency and Δt be the inter-frame interval. If τ > Δt, shared templates refer to an older target state. Under rapid camera motion or viewpoint change, the temporal smoothness assumption behind Equation 3 is violated. Veto: any claim that full ASNet is superior under arbitrary latency is false. The verifiable fallback is ASNet without template sharing, which still benefits from re-detection and view-aware fusion [PAGE 9].

Biomechanical layer veto: target category and motion.
The target categories include “pedestrian, car, carriage, motor, bicycle, tricycle, truck, dog, bus” [PAGE 4]. These have different motion dynamics and appearance variation. A pedestrian can change pose, a car can change viewpoint, a dog can be occluded by low obstacles. A tracker tuned for one category may not generalize across all. Veto: any claim of category-invariant robustness must be tested per category or per attribute. The paper reports attribute-based performance, showing weaker performance on “CM, FOC, VC and IV” [PAGE 7]. This supports a category- and condition-specific veto.

Weather and illumination veto.
The dataset includes “Daytime,” “Night,” and “Illumination Variation” [PAGE 5]. Night sequences reduce visible-light contrast. Illumination variation changes appearance within a sequence. If the target region is underexposed or saturated, correlation features become unreliable. Veto: ASNet’s gains are not guaranteed under all illumination conditions; the source’s own attribute analysis shows illumination variation is hard [PAGE 7].

Scoring layer veto: AFS cannot exceed IFS under valid one-hot selection.
The paper defines IFS as an upper bound: “IFS is the theoretically optimal fusion performance (upper bound) of a multi-drone tracker” [PAGE 2]. ASNet uses one-hot selection: “The weight wv of the drone tracker with the best response map obtained from Eq. 8 is set as one and the rest as zero” [PAGE 6]. Therefore, for each frame:
\[
\mathrm{AFS}_t \le \mathrm{IFS}_t.
\]
If an implementation reports AFS > IFS on the same data, one of the following must be true: the weights are not one-hot; the score function is not bounded; the frame sets differ; or the oracle is constrained differently. This is a formal veto against metric gaming.

Ablation layer veto: template sharing is not the dominant component under all conditions.
The ablation shows full ASNet improves over DSiam by “precision score (14.9/14.1) and success score (10.6/10.4)” [PAGE 8]. However, view-aware fusion alone already improves performance: “if we only apply the view-aware fusion in the DSiam tracker, the performance is greatly improved” [PAGE 8]. Template sharing alone gives smaller improvements: “precision score (0.9/1.3) and success score (0.6/0.6)” [PAGE 8]. Therefore, any claim that template sharing alone is the primary source of gain is vetoed by the paper’s own ablation. The dominant gain comes from the combination of re-detection and view-aware fusion, with template sharing adding an increment under favorable synchrony.

Final cross-layer judgment: ASNet is admissible as a multi-drone tracking baseline under visible-light, synchronous, moderate-latency conditions with at least one informative view. It is inadmissible as a universal solution for all-weather, low-resolution, fully occluded, out-of-view, high-latency, or asynchronous drone networks. The IFS upper bound remains the hard ceiling, and the AFS-IFS gap measures the remaining fusion deficit.

RLVR-style verification requires that any learned policy be checked against verifiable counterfactual constraints. For this source, verifiable rewards can be recomputed from logs using the paper’s own operators:
\[
R_{\mathrm{AFS}}
=
\frac{1}{n}
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\sum_{v=1}^{V}
w_{i,v}
s(h_i^v, y_i^v),
\]
and:
\[
R_{\mathrm{IFS}}
=
\frac{1}{n}
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\max_v s(h_i^v, y_i^v).
\]
The following three counterfactual veto scenarios are verifiable.

Veto scenario 1: latency veto on template sharing.
Assumptions: template sharing requires transmission of template features or response evidence across drones. Let τ be the communication delay and Δt the frame interval. If τ is large enough that shared templates refer to a previous target state, the benefit of template sharing should vanish or reverse.
Counterfactual test: replay MDOT with artificial delay τ applied only to template-sharing inputs. Compare full ASNet against ASNet without template sharing, which corresponds to ablation row (7) on [PAGE 9].
Verifiable prediction: for sufficiently large τ, the reward difference
\[
\Delta R_{\mathrm{TS}}(\tau)
=
R_{\mathrm{ASNet}}(\tau)
-
R_{\mathrm{ASNet\ w/o\ TS}}
\]
should approach zero or become negative. Under the paper’s synchronous assumption, full ASNet is better: row (7) ASNet w/o TS has 47.6/52.7 success and 73.4/76.6 precision, while row (8) ASNet has 48.2/53.3 success and 74.3/78.2 precision [PAGE 9]. The veto is verified if delayed template sharing removes that advantage.
Why it is a veto: it prevents an RLVR reward model from crediting template sharing when the clock violates the paper’s synchrony assumption [PAGE 8].

Veto scenario 2: single-view dominance veto.
Assumptions: view-aware fusion selects the maximum response-map peak:
\[
b_t = \arg\max_v g(s_t^v).
\]
If one drone’s response map is consistently dominant and correct, the multi-drone system reduces to that single drone plus re-detection.
Counterfactual test: compute the fraction of test frames where b_t is the same drone. If b_t is nearly constant and the selected drone’s score matches the oracle score, then template sharing and cross-view fusion provide little additional evidence.
Verifiable prediction: when the selected drone is dominant,
\[
\mathrm{AFS} \approx \mathrm{IFS},
\]
and the marginal gain from template sharing approaches zero. In ablation terms, ASNet w/o TS and full ASNet should be nearly equal on that subset. The paper’s overall ablation still shows a positive gain from template sharing [PAGE 9], so this veto applies to subsets where one view dominates, not to the full benchmark.
Why it is a veto: it prevents an RLVR reward model from assuming that multi-drone fusion always helps when the environment is effectively single-view.

Veto scenario 3: metric-constraint veto on AFS weights.
Assumptions: the paper says, “The value of wv should be zero or one” [PAGE 4], and ASNet sets one weight to one and the rest to zero [PAGE 6]. If an implementation allows multiple active weights without normalization, AFS may no longer be comparable to IFS or may exceed a bounded score scale.
Counterfactual test: audit the logged weights w_{i,v}. Verify whether:
\[
w_{i,v}\in\{0,1\}
\]
and:
\[
\sum_{v=1}^{V} w_{i,v} \in \{0,1\}.
\]
If the implementation instead uses arbitrary real weights, recompute AFS under the constrained one-hot rule and compare.
Verifiable prediction: if the original unconstrained AFS is greater than the constrained AFS, the original reward is invalid as a direct realization of the paper’s Definition 1. If constrained AFS remains greater than or equal to baseline DSiam but less than or equal to IFS, the reward is valid.
Why it is a veto: it protects the formal inequality
\[
\mathrm{AFS} \le \mathrm{IFS}
\]
under one-hot selection. Without this constraint, an RLVR reward could be gamed by counting multiple drone hypotheses as if they were a single normalized decision.

These three vetoes are verifiable because they rely only on logged scores, frame indices, drone indices, weights, response peaks, and optional latency controls. They do not require unobservable intentions. They enforce the source’s own formal operators and prevent reward hacking in a multi-agent tracking evaluation.
