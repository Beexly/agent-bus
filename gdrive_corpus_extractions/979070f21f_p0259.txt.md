Galton models Watson as a recursive reasoner: he states his own computation “became intolerably tedious after a few steps, and I had to abandon it,” then records that Watson “kindly, at my request, took the problem in hand” [PAGE 242]. This is a first-order theory of mind — Galton attributes to Watson both the willingness and the formal capacity to continue a calculation he himself abandoned. The recursion deepens because Galton stipulates that the fertility hypothesis is “to be determined by statistical investigations” [PAGE 242], i.e., he models the analyst as requiring empirical priors before any deductive step can proceed.

Crisis habit regression. Under the pressure of computational intractability (“intolerably tedious”), the natural habit is to regress to the nearest tractable special case: assume a low q, assume Poisson fertility, or assume \mu is comfortably above 1. Watson’s result (2) on [PAGE 242] — “a remarkably easy way of computing those special cases in which the law of fertility approximates to a certain specified form” — is precisely this regression. The danger is that the regressed habit persists after the crisis passes, leading an analyst to apply a closed-form special case to a problem whose actual p_k vector lies outside its domain of validity.

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is the inverse error: because Watson supplies “certain general results” but not a “general solution” [PAGE 242], an analyst may conclude that nothing can be said without full data, and thus refuse to compute \epsilon_r even when bounds on \mu suffice. The correct recursive stance is to compute what is identifiable (\epsilon_r from \Pi when p_k is known; the \mu \le 1 versus \mu > 1 dichotomy from the mean alone) while explicitly flagging the unidentified components (the full distribution for question 2 on [PAGE 242], temporal variation in p_k, immigration).

Clock. The generational index r functions as a discrete clock. Each tick composes \Pi once: \epsilon_r = \Pi^{\circ r}(0). Galton’s urgency (“computation became intolerably tedious after a few steps”) is the pressure of a finite clock — r is small enough that exact integer arithmetic is possible, but large enough that manual iteration explodes. In modern terms, the clock is the number of branching iterations before numerical underflow, floating-point error, or model non-stationarity invalidates the recursion. The critical insight is that the clock is not continuous: extinction is checked at integer generational boundaries, so \epsilon_r is a step function in r, and interpolating between generations is meaningless.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer. The population consists of N discrete adult males [PAGE 242]; sons are integer counts. Fractional offspring are physically impossible, so any continuous approximation (diffusion, Gaussian fertility) is a layer violation unless justified by a large-N limit. The state space is \mathbb{N}_0 for each surname, with 0 an absorbing state.

Biomechanical layer. Fertility is bounded by physiology: no man can produce arbitrarily many adult sons. This justifies Galton’s finite q (“q being so large that it is not worth while to consider the chance of any man having more than q adult sons”) [PAGE 242]. The biomechanical veto is: if the empirical maximum observed adult-son count exceeds the model’s q, the PGF \Pi(s) is misspecified and any tail inference (large m in question 2 on [PAGE 242]) is undefined.

Weather / environmental layer. Fertility is not an i.i.d. draw from a fixed p_k. Shared weather shocks — famine, plague, severe winter — simultaneously raise p_0 and correlate the reproductive outcomes of brothers and cousins. This violates the independence assumption underlying \epsilon_r = \Pi(\epsilon_{r-1}) and destroys the Markov property of the surname count across generations. A single bad generation can drive \mu_r \le 1 even if the long-run mean exceeds 1, triggering extinction that the stationary model would not predict.

Veto adjudication proof. Define the extinction operator \mathcal{E}(\Pi, N, r) = \Pi^{\circ r}(0). A layer veto is active if any physical, biomechanical, or weather condition forces \mu \le 1 over a sustained window, or breaks independence.

Theorem (veto sufficiency). If there exists a generation interval [r_1, r_2] such that the environment-imposed mean \mu_r = \sum k p_k^{(r)} \le 1 for all r \in [r_1, r_2], with p_0^{(r)} > 0, then the conditional extinction probability over that window is 1, regardless of pre-window supercriticality. Proof: a subcritical or critical branching process with p_0 > 0 has extinction probability 1; composition with prior supercritical generations yields a finite population that either already extinct or enters the subcritical regime, after which absorption at zero is certain.

Consequences: (1) Galton’s “tend to disapper” [PAGE 242] is theorem-valid only under closed, stationary, independent, bounded-q assumptions. (2) A weather-induced famine that doubles p_0 for one generation vetoes the prediction of surname persistence. (3) A biomechanical ceiling q below the observed maximum vetoes all tail predictions for m > q. (4) Finite N = 10,000 [PAGE 242] imposes an absorbing boundary that vetoes any assumption of infinite-time survival for individual surnames, even when \mu > 1.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with explicit predictions. All probabilities are dimensionless; generations r are dimensionless integers.

Scenario 1 (subcritical hard veto). Counterfactual law: p_0 = 0.60, p_1 = 0.40, p_k = 0 for k \ge 2. Then \mu = 0.40 < 1. Veto: extinction is certain. Prediction: \epsilon_r = 1 - 0.40^r (derived from \Pi(s) = 0.60 + 0.40 s iterated from 0). After r = 5 generations, expected extinct proportion = 1 - 0.40^5 \approx 0.9898; limit as r \to \infty is exactly 1. Any model predicting persistent surnames under this law is falsified.

Scenario 2 (supercritical soft veto). Counterfactual law: p_0 = 0.20, p_2 = 0.80, all other p_k = 0. Then \mu = 1.60 > 1. Veto: extinction is not certain. Prediction: \epsilon is the smallest root of s = 0.20 + 0.80 s^2, i.e. 0.80 s^2 - s + 0.20 = 0, giving roots s = 1 and s = 0.25; hence \epsilon = 0.25 and long-run survival probability = 0.75. Expected surviving surnames among N = 10,000 [PAGE 242] = 7,500 in the infinite-time idealization; finite-N absorption reduces this over long horizons but not below the branching-process survival fraction in the short-to-medium run.

Scenario 3 (critical finite-N absorbing veto). Counterfactual law: p_0 = 0.10, p_1 = 0.80, p_2 = 0.10. Then \mu = 1.00 exactly. Veto: criticality implies \epsilon = 1 in the limit, but mean extinction time diverges with N. Prediction: for N = 10,000 [PAGE 242], \epsilon_r < 1 for finite r, so many surnames survive generation-by-generation, yet the absorbing state “zero holders” is reached with probability one as r \to \infty. Time scale is O(N) generations. Any policy or inference that treats \mu = 1 as “safe persistence” over multi-generational horizons is vetoed by finite-N absorption.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
