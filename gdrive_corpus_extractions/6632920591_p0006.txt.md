<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The threshold-sweep methodology in US 11,049,031 B2 descends from the Neyman–Pearson lemma (1933), which fixes a false-positive rate and maximizes true-positive detection via a likelihood-ratio threshold, and from ROC operating-point analysis formalized by Swets (1988). Sports injury prediction adds a domain-specific layer: the workload–injury literature (e.g., Bittencourt et al., 2016; Timpka et al.) models acute:chronic load ratios as risk drivers. The patent's table [PAGE 1] collapses this literature into a discrete grid scan (τ = 0.05 to 14), but it omits the temporal dimension that defines the multi-week trap.

A retrocausal lineage issue arises because the optimal threshold is selected using injury labels that occur in the future relative to the measurement window. If the same athlete cohort is used both to choose τ and to evaluate ACC=0.83 [PAGE 1], the result is look-ahead bias: the threshold "knows" which athletes eventually got injured. In a multi-week training cycle, load accumulates nonlinearly; a threshold optimal in week 1 may be dangerously miscalibrated by week 4 because fatigue, sleep debt, and match congestion shift the underlying injury base rate. The table provides no prevalence, no validation-set separation, and no temporal holdout [PAGE 1], so the 0.83 accuracy plateau may be an artifact of retrocausal overfitting to a single injury window rather than a generalizable operating point.

Another multi-week trap is the integer-threshold regime (2–14) [PAGE 1]. If τ represents a cumulative risk score updated weekly, the score distribution drifts as the season progresses. A static grid sweep cannot capture this drift; without weekly recalibration, the model will systematically over-predict risk early in the season (when loads are low) and under-predict it late in the season (when cumulative fatigue peaks). The retrocausal genealogy thus traces from classical decision theory through sports epidemiology into a patent implementation that, as shown on [PAGE 1], lacks explicit temporal cross-validation and prevalence adjustment.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression describes the tendency of coaching and medical staff, under acute injury pressure, to abandon model thresholds in favor of heuristics such as "rest any player with a tweak" or "play through minor soreness." The [PAGE 1] table enables this regression: because accuracy is flat at 0.83 across τ=2–12 [PAGE 1], staff have no model-based reason to prefer one threshold over another, so they revert to crisis-era habits that ignore the quantitative trade-off between FPR and FNR. At τ=0.05 the FPR=0.7 [PAGE 1] would trigger mass false alarms, provoking a crisis habit of overriding the system entirely; at τ=14 the FNR=0.6 [PAGE 1] would allow injured athletes to train, provoking the opposite crisis habit of blind trust after the first missed injury.

The counter-trap is adversarial or strategic: if opposing teams or internal stakeholders learn that the injury model uses τ=14 (conservative, low sensitivity), they may exploit the known blind spot by scheduling high-load sessions when the model is least likely to flag risk. Conversely, if τ=0.05 were known (high false alarms), opponents could anticipate unnecessary rotations and adjust their own tactical preparation. The theory of mind recursion is that the model's threshold choice is not a private parameter; it shapes the behavior of coaches, athletes, and opponents, which in turn changes the base rate of injuries and invalidates the original confusion matrix on [PAGE 1].

The clock imposes hard constraints: microcycle (3–7 days between matches), mesocycle (4–6 week loading blocks), and season clock (playoff push, fixture congestion). A threshold that produces an actionable flag must do so before the next training session. The [PAGE 1] table has no latency or lead-time variable, so it cannot answer whether a flag at τ=2 arrives early enough to modify this week's load, or whether τ=14's delayed sensitivity only captures injuries after they have already occurred. The clock therefore vetoes any threshold whose positive predictive value emerges only after the injury event.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The injury model output is a probability or score, not a medical clearance. Three independent veto layers can override any threshold decision on [PAGE 1].

Physical layer veto: An athlete with an acute structural lesion (e.g., grade II hamstring strain, joint effusion, stress reaction on imaging) is removed from play regardless of model score. This is a hard constraint; τ cannot trade off a false negative against a confirmed tissue lesion because the cost function is dominated by catastrophic re-injury risk.

Biomechanical layer veto: Asymmetrical force production exceeding 15% interlimb difference, reduced hop symmetry, or altered kinematic patterns during screening veto model clearance. These are direct observables of injury mechanism and supersede any probabilistic threshold from the table [PAGE 1].

Weather and environmental layer veto: High wet-bulb temperature, hard or uneven surfaces, and poor illumination independently elevate injury risk. If environmental risk crosses a local safety threshold, the model probability must be recalibrated upward or the session canceled, independent of τ.

Adjudication hierarchy: Physical veto > Biomechanical veto > Weather veto > Model threshold (τ). The [PAGE 1] accuracy figure of 0.83 is irrelevant once any veto layer fires, because the decision is no longer a statistical optimization but a safety constraint. The model operates only in the residual subpopulation that passes all veto layers.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
