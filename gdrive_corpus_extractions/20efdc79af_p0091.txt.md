$$Q(s,a) = \underbrace{\gamma \sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a) \max_{a'} Q(s',a')}_{\text{Spatial Dynamics}} + \underbrace{r(s,D_t)}_{\text{Drive-Specific Reward}}$$
where $r(s,D_t)$ is non-zero only at the goal node $s_g$ and depends on $D_t$.

**Day 10 Transition Shock:**
At $t=10$, $D_t$ switches from $Thirst$ to $Hunger$, and the physical reward changes from water to food at $s_g$. However, the value vector $V_{10}$ still encodes high value for water at $s_g$ based on previous experience. The new reward function $r(s,Hunger)$ is initially zero everywhere because the animal has not yet encountered food in this context.

The **Prediction Error** $\delta_t$ at the goal becomes:
$$\delta_{10}(s_g) = r(s_g, Hunger) - V_{10}(s_g) = 0 - V_{water}(s_g) < 0$$
This large negative TD error causes a temporary devaluation of the goal path, leading to increased exploration (errors) and latency as the animal searches for a now-absent water reward.

**Belief Update Rule (Day 11):**
After one successful foraging episode on Day 10 (finding food), the value vector updates via temporal difference learning:
$$V_{new}(s) \leftarrow V_{old}(s) + \alpha [\delta_t + \gamma \max_{a} V_{old}(s') - V_{old}(s)]$$
Crucially, the spatial transition matrix $P(s'|s,a)$ in $M$ does not need updating. Only the scalar value at $s_g$ needs to be rebound from "water value" to "food value". Because the map $M$ is already highly accurate, the re-planning after a single positive reinforcement event is extremely efficient, explaining the "as if nothing had happened" recovery.

**Units & Breakdown Regimes:**

- $\delta_t$: Prediction error (same units).
- Learning rate $\alpha$: Dimensionless, typically $0 < \alpha < 1$.
- **Singularity**: If $\alpha \to 0$, learning stalls; if $\alpha \to 1$, catastrophic forgetting of the old value may occur before the new one is consolidated. The observed smooth transition suggests an intermediate, biologically tuned $\alpha$.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman’s work stands in direct opposition to S-R (Stimulus-Response) behaviorism dominant in the 1930s. Watson and Skinner would explain the Day 10 spike as extinction of the "run-to-water" habit followed by acquisition of a "run-to-food" habit. Tolman argues instead for a *cognitive map* that exists prior to its use. This lineage connects directly to modern theories of episodic memory and mental time travel. The "old cognitive expectation" is a retrocausal trace: the past experience of water shapes present behavior even in the absence of water.

Historically, this experiment paved the way for O’Keefe and Nadel’s place cell theory. The stability of the map $M$ across drive states mirrors the stability of place fields across motivational contexts. The multi-week trap in interpreting such data is assuming that any improvement in performance equals learning of the path. Tolman showed that learning can be latent—acquired but not expressed until motivationally relevant. This challenges simple reinforcement accounts and forces us to consider internal models.

From a genealogical perspective, the concept of a "cognitive expectation" prefigures predictive coding and active inference frameworks. The brain is hypothesized to minimize free energy by updating beliefs to match sensory inputs. The Day 10 shock is a massive violation of the brain's generative model, triggering rapid plasticity. This links Tolman’s behavioral observations to contemporary neurobiology: dopamine neurons signal reward prediction errors similar to our $\delta_t$, and hippocampal sharp-wave ripples replay sequences during sleep, consolidating the updated value map between Day 10 and 11.
