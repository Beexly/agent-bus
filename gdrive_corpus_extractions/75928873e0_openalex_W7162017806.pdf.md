The effect-size operator is the standardized mean difference between treatment and control posttest means. Let \bar{X}_T and \bar{X}_C denote the first posttest means for treatment and control groups, S_T^2 and S_C^2 their variances, and n_T, n_C their sample sizes. The pooled standard deviation is
\[ S_{\text{pooled}} = \sqrt{\frac{(n_T-1)S_T^2 + (n_C-1)S_C^2}{n_T + n_C - 2}}, \]
and Cohen's d is
\[ d = \frac{\bar{X}_T - \bar{X}_C}{S_{\text{pooled}}}. \]
Cohen's d is dimensionless, expressed in pooled-standard-deviation units [PAGE 28]. Each effect is weighted by inverse variance,
\[ w_i = \frac{1}{\operatorname{Var}(d_i)}, \qquad \bar{d} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i d_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i}, \]
under a random-effects model chosen for generalizability beyond the collected studies [PAGE 28]. Heterogeneity is assessed with Cochran's Q,
\[ Q = \sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i (d_i - \bar{d})^2, \]
which under the null of homogeneity follows approximately a chi-square distribution with df = k − 1; and the I² index,
\[ I^2 = \frac{Q - df}{Q} \times 100\%, \]
bounded between 0% and 100%, quantifying the percentage of total variation due to true between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error [PAGE 28]. Confidence intervals are reported in Cohen's d units, and p-values are dimensionless probabilities [PAGE 47–49].

The conceptual lineage of the construct begins with Titchener's 1909 translation of the German "Einfühlung" into English "empathy," originally denoting an aesthetic "feeling into" objects [PAGE 14]. Before 1925, empathy resided largely in aesthetic and imaginative discourse rather than ethical or developmental psychology [PAGE 15]. The modern psychological architecture emerged with Rogers's 1957 framework specifying affective, cognitive, and communicative dimensions, later refined by Davis's 1983 multidimensional model separating emotional sharing/ mirroring from cognitive perspective-taking [PAGE 15]. Baston's 2009 formulation enumerated eight related but distinct empathy phenomena, laying groundwork for the thesis's decision to bundle empathy, sympathy, and compassion as "feeling with/for others" [PAGE 15, 17]. The philosophical critique culminates in Bloom's 2016 argument that compassion ("feeling for") should supplant emotional empathy as a moral motivation, a position the thesis echoes when noting that sympathy and compassion are "feeling for" targets while empathy is "feeling with" [PAGE 16–17].

On the media-effects side, the Differential Susceptibility to Media Effects Model (DSMM) by Valkenburg and Peter (2013) integrates Social Cognitive Theory, the Elaboration Likelihood Model, the General Aggression Model, Uses & Gratifications, and others into a single process model [PAGE 18–19]. DSMM specifies three user-difference classes (dispositional, developmental, social), three response-state classes (cognitive, emotional, excitative), and four propositions, the fourth being transactional feedback [PAGE 19]. The thesis positions itself as a test and enrichment of DSMM, using video-game content features as predictors of empathy response states [PAGE 23].

Historical traps appear at multiple temporal scales. Second, the catharsis hypothesis from 1971—violent games reduce real-life aggression—was contradicted by Anderson and Bushman's 2001 meta-analysis, establishing a contentious precedent that the thesis inherits when it finds violent games have only a non-significant negative trend on empathy [PAGE 12, 49]. Third, trait-state conversion timescales are unobserved: the meta-analysis uses first posttest data only [PAGE 28], so it cannot capture the weeks-to-months consolidation required for state empathy to become trait empathy, nor the multi-week habit loop by which habitual gaming reshapes empathy traits (DSMM Proposition 4) [PAGE 38]. Video-game empathy is inherently recursive theory of mind: the player must model the mental states of non-player characters (NPCs) or avatars while the game system may simultaneously model the player. The thesis's coding taxonomy captures this through perspective-taking (PT) and empathic concern (EC) dimensions, with Hess and Fila's (2016) two-by-two matrix dividing empathy into emotional congruence, projection, empathic concern, and perspective-taking [PAGE 16]. In experimental designs, players inhabit morally good or bad roles, and Gao et al. (2017) found induced empathy made morally good role-players more aggressive but morally bad role-players less aggressive—a recursive modulation in which the player's inferred moral identity of the target reverses the behavioral output of the same empathic manipulation [PAGE 31].

Crisis habit regression operates through DSMM's excitative response state. When content is highly arousing or threatens existing beliefs, users may deploy self-regulation—diverting attention or cognitive reappraisal—to mitigate impact [PAGE 19]. Under violent-game exposure, the excitative system may override empathic concern, regressing the player to a hostile-perception habit. The counter-trap is that prosocial games can elevate empathic concern but simultaneously induce personal distress; per Bloom's critique, emotional congruence without regulation yields distress rather than altruism, potentially causing avoidance of the very prosocial content intended to build empathy [PAGE 16–17, 31].

The clock structure of the evidence is asymmetric. State empathy is measured immediately after brief experimental exposure (first posttest only) [PAGE 28], capturing a sub-hour window. Trait empathy, by contrast, is a dispositional susceptibility requiring months to shift [PAGE 19]. The thesis finds correlational evidence that habitual violent or prosocial gaming predicts empathy traits [PAGE 38], but the meta-analysis cannot bridge the clock gap: it synthesizes acute state shifts (d ≈ 0.448 to 0.699) while remaining silent on the trait conversion rate. A counter-trap to the policy implication "prosocial games teach empathy" is that acute state empathy may decay within days unless reinforced by repeated usage, a feedback loop DSMM Proposition 4 describes but the data do not test [PAGE 38, 19].
