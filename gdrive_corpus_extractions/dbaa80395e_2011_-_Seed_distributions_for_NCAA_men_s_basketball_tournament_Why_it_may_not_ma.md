First, the bracket is translated into ordered seed sets. In round 1, seed \(n\) plays seed \(17-n\), so the possible winner set is \(\{n,17-n\}\), \(n=1,\ldots,8\) [PAGE 4, PAGE 17]. If the better seed wins, round 2 pairs seed \(k\) with \(9-k\), and round 3 pairs seed \(k\) with \(5-k\) [PAGE 6, PAGE 7]. The source generalizes this into \(2^{4-r}\) non-overlapping sets of \(2^r\) possible winners for rounds \(r=1,2,3\), and one set of 16 possible winners for rounds \(r=4,5,6\) [PAGE 17]. This is why the model is not a game-by-game matchup model: it is a round-by-round seed-position distribution model.

Second, the model treats advancement within a set as a geometric waiting-time process over seed positions. The source defines \(Z_{j,r}\) as the \(j\)-th set in round \(r\), and \(t_{i,j,r}\) as the \(i\)-th element in that set [PAGE 17]. The key theorem states that a geometric random variable is characterized by a constant conditional success probability: given that the first \(i-1\) seed positions have not advanced, the probability that the \(i\)-th seed position advances is \(p\), the same for all seed positions \(i\) [PAGE 16]. That is the central assumption. It is stronger than saying “better seeds win more often”; it says the hazard of advancement is constant across ordered seed positions within a set, conditional on no earlier success.

Third, because the seed set is finite, the source truncates the geometric distribution and renormalizes it. For set \(j\) in round \(r\), the probability assigned to the \(i\)-th position is \(\kappa_{j,r}p_{j,r}(1-p_{j,r})^{i-1}\), with \(i=1,\ldots,\min(2^r,16)\), \(j=1,\ldots,\max(2^{4-r},1)\), and \(r=1,\ldots,6\) [PAGE 18]. The normalizing coefficient is \(\kappa_{j,r}=1/[1-(1-p_{j,r})^{2^r}]\) for rounds \(r=1,2,3\), and \(\kappa_{r}=1/[1-(1-p_{r,1})^{16}]\) for rounds \(r=4,5,6\) [PAGE 18]. The source explicitly warns: “Important Note: \(p_{j,r}\) must be estimated for each position in each set in each round” [PAGE 18]. That warning is the gray-zone: the model is not parameter-free, and the number of parameters grows with the set structure.

Fourth, the source estimates \(p_{j,r}\) by method of moments rather than by direct Bernoulli maximum likelihood. It uses the truncated geometric mean
\[
E(Y(n,p))=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{n(1-p)^n}{1-(1-p)^n}
\]
and solves for \(p\) by an iterative bisection algorithm using the average seed position over the past 26 tournaments in each set and round [PAGE 21]. This is an important modeling choice. The observed statistic is an average seed position, not a set of binary advancement indicators. The estimate therefore compresses the seed distribution into a single moment.

Fifth, the source computes seed-combination probabilities by multiplying probabilities across independent regions and by counting distinct permutations of seeds across the four regions [PAGE 20]. That product structure assumes four independent geometric random variables for each set in rounds \(r=1,2,3,4\), two independent geometric variables for \(r=5\), and one for \(r=6\) [PAGE 20]. The Final Four tables then compare actual seed frequencies with expected frequencies [PAGE 23], and compare seed-combination probabilities with expected frequencies in years [PAGE 24, PAGE 25].

The gray-zone audit is substantial. The model’s “why it may not matter who plays whom” claim is internal to the seed-position hazard assumption: if \(p_{j,r}\) is constant within a set, then the specific pairing within that set does not change the geometric hazard. But the source’s own assumptions are strong: “Historical data are a representative sample of each seed’s performance” and “Each seed has a constant probability of winning against any other seed in a specified round” [PAGE 13]. Those assumptions are not physical laws. They can fail under rule changes, selection-committee changes, conference realignment, injury, fatigue, travel, or region-specific effects. The source acknowledges that “When subsets are taken based on seeds and rounds, sample sizes drop dramatically” [PAGE 13], and that rule changes may impact seed winning probabilities over time [PAGE 29]. It also states that the distribution “fits closest (via X2 goodness of fit test) in later rounds of tournament (Elite Eight and onwards)” [PAGE 29]. That is a clue that early-round fit is weaker and that sparse cells matter.

A further gray-zone issue is the table’s “standardized measure.” The seed-frequency table reports \(\delta_1=0.28\) for seed 1 with actual 45 and expected 41.6 [PAGE 23]. The numeric value is consistent with the squared Pearson contribution \((45-41.6)^2/41.6=0.278\), not with a z-score \((45-41.6)/\sqrt{41.6}=0.527\). So I treat the source’s \(\delta\) as a chi-square component, not a signed standard deviation. This matters because the paper’s inferential reading depends on chi-square logic, and chi-square asymptotic validity is weakest exactly where expected counts are small. The 2011 Final Four seed combination 3,4,8,11 is highlighted as an extreme historical case [PAGE 27], but a single rare event cannot validate or invalidate the model without a pre-specified test.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source uses probability, expected count, expected frequency, and chi-square operators. All probabilities are dimensionless; \(p_{j,r}\) is a dimensionless conditional advancement probability; \(i,j,r\) are dimensionless indices; expected counts are counts of appearances or games; expected frequency in years has unit years; and chi-square contributions are dimensionless.

1. Geometric waiting-time law. The source defines \(Y\) as geometric with probability \(p\) if
\[
P\{Y=k\}=(1-p)^{k-1}p,\qquad k=1,2,\ldots
\]
[PAGE 15]. Here \(p\in(0,1]\) for a nondegenerate-to-degenerate geometric law; \(p=0\) is not a valid probability for a positive waiting time.

2. Key conditional characterization. The source states: “Let \(X_1,X_2,\ldots\) be an arbitrary sequence of Bernoulli trials. Let \(Z\) be the number of these Bernoulli trials until the first success. Then \(Z\) is a geometric random variable with probability \(p\) iff
\[
P\{X_i=1\mid X_1=0,\ldots,X_{i-1}=0\}=p
\]
for all \(i=1,2,\ldots\)” [PAGE 16]. The source’s intuition is: “If the first \(i-1\) seed positions have not advanced to the next round (i.e., won), then the probability that the \(i\)th seed position advances is \(p\), the same value for all seed positions \(i\)” [PAGE 16]. This is the exact assumption that can break under cross-layer effects.

3. Seed-set operator. Define \(Z_{j,r}\) as the \(j\)-th set in round \(r\), and \(t_{i,j,r}\) as the \(i\)-th element in that set [PAGE 17]. The index ranges are
\[
i=1,\ldots,\min\{2^r,16\},\qquad
j=1,\ldots,\max\{2^{4-r},1\},\qquad
r=1,\ldots,6
\]
[PAGE 18]. For \(r=1\), the sets are \(\{1,16\},\{2,15\},\ldots,\{8,9\}\); for \(r=2\), \(\{1,8,9,16\},\ldots\); for \(r=3\), two sets; for \(r=4,5,6\), one set of 16 possible winners [PAGE 17].

4. Truncated geometric probability mass function. For set \(j\) in round \(r\),
\[
P\{Z_{j,r}=t_{i,j,r}\}=\kappa_{j,r}\,p_{j,r}(1-p_{j,r})^{i-1}
\]
[PAGE 18]. The normalizing coefficient is
\[
\kappa_{j,r}=\frac{1}{1-(1-p_{j,r})^{2^r}}
\quad\text{for }r=1,2,3,
\]
and
\[
\kappa_{r}=\frac{1}{1-(1-p_{r,1})^{16}}
\quad\text{for }r=4,5,6
\]
[PAGE 18]. The source’s exact warning is: “Important Note: \(p_{j,r}\) must be estimated for each position in each set in each round” [PAGE 18].

5. Method-of-moments equation. The source uses the truncated geometric mean
\[
E(Y(n,p))=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{n(1-p)^n}{1-(1-p)^n}
\]
[PAGE 21], and says “Estimates for \(p_{j,r}\) computed by method of moments” [PAGE 21]. The source solves this by “Iterative bisection algorithm” using the average seed position over the past 26 tournaments in each set and round [PAGE 21]. The data source is “NCAA: Historical tournament results (1985 – 2010)” [PAGE 12].

6. Seed-combination probability operator. The source computes the probability of seed combinations by taking the product of the probabilities of each seed appearing in that round and the number of distinct permutations that the four seeds can assume in set \(j\) in round \(r\) across the four regions [PAGE 20]. Formally, under the independent-region product model,
\[
P(\mathbf{s}_r)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathrm{Perm}(\mathbf{s}_r)}
\prod_{q=1}^{m_{j,r}} P\{Z_{j,r}=s_{\pi(q)}\},
\]
where the source specifies four independent geometric random variables for each set in rounds \(r=1,2,3,4\), two independent geometric variables for \(r=5\), and one geometric variable for \(r=6\) [PAGE 20]. The permutation count is essential because “Two identical seeds can play in a single game” [PAGE 9].

7. Expected count and chi-square operator. The source compares actual seed appearances with expected appearances in the Final Four [PAGE 23] and seed-combination probabilities with expected frequencies in years [PAGE 24, PAGE 25]. The Pearson contribution is
\[
\chi^2=\sum_{n=1}^{16}\frac{(O_n-E_n)^2}{E_n},
\qquad
\delta_n=\frac{(O_n-E_n)^2}{E_n}.
\]
For seed 1, \(O_1=45\), \(E_1=41.6\), so \((45-41.6)^2/41.6=0.278\), reported as 0.28 [PAGE 23]. The source calls this a “Standardized Measure” [PAGE 23], but numerically it behaves as a squared Pearson contribution, not a signed z-score.

Exact singularity and breakdown regimes:
- \(p_{j,r}=0\): the normalizing coefficient \(1/[1-(1-p)^{2^r}]\) has denominator zero; the truncated geometric PMF is undefined. The method-of-moments mean \(1/p-n(1-p)^n/[1-(1-p)^n]\) is also singular at \(p=0\). Therefore the model requires \(p_{j,r}>0\) for any set with positive support.
- \(p_{j,r}=1\): the distribution degenerates on the first seed position; variance is zero and the geometric waiting-time interpretation collapses to certainty. This is valid mathematically but statistically uninformative if all observed positions are identical.
- \(p_{j,r}\notin[0,1]\): invalid probability; bisection must be constrained to the probability simplex.
- Nonconstant \(p_{j,r}\) within a set: the key theorem’s conditional hazard condition fails [PAGE 16], so the geometric characterization is invalid.
- Dependence across regions or sets: the product formula [PAGE 20] is invalid because the joint probability is not the product of marginals.
- Sparse expected counts: chi-square goodness-of-fit is asymptotic; the source itself notes that “sample sizes drop dramatically” for subsets [PAGE 13], and many seed-level expected counts are small [PAGE 23].
- Structural breaks: rule changes such as the 35-second clock and expanded three-point arc may shift \(p_{j,r}\) over time [PAGE 29], so pooling 1985-2010 assumes stationarity that may not hold.
- Seed-label contamination: the selection committee considers “geography, conference affiliation” and other issues “unrelated to team skill” when placing teams in regions [PAGE 4], so seed is not a pure skill rank.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from Bernoulli trials and the geometric waiting-time law [PAGE 15] to the Shishebor and Towhidi (2004) conditional characterization cited on [PAGE 16], then to truncation and renormalization over finite seed sets [PAGE 18], then to method-of-moments estimation by bisection [PAGE 21], and finally to chi-square goodness-of-fit comparisons [PAGE 12, PAGE 29]. The historical tournament lineage is also explicit: the tournament was first held in 1939 with 8 teams, expanded to 64 teams in 1985, and increased to 68 teams with four play-in games in 2011 [PAGE 2]. The study uses 1985-2010 tournament results [PAGE 12], excluding play-in and First Four games [PAGE 13]. Earlier work by Jacobson and King (2009) focused on the top three seeds [PAGE 11], while this deck generalizes to seed distributions across rounds and seed combinations.

The retrocausal traps are multi-week and multi-era. First, recency trap: the 2011 Final Four with seeds 3,4,8,11 is an extreme case [PAGE 27], but the model is fit to 1985-2010 and then illustrated on 2011. Treating 2011 as a refutation or confirmation without out-of-sample scoring is a trap. Second, annual-update trap: the source says “Distribution parameters, \(p_{j,r}\), must be updated annually following each year’s tournament” [PAGE 29]. A static parameter table is therefore a stale parameter table. Third, rule-change trap: the 35-second clock and expanded three-point arc are named as possible impacts on seed winning probabilities [PAGE 29], so pooling across rule regimes is a hidden nonstationarity assumption. Fourth, bracket-structure trap: the set membership changes by round, and later rounds have one set of 16 possible winners [PAGE 17], so parameter estimates from early rounds cannot be transplanted into later rounds without checking the set definition. Fifth, selection-committee trap: geography and conference affiliation influence region placement [PAGE 4], which means the seed-to-skill mapping is not fixed. Sixth, sample-size trap: 1,638 total games are available, but “When subsets are taken based on seeds and rounds, sample sizes drop dramatically” [PAGE 13]. That makes rare seed combinations especially fragile.

A further genealogical point is that the deck’s website functionality is explicitly limited to Elite Eight, Final Four, National Finals, and National Championship seed combinations [PAGE 31], even though “Model can do much more than the web site functionality” [PAGE 31]. That is a useful audit boundary: public-facing outputs are a subset of the model, and the subset is exactly the later rounds where the source says the fit is closest [PAGE 29]. The lineage therefore contains a selection effect: the model is showcased where the fit is best, while early-round fit is less secure.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive agent problem is not about hidden intentions inside the model; it is about forecasters reasoning about other forecasters and about the selection committee. The simple heuristic is “Pick the better seed” [PAGE 10]. Under uncertainty, agents regress to that habit because it is simple and because the committee “tends to assign better seeds to better teams” [PAGE 10]. That is crisis habit regression: when the bracket becomes chaotic, the agent falls back to seed order rather than updating a full probability model. The counter-trap is overcorrecting after a rare event. The 2011 Final Four seed combination 3,4,8,11 [PAGE 27] can tempt an agent to abandon seed structure entirely or, conversely, to overfit a “chaos” rule. Both are traps because the source’s model is a distribution over seed positions, not a deterministic rule that rare combinations cannot happen. The source’s own seed-combination table includes extremely rare combinations with very small probabilities and very long expected frequencies [PAGE 25, PAGE 26]; rarity is not impossibility.

The clock layer is both literal and figurative. Literally, the source names the “Introduction of 35 second clock” and “Expansion of three point arc” as rule changes that may impact seed winning probabilities over time [PAGE 29]. Figuratively, the clock is the annual update requirement: “Distribution parameters, \(p_{j,r}\), must be updated annually following each year’s tournament” [PAGE 29]. An agent who ignores the clock uses a stale prior. An agent who overreacts to the clock treats every rule change as a total reset. The correct recursive stance is to model the committee’s seed assignment as a noisy signal of skill [PAGE 4], model other agents as likely to use the better-seed heuristic [PAGE 10], and then ask whether the seed-position hazard \(p_{j,r}\) remains stable enough to support the geometric characterization [PAGE 16]. If the hazard is stable, simple seed-order reasoning is coherent. If the hazard shifts by region, round, or rule regime, the simple heuristic is a habit that survives because it is cognitively cheap, not because it is calibrated.

The recursive theory-of-mind audit therefore separates three objects: the team’s latent skill, the committee’s seed label, and the forecaster’s model of both. The source explicitly says the committee considers geography and conference affiliation “unrelated to team skill” [PAGE 4]. That means a forecaster who treats seed as skill is modeling the committee’s output, not the team’s underlying strength. The counter-trap is to “correct” for that by inventing an unverified skill adjustment. The source-supported move is narrower: estimate the conditional seed-position hazard by round and set, test fit, and update annually [PAGE 21, PAGE 29].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Let \(W\) be a cross-layer vector containing physical state, biomechanical state, and weather/travel state. The model assumes
\[
P\{X_i=1\mid X_1=0,\ldots,X_{i-1}=0\}=p_{j,r}
\]
for all seed positions \(i\) in set \(j\) in round \(r\) [PAGE 16]. A cross-layer veto is warranted if
\[
P\{X_i=1\mid X_1=0,\ldots,X_{i-1}=0,W=w\}
\]
depends on \(i\), on \(w\), or on the interaction \(i\times w\) within a set. In that case the geometric characterization fails, and the product-over-regions formula [PAGE 20] also loses its independence justification.

Physical veto: basketball is an indoor sport, so direct weather exposure is weak. The stronger physical layers are injury, fatigue, depth, and travel. The source does not include injury or fatigue covariates; it uses only historical seed results [PAGE 12]. Therefore a physical veto cannot be proven from the source alone. It can be triggered only by evidence that the conditional advancement hazard changes with physical state. The source’s own statement that “Each seed has a constant probability of winning against any other seed in a specified round” [PAGE 13] is the exact assumption that a physical-state effect would violate.

Biomechanical veto: the source names the 35-second clock and expanded three-point arc as rule changes that may affect seed winning probabilities [PAGE 29]. These are biomechanical-environmental shifts: they alter pace, shot selection, spacing, and fatigue profiles. A pre-change and post-change pooling of \(p_{j,r}\) is therefore a structural-break risk. The veto proof is conditional: if the estimated \(p_{j,r}\) differs across rule regimes beyond sampling error, the constant-hazard model is misspecified. The source itself recommends annual updates [PAGE 29], which is an admission that the parameter is not era-invariant.

Weather veto: direct weather is a weak veto for an indoor tournament. Weather can act indirectly through travel delays, arrival timing, and recovery, but the source provides no weather covariates and no travel data. Therefore a weather veto is not proven by this deck. The honest adjudication is: physical and biomechanical vetoes are plausible and testable; weather is a weak, indirect layer that cannot be adjudicated without external data. The model should not be declared physically valid merely because it fits later rounds [PAGE 29]; fit is necessary but not sufficient for cross-layer invariance.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
I define three verifiable reinforcement-learning-with-verifiable-rewards (RLVR) veto scenarios. Each has a pass/fail prediction that can be checked against the source’s data structure or a reimplementation.

1. Nonstationarity veto across rule regimes. Split the 1985-2010 data into pre-change and post-change windows using the rule-change dates named by the source: the 35-second clock and expanded three-point arc [PAGE 29]. Estimate \(p_{j,r}\) separately for each window using the method-of-moments equation [PAGE 21]. Prediction: if the constant-hazard model is stable, the two estimates for each set-round cell should be compatible within a bootstrap or Monte Carlo confidence interval; if the model is nonstationary, at least one later-round cell, especially the Elite Eight or champion set, will show a shift that rejects equality at the 5% level. Veto if the rejection occurs. This is verifiable because the source gives the parameter-estimation equation and the historical window [PAGE 12, PAGE 21].

2. Product-independence veto for Final Four seed combinations. The source computes seed-combination probabilities by multiplying regional probabilities and counting permutations [PAGE 20]. It also reports rare combinations such as “One or More 15 or 16” with probability 0.002037 and expected frequency 491 years [PAGE 26], and very rare combinations such as 1,5,8,8 with probability 0.0000312 and expected frequency 32015 years [PAGE 25]. Prediction: if the four regions are independent and identically parameterized, the observed Final Four combination frequencies should be consistent with the product model in a multinomial or Monte Carlo test after pooling sparse cells. If the observed joint distribution shows excess clustering or anti-clustering beyond the product model, the independence assumption is vetoed. Veto if the Monte Carlo p-value is below 0.05 with a pre-specified sparse-cell pooling rule. This is verifiable because the source provides the product construction and the combination probabilities [PAGE 20, PAGE 24, PAGE 25].

3. Sparse-cell chi-square veto. The source uses chi-square goodness-of-fit and reports many small expected counts, for example seed 7 expected 1.9, seed 8 expected 1.2, seed 9 expected 0.7, and seed 11 expected 0.3 [PAGE 23]. Prediction: a nominal 5% chi-square test applied to the full 16-seed table will not have 5% Type I error under a simulated truncated-geometric null; sparse cells will inflate or deflate rejection rates. Veto if the simulated rejection rate exceeds 10% or falls below 1% at the nominal 5% level. This is verifiable by simulating the truncated geometric PMF [PAGE 18], fitting by the method-of-moments equation [PAGE 21], and computing the Pearson statistic. If the veto fires, early-round or rare-seed goodness-of-fit claims must be replaced by exact or Monte Carlo tests. The source’s own warning that “sample sizes drop dramatically” [PAGE 13] supports this audit.
