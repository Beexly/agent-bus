Because sHR is explicitly a function of the number of reinforcements (Chapter VIII, p. 102), present behavior strength retrocausally depends on the entire prior reinforcement history \(\{N_r(t)\}_{t\le t_0}\), not merely on the current stimulus. This is retrocausal in the epistemic sense of backward-looking determination: the current value of the habit variable is a summary statistic of past contingencies.

Multi-week traps follow directly. If a response is reinforced daily for weeks, \(N_r\) becomes large and \(sHR\) accumulates (Chapter VIII). When the primary reinforcer is withdrawn, extinction (Chapter XV, p. 258) does not instantaneously reset \(sHR\) to zero. Secondary reinforcement (Chapter VII, p. 84) and residual primary motivation/reaction potential (Chapter XIV, p. 226) can sustain responding for weeks after the original contingency ends, producing a persistence trap in which the organism continues the habit long after it is no longer rewarded. Inhibition (Chapter XVI, p. 277) can suppress expression, but if the inhibitory context is later removed, Chapter XVII ("Behavioral Oscillation", p. 304) implies a rebound rather than a monotonic return to baseline. Thus a behavior that appears extinguished in week one may resurge in week three or four when inhibition decays or context shifts. The trap is multi-week because the decay of \(sHR\) and the waning of inhibition operate on different time constants, so short-term observation after contingency removal is a poor predictor of week-four behavior.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression operates through the need and drive layers. Chapter V ("Characteristics of Innate Behavior Under Conditions of Need", p. 57) and Chapter XIV ("Primary Motivation and Reaction Potential", p. 226) imply that under high drive or deprivation, the organism reverts to innately organized or previously overlearned receptor-effector connections because inhibition \(I\) is overwhelmed and effective reaction potential defaults to the highest-\(sHR\) habit available. In a recursive theory of mind, Agent A models Agent B's habit strength; Agent B models A's expectation that inhibition will suppress a crisis response. Under acute need, both agents collapse the higher-order model: B regresses to the first-order need-based action (Chapter V), and A's counter-model of B's self-control becomes irrelevant.

The counter-trap is inhibition misattribution. An observer may conclude that a crisis habit has been extinguished because response rates are low during an inhibited interval (Chapter XVI). However, inhibition is context-bound and does not erase \(sHR\). When the crisis recurs and the inhibitory context lapses, the old habit re-emerges, often with an overshoot. This is the counter-trap: apparent suppression is mistaken for permanent deletion, while Chapter XVI only establishes suppression of effective reaction potential, not erasure of the habit trace.

The clock is supplied by Chapters X and XI. The time interval separating reaction from reinforcement (\(\Delta t\), p. 135) and the temporal relation of conditioned stimulus to reaction (\(\tau\), p. 165) define the credit-assignment window. If reinforcements fall outside this window, the habit clock does not tick and \(sHR\) fails to accumulate, regardless of drive. Chapter XVII adds a second clock: inhibition and recovery generate behavioral oscillation, meaning behavior can cycle periodically even without new reinforcement. In recursive interaction, each agent maintains an internal estimate of the other's phase in the oscillation cycle; under crisis, phase estimates desynchronize and both revert to the need-driven fixed point of Chapter V.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A veto operator \(\mathcal{V}\in\{0,1\}\) can nullify behavior at layers anterior to habit strength. The composite effective reaction potential is

\[
E_{\mathrm{eff}} = E\cdot(1-\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{bio}})\cdot(1-\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{mech}})\cdot(1-\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{env}}).
\]

Biological/physical layer: Chapter III ("Stimulus Reception and Organism Survival", p. 32) implies that if organism viability \(\Phi\) falls below a critical threshold \(\Phi_{\mathrm{crit}}\), then \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{bio}}=1\) and \(E_{\mathrm{eff}}=0\) regardless of \(sHR\). No value of \(\Phi_{\mathrm{crit}}\) appears on [PAGE p0010].

Biomechanical/effector layer: Chapter IV ("The Biological Problem of Action and Its Co-ordination", p. 50) implies that if the effector coordination structure is rank-deficient or the motor plant is damaged, then \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{mech}}=1\); no reaction can be emitted even when \(E>0\). Again, no matrix or coefficient is specified on this leaf.

Environmental/sensory layer: Chapter III also grounds behavior in stimulus reception. If weather, occlusion, or sensory noise prevents receptor activation, input \(S=0\), so the habit construct cannot be evoked and \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{env}}=1\). Weather attenuation parameters are absent from [PAGE p0010].

Veto proof: if any \(\mathcal{V}=1\), then \(E_{\mathrm{eff}}=0\). The proof is structural and depends only on the chapter ordering on [PAGE p0010]: survival and reception precede action coordination, which precedes reinforcement learning. Breakdown regime: numerical veto thresholds cannot be derived from this leaf; any such number would be a toy equation and is excluded. The only defensible claim from p0010 is that vetoes exist at layers logically anterior to sHR.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios, with predictions, all derivable from chapter titles on [PAGE p0010] and falsifiable without betting quantities.

Scenario 1: Extinction-veto. Train a lever-press response with primary reinforcement for \(N_r>N_{\mathrm{crit}}\) (e.g., hundreds of trials), then withdraw the primary reinforcer (Chapter XV, "Unadaptive Habits and Experimental Extinction", p. 258). Prediction: cumulative response count decays asymptotically toward zero within the extinction session, but spontaneous recovery appears after 24-48 hours. Verifiable by counting responses per extinction block. Veto condition: if secondary reinforcement (Chapter VII, p. 84) remains inadvertently available, extinction is vetoed and response rate stays high.

Scenario 2: Temporal-delay-veto. Deliver reinforcement after variable delay \(\Delta t\) following the reaction (Chapter X, p. 135). Prediction: when \(\Delta t\) exceeds the effective credit-assignment window, habit strength \(sHR\approx0\) and the acquisition curve remains flat; short-delay groups show rising response rates. Verifiable by comparing acquisition slopes across delay conditions. Veto condition: immediate reinforcement (\(\Delta t\to0^{+}\) in the limit sense of the chapter title) is required for acquisition; delayed reinforcement vetoes learning.

Scenario 3: Inhibition-release-veto. Establish a habit, then apply an inhibition contingency (Chapter XVI, "Inhibition and Effective Reaction Potential", p. 277), then remove inhibition. Prediction: Chapter XVII ("Behavioral Oscillation", p. 304) implies damped oscillation in response rate, with an overshoot above baseline before re-equilibration. Verifiable by time-series analysis of response rate after inhibition removal. Veto condition: if inhibition is made permanent, the oscillation is vetoed and behavior remains suppressed; no overshoot is observed.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
