Physical layer. The die example on [PAGE 189] tacitly assumes a physical symmetry encoded by the uniform measure μ({i}) = 1/6. Halmos notes that the nested certainty C_G(E) = 1 is "independent ... of any such numerical assumptions as the equal likelihood of the faces," but the F-case is not: C_F(E) = μ({2}) / μ({1,2}) depends on the actual physical weights of the faces. Veto: any physical model using a loaded or asymmetric die must specify μ non-uniformly; assuming uniformity without physical justification is an invalid layer transition. If the die is loaded such that μ({2}) ≠ μ({1}), then C_F(E) ≠ 1/2.

Biomechanical / observer layer. Conditioning on F assumes that observing the condition does not disturb the mechanism generating E. In a classical die this holds, but in a biomechanical or sensor system, the act of measuring F (e.g., detecting a limb position or a physiological threshold) can alter the state distribution for E. Veto: if the observation of F perturbs the system, the ratio μ(E∩F)/μ(F) computed from the pre-measurement μ no longer describes the post-observation measure; a measurement-disturbance term must be added or the model is invalid.

Weather / geophysical layer. For continuous variables (e.g., exact temperature or pressure), the conditioning event F = {X = x} often has μ(F) = 0. The pointwise ratio is then 0/0 and singular. Veto: one cannot assign a scalar C_F(E) to an exact-value condition without a limiting procedure, e.g., F_ε = {x ≤ X ≤ x + ε}, or a regular conditional distribution. The elementary formula on [PAGE 189] breaks down for null sets.

Veto proof summary. Any candidate probability p for C_F(E) must satisfy p ∈ [0,1], countable additivity in E, normalization C_F(Ω) = 1, and nested certainty C_G(E) = 1 whenever G ⊆ E with μ(G) > 0. The source's literal rendering that disjoint equiprobable outcomes each have "probability 1" ([PAGE 189]) sums to 2 and therefore fails additivity; it is vetoed as an OCR/print singularity and cannot stand as a formal probability assignment.
