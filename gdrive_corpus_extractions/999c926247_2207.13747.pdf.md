The sample spans 2004–2021 but excludes 2020 because of COVID-19 uniqueness [PAGE 2], and the last five of seventeen seasons (2017–2021) serve as holdout test data [PAGE 7]. This creates a temporal non-stationarity trap: team pace \xi_k is estimated over the full historical span, yet offensive tempo has evolved (e.g., the spread of air-raid/up-tempo systems associated with Mike Leach) [PAGE 3], so early-season pace estimates may not transfer to late-sample games. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears in the random forest comparator, which "is too quick to jump to a win probability close to 0 or 1, especially early in the game" [PAGE 12]. Under low information early in a contest, the non-Bayesian learner overcommits to extreme probabilities—a recursive habit of treating sparse play-level signal as decisive. The dynamic Bayesian estimator counteracts this through conjugate shrinkage: the beta prior contributes pseudo-wins and pseudo-losses, so cells with small N are pulled toward expert-elicited center rather than collapsing to 0 or 1 [PAGE 9–10].

The adjusted dynamic Bayesian estimator introduces a metacognitive counter-trap. Early in the game, when signal-to-noise is low, weight D2 is small and the output leans on the pregame win probability ^p_{pp}; as t grows and score differential |\ell| widens, D2 increases and control transfers to the cell-level dynamic estimator \hat p [PAGE 10–11]. This is a recursive theory-of-mind switch: the model asks "what would experts say before kickoff?" early, and "what does the realized possession score say now?" late. The clock variable t enters D2 linearly, while \tau governs the cell window that collapses to zero width at \tau = 2 [PAGE 8, 10]. Thus the game clock compresses the effective hypothesis space exactly when leverage is highest, shifting variance from the prior to the sparse-binomial likelihood in the final possessions.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: the model assumes a rigid 3600-second regulation clock in Eq. (1) [PAGE 4]. Overtime is not represented; any game extending beyond t = 3600 produces \tau = 0 and exits the modeled state space, constituting an automatic veto.

Biomechanical layer: Table 2 predictors include down, distance, yards to end zone, and pace, but no player-fatigue, injury, or substitution state [PAGE 4]. Pace \xi is a team-season average, not an in-game fatigue trajectory. A mid-game quarterback injury or defensive depletion would alter expected score \omega without being captured by the XGBoost features, vetoing calibrated output for the remainder of that game.

Weather layer: ESPN play-by-play scraping contains no weather covariates [PAGE 2]. Troch notes that pass plays stop the clock more frequently than run plays, so wind or precipitation that suppresses passing changes possession counts independently of team tempo [PAGE 3]. Eq. (1) weights season-average paces by clock time only; if weather materially changes tempo, \tau is mis-specified and win-probability posteriors drift.

Veto proof summary: the model is invalidated for (a) overtime games, (b) games with material mid-game roster injury not encoded in predictors, and (c) severe-weather contests where actual possession tempo deviates from season-average \xi. External appending of an overtime state, injury indicator, or weather modifier would be required to lift the veto.
