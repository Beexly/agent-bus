<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive evaluator has to model three clocks simultaneously. The calibration clock is 2018, when “every player at 1000” [PAGE 4]. The season-to-date clock is 2019 through September 10 [PAGE 1]. The forecast clock is future performance, which the source explicitly disclaims: “playerElo only evaluates historical season performance to-date, and is not a prediction statistic” [PAGE 9]. Crisis-habit regression occurs when an analyst, under uncertainty, abandons the schedule-adjusted Elo and reverts to familiar counting stats or a short hot streak. The source warns that playerElo “does not assign extra value to players who simply come up to the plate in high-powered offenses” [PAGE 11], so a naive observer who sees RBIs or runs scored during a multi-week stretch is precisely the kind of evaluator the model is designed to correct.

The counter-trap is overcorrection. Once an analyst learns that quality of competition matters, the tempting move is to discount every strong performance against a weak opponent and every weak performance against a strong opponent. But the source’s own examples cut both ways: Mike Minor ranks highly after success against the Astros, Athletics, Angels, and Mariners [PAGE 6], while Bauer struggles “against relatively easy divisions of the AL Central and NL Central” [PAGE 6]. So schedule adjustment is not a license to invert every signal. The recursive theory of mind is: the analyst models the player, the player’s opponents model the analyst’s public rating, and the front office models both. If the rating becomes a acquisition target, players and teams adapt. The counter-trap is to treat the Elo number as a revealed truth rather than a historical residual.

The clock discipline is to ask which question is being answered. For “who has been most valuable to date after context?” use playerElo. For “who will decline or break out?” the source recommends combining playerElo with “underlying swing metrics” such as xwOBA, exit velocity, and hard-hit percentage [PAGE 9]. It identifies J.D. That is a recursive forecast problem, not a playerElo problem. The crisis habit is to collapse all three clocks into one number; the counter-trap is to overfit the schedule correction; the clock solution is to keep description, biomechanics, and forecast horizon separate.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The statistical layer is not allowed to veto the physical layer. playerElo is a function of observed at-bat outcomes, base-out state, opponent Elo, and unspecified park and home-field adjustments [PAGE 3; PAGE 4]. It explicitly excludes exit velocity and launch angle [PAGE 5], defense, stolen bases, passed balls, and wild pitches [PAGE 4]. Therefore the conditional-independence assumption needed for playerElo to be a sufficient statistic is
\[
RV \perp\!\!\!\perp Z \mid (s,\theta_b,\theta_p),
\]
where \(Z\) is the omitted physical or environmental layer. If \(Z\) affects \(RV\) and is correlated with \(\theta\), the Elo estimate is biased. A biomechanical veto is triggered when a measured upstream variable changes sign relative to Elo: for example, a pitcher’s velocity, release point, or injury status deteriorates while playerElo remains high, or a batter’s exit velocity and launch angle improve while playerElo remains low. The source itself concedes that “playerElo does not incorporate statistics like exit velocity and launch angle in its calculations” [PAGE 5], so the physical layer has independent standing.

A defensive and speed veto is triggered for team aggregation. The source says “playerElo does not take into account defense or stolen bases” and that speedster teams such as the Royals, Rangers, and Mariners “will rank slightly lower than their true abilities,” while the Padres and Cubs “will rank slightly higher than true value” [PAGE 8]. The weather veto is adjacent: the run-expectancy matrix is built from 2016-2018 at-bats and rounded to 0.01 runs [PAGE 3]. Extreme wind, temperature, or precipitation can change run expectancy and quality of contact in ways not represented by the published park-factor and home-field adjustments. If the current game’s physical environment is far outside the calibration distribution, the Elo residual is not a valid causal attribution. The adjudication rule is therefore: veto playerElo as a sole decision rule when (i) an omitted physical or environmental variable is measured, (ii) its mechanism is upstream of run value, and (iii) its sign contradicts the Elo sign. In that case, playerElo remains a historical descriptor, but the physical layer controls the evaluation.
