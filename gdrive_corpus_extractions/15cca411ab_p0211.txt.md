This distinction is not just a pedantic footnote; it is the boundary condition for the additivity of variance. The equation $\sigma^2(f+g) = \sigma^2(f) + \sigma^2(g)$ holds if and only if the cross-term $2E[(f-Ef)(g-Eg)]$ is zero. [PAGE 196] The coefficient of correlation for measurable functions $f, g$ with finite non-zero variances is defined as:
$r(f,g) = \frac{E[(f - Ef)(g - Eg)]}{\sigma(f)\sigma(g)}$.
Units: Dimensionless. Assumptions: $(X, S, \mu)$ is a probability space, $f,g \in L^2(\mu)$.
Breakdown Regime: If $\sigma(f)=0$ or $\sigma(g)=0$, the correlation is undefined (division by zero). This occurs if one variable is almost surely constant.

[PAGE 196] Variance Additivity Law:
$\sigma^2(f+g) = \sigma^2(f) + \sigma^2(g) + 2\text{Cov}(f,g)$.
Necessary and sufficient condition for equality: $r(f,g) = 0$ (uncorrelated).
Information Units: The variance measures the spread of information entropy around the mean; additivity signifies orthogonal information channels.

[PAGE 196] Counterexample Geometry:
On $X=[0,1]$ with Lebesgue measure, let $f(x) = \sin(2\pi x)$, $g(x) = \cos(2\pi x)$.
Then $E[f]=E[g]=E[f g] = \int_0^1 \sin(2\pi x)\cos(2\pi x) dx = 0$. Thus $r(f,g)=0$.
However, $f^2 + g^2 = 1$ almost everywhere, proving strict functional dependence.

[PAGE 196] Kolmogoroff's Inequality:
Let $f_i, i=1,\dots,n$ be independent, $E[f_i]=0$, $E[f_i^2]<\infty$. Let $S_k = \sum_{i=1}^k f_i$ and $M_n = \max_{1\le k \le n} |S_k|$.
For any $\epsilon > 0$:
$\mu(\{x : M_n(x) \ge \epsilon\}) \le \frac{1}{\epsilon^2} \sum_{i=1}^n E[f_i^2]$.
Assumptions: Independence is critical. Without it, cross-terms in $E[S_n^2]$ do not vanish, and the bound collapses.
Singularity: If $\sum E[f_i^2] = \infty$, the bound becomes trivial ($1/\epsilon^2 \cdot \infty$).

Halmos’s formalization in 1950 abstracted these empirical struggles into pure measure theory, divorcing correlation from physical measurement and grounding it in the geometry of $L^2$ spaces.

The historical trap lies in the assumption that because frequencies converge (Weak Law of Large Numbers), paths must be well-behaved. A multi-week trap for modern practitioners is applying these Gaussian-centric intuitions (where uncorrelatedness *does* imply independence) to heavy-tailed financial or network data, leading to catastrophic model failures during black swan events.
