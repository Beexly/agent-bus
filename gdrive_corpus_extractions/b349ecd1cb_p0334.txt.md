<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Crisis habit regression. Under Knightian uncertainty, agents cannot compute a unique expected-utility-maximizing portfolio. The recursive habit is to regress to liquidity hoarding: holding money or the most liquid title not because of a stable time-preference rate, but because the set of priors \(\Pi\) is too wide to rank illiquid capital projects. On [PAGE 821], Knight’s rejection of “instinct” explanations means this hoarding is not a fixed “hoarding instinct”; it is a context-sensitive manifestation of general action tendencies under non-identifiable payoff distributions. In a crisis, the regression is to the most conservative element of \(\Pi\)—the worst-case yield path—causing investment \(I(t)=s(t)Y(t)\) to collapse even when \(s(t)\) is unchanged.

Counter-trap. The policy counter-trap is to treat the crisis interest spread as a measurable risk premium and supply liquidity as if the only problem were a temporary mispricing of \(\mathbb{P}\). If the true condition is Knightian (\(\Pi\) non-singleton), then expanding credit against collateral does not reduce ambiguity; it may increase the set of unverifiable future states, deepening the liquidity preference. The counter-trap is therefore to mistake a narrowing of bid-ask spreads (measurable risk) for a collapse of ambiguity (Knightian uncertainty).

Clock. Two clocks operate asynchronously. The time-preference clock (\(\rho\)) is psychological and relatively stable, ticking at the frequency of intertemporal consumption decisions. The uncertainty clock (\(r_{\text{present}}\)) ticks at the frequency of production surprises, technological form-shifts, and ownership-distribution changes. When the two clocks desynchronize—e.g., psychological patience is high but production ambiguity is also high—the market interest rate reflects the uncertainty clock, not the time-preference clock. Knight’s footnote 1 warning—that the capitalization rate and interest proper are “very different”—is precisely a warning against reading one clock with the other’s instrument.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Physical/biomechanical/environmental veto proof.

Physical layer veto. The premise “productive goods fixed in amount” (Case C) is vetoed by the second law of thermodynamics. Capital equipment undergoes entropy-driven depreciation: \(\delta K(t) > 0\) unless energy and material inputs maintain it. Maintenance is itself investment, so \(dK/dt = 0\) cannot hold without a positive gross investment flow that is subject to production uncertainty. Therefore the “fixed amount, no net saving” condition is physically singular: it can hold only as an idealized limit with perfect, costless maintenance, which does not exist in any material economy. Veto: any model assuming truly fixed capital stock without maintenance investment is physically invalid.

Biomechanical/cognitive layer veto. The footnote’s dismissal of “creative instinct” aligns with bounded rationality: saving and investment are not outputs of a dedicated “accumulation organ” but emerge from distributed prefrontal-striatal computation under cognitive load and time pressure. Because human computation of \(\Pi\) is limited, agents cannot maintain a stable, well-defined set of priors over capital yields. The veto is that the representative-agent time-preference equation \(r_{\text{static}} = \rho(\text{time-preference})\) assumes a computational capacity (stable, consistent discounting) that biological agents do not possess under crisis load.

Environmental/systemic layer veto. “Changeable in form” (Case C) presupposes an exogenous or endogenous process of technological reconfiguration. Environmental volatility—resource shocks, climate events, supply-network ruptures—alters the distribution of feasible capital forms \(A(t)\) independently of agent saving decisions. If the environment injects non-stationary shocks into \(\partial A/\partial t\), then interest cannot be reduced to a rent-like renaming; it carries genuine distributional consequences because the reconfiguration of capital forms redistributes claims across agents. Veto: the Case C claim that interest makes “no appreciable difference in the distribution of income” fails whenever environmental or technological volatility makes \(\partial A/\partial t\) stochastic and non-diversifiable.

Adjudication. The trichotomy on [PAGE 821] survives only as a limiting-case exposition, not as an equilibrium description. The physical layer vetoes exact stationarity; the biomechanical layer vetoes stable time-preference calculation; the environmental layer vetoes distributional neutrality. Knight’s argument therefore gains force: because all three layers break down, interest “in the proper sense” is unavoidable and distributive under real-world conditions.
