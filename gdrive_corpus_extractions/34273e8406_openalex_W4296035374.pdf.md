The source is a cross-sectional genetic-association study, not a prospective talent-identification trial. Its core chain is: genotype each athlete at preselected polymorphisms [PAGE 1, PAGE 18]; assign each genotype a “score” of 0, 1, or 2 according to an a priori “optimal” direction [PAGE 19]; sum scores within four polygenic profiles; rescale each sum to 0–100 arbitrary units to obtain the Total Genotype Score (TGS) [PAGE 1, PAGE 19]; compare TGS distributions between professional athletes and non-athletes; use ROC cutoffs and binary logistic regression to estimate ORs for athlete status [PAGE 4, PAGE 7, PAGE 8, PAGE 11]. The paper reports that “All the polymorphisms analysed met the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE)” [PAGE 4], which is necessary but not sufficient for causal or predictive validity.

Multi-hop, the inference is: allele/genotype frequency difference → TGS mean shift → ROC discrimination → threshold OR → “genetic selection” in endurance and football. Each hop has a gray zone. First, the genotype-score construction embeds prior assumptions about which genotype is “optimal”; the TGS is therefore a composite of expert-coded directions, not a direct measurement of performance. Second, the reported AUCs are modest: liver metabolism AUC = 0.605 [PAGE 4]; iron/energy AUC = 0.638 [PAGE 7]; cardiorespiratory AUC = 0.545 with \(p=0.152\) [PAGE 8]; muscle-injury profile AUC = 0.600 [PAGE 11]. Third, the ORs are threshold contrasts, not per-unit effects: liver cutoff 64.2 a.u., OR 1.96 [PAGE 4]; iron/energy cutoff 43.7 a.u., OR 2.21 [PAGE 7]; cardiorespiratory cutoff 53.5 a.u., OR 1.38, \(p=0.129\) [PAGE 8]; muscle cutoff 53.5 a.u., OR 2.70 [PAGE 11]. Fourth, the sample is male, adult, and sport-selected: 160 elite male endurance athletes, 132 male professional football players, and 160 non-athlete male subjects [PAGE 18]. The mean ages differ: athletes 24.9 years, endurance 26.1, football 23.4, non-athletes 27.9 [PAGE 4]. Age, training history, ancestry, body composition, injury history, and selection into professional sport are not fully modeled. Fifth, multiple polymorphisms and four profiles are compared, raising multiplicity and overfitting risk. Sixth, the non-athlete comparison group is not a youth talent pool; it cannot answer whether a child with a high TGS will become an elite adult.

The paper itself supplies the strongest gray-zone audit: “Currently, we cannot use genetic information for the identification of talent because few genes have been discovered related to sports performance and many of the results shown in the literature are still contradictory [9, 10]” [PAGE 17]. It also states: “These contradictory effects demonstrate that there is not a singular genetic profile that confers sporting success, but that the required genetic profiles are likely specific for the characteristics of each sport [9, 11]” [PAGE 17]. And it qualifies use: “Whilst genetic testing has the potential to assist in the identification of future talented performers, genetic tests should be combined with other tools, such as physiological values, and injury data to obtain an accurate identification of those athletes predisposed to succeed in the sport” [PAGE 16]. Thus the defensible claim is association and hypothesis generation, not deterministic talent diagnosis. The “first study” claim [PAGE 11] is about breadth of polygenic profiles, not about validated predictive utility. The TGS endpoints are also explicit: “a TGS of 100 a.u. represents a "perfect" profile and a TGS of 0 a.u. would be the "worst" profile possible when all GS have a score of 0 a.u. [81]” [PAGE 19]. That scaling is arbitrary-unit bookkeeping, not a biological law.

These are the paper’s equations 1–4 [PAGE 19]. The ROC operator is the probability that a randomly chosen athlete has a higher score than a randomly chosen non-athlete:

\[
\mathrm{AUC}
=
\Pr\left(S_{\text{athlete}}\gt S_{\text{non-athlete}}\right),
\quad \mathrm{AUC}\in[0,1].
\]

Sensitivity and specificity are also probabilities in \([0,1]\). The threshold logistic model is:

\[
\operatorname{logit}\pi(t)
=
\beta_0+\beta_c\,\mathbf{1}\{t\gt c\},
\quad
\pi(t)=\Pr(Y=1\mid T=t),
\]

where \(Y=1\) denotes professional-athlete status, \(t\) is TGS in a.u., \(c\) is the ROC-derived cutoff, and \(\mathrm{OR}=\exp(\beta_c)\) is dimensionless. Reported threshold results are: liver \(c=64.2\) a.u., OR \(=1.96\), \(95\%\ \mathrm{CI}:1.28\text{–}3.01\), \(p=0.002\) [PAGE 4]; iron/energy \(c=43.7\) a.u., OR \(=2.21\), \(95\%\ \mathrm{CI}:1.42\text{–}3.43\), \(p\lt0.001\) [PAGE 7]; cardiorespiratory \(c=53.5\) a.u., OR \(=1.38\), \(95\%\ \mathrm{CI}:0.90\text{–}2.12\), \(p=0.129\) [PAGE 8]; muscle \(c=53.5\) a.u., OR \(=2.70\), \(95\%\ \mathrm{CI}:1.75\text{–}4.16\), \(p\lt0.001\) [PAGE 11]. Corresponding AUCs are 0.605, 0.638, 0.545, and 0.600 [PAGE 4, PAGE 7, PAGE 8, PAGE 11].

Assumptions: independent observations; biallelic scoring except GSTM1; correct “optimal” genotype direction; no genotyping error; no substantial population stratification; HWE [PAGE 4]; fixed cutoffs; non-athletes representative of the same ancestral and environmental population; no unmeasured training, injury, or selection confounding. Breakdown regimes: if \(\mathrm{AUC}=0.5\), the score has no rank discrimination; if a logistic model separates completely, \(\beta_c\) and OR become unstable or infinite; if a genotype cell has zero counts, as with HFE GG in non-athletes [PAGE 9], OR estimates and \(\chi^2\) approximations are fragile; if \(t=0\) or \(t=100\), probability is bounded by the logistic link and the score saturates; if cutoffs are optimized in-sample, reported sensitivity/specificity are optimistic; if TGS is used across ancestries or sexes without calibration, the mapping from score to probability breaks; and if TGS is treated as a deterministic talent test, the model is misspecified because it omits training, biomechanics, environment, and chance.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from single-gene “speed” and “endurance” claims toward polygenic scores. ACTN3 c.1729C>T (rs1815739) and ACE I/D (rs4340) became the most frequently sold direct-to-consumer variants, with ACE II linked to endurance and ACTN3 CC to speed/power in the paper’s introduction [PAGE 2]. HFE, AMPD1, PGC1α, CKM, MLCK, NOS3, ADRA2A, ADRB2, and BDKRB2 were added to cover iron handling, energy efficiency, cardiorespiratory fitness, and muscle-injury risk [PAGE 2, PAGE 3, PAGE 18]. The TGS approach follows the Williams–Folland polygenic-score logic: sum favorable genotypes, rescale to 0–100, and compare distributions [PAGE 19]. The 2022 paper extends this to liver metabolism, iron/energy, cardiorespiratory fitness, and muscle injury in elite endurance athletes and professional football players [PAGE 11].

The retrocausal trap is to reason backward from adult elite survivors to youth talent. If a genotype is enriched among adult professionals, one may infer that testing children will identify future professionals. That inference is invalid without prospective prediction, because adult status is the outcome of selection, training, injury, opportunity, and survivorship. The paper’s own caution is decisive: “Currently, we cannot use genetic information for the identification of talent because few genes have been discovered related to sports performance and many of the results shown in the literature are still contradictory [9, 10]” [PAGE 17]. The multi-week trap is physiological accumulation: “It is known that in competitions like cycling and elite running the accumulated efforts over several weeks affect performance, which is also the case in professional football, due to the alteration in the redox-system of the systemic homeostasis and withdrawal of toxic products generated by the high oxidative stress produced by these sports disciplines [61, 62]” [PAGE 13]. A static genotype score cannot capture week-to-week redox state, fatigue, muscle damage, fixture congestion, or injury history. The historical lineage therefore supports “polygenic association” as a research program, not “genetic talent identification” as a validated practice.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Consider three recursive stakeholder modes. Crisis habit regression: a parent or coach, facing uncertainty, mentally models a scout as “someone who will choose the genetically gifted child,” and therefore regresses to a single-gene or DTC test as reassurance. The recursion is “I believe that you believe that the club will select on genes, so I test now.” This habit is cognitively cheap but evidentially weak because the reported AUCs are about 0.55–0.64 [PAGE 4, PAGE 7, PAGE 8, PAGE 11]. Counter-trap: a club reasons “if competitors use genetic tests, we must also use them,” even if the test has low discrimination. This is a recursive arms race in beliefs, not an improvement in prediction. It can produce false positives, false negatives, and ethical harm. Clock: the decision clock is developmental and longitudinal. A score at age 12 is not a score at age 24; training age, maturation, injury, and sport-specific skill change the outcome. The paper’s non-athlete comparison is adult [PAGE 4, PAGE 18], so it cannot calibrate a youth clock. The counter-trap is to confuse a cross-sectional adult association with a prospective youth forecast; the clock-aware strategy is to treat TGS as one adjunct among physiological, biomechanical, training, and injury data, exactly as the paper advises: genetic tests “should be combined with other tools, such as physiological values, and injury data” [PAGE 16].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather layers can veto genetic claims. Let \(Y=1\) denote elite performance and let \(G\) be genotype/TGS, \(T\) training, \(B\) biomechanical capacity, \(W\) environmental load, and \(I\) injury history. A minimal latent model is:

\[
Y=\mathbf{1}\{\eta_0+\eta_G G+\eta_T T+\eta_B B+\eta_W W+\eta_I I+\varepsilon\gt0\},
\]

with dimensionless latent scale. The veto is:

\[
B\lt B_{\min}\;\Rightarrow\; \Pr(Y=1\mid G=g,B=b,W=w)=0
\quad \text{for all }g,
\]

and

\[
W\gt W_{\max}\;\Rightarrow\; \Pr(Y=1\mid G=g,B=b,W=w)=0
\quad \text{for all }g.
\]

Physical layer: cardiorespiratory fitness is “the capacity of the circulatory and respiratory systems to supply oxygen to skeletal muscle mitochondria for energy production needed during physical activity” [PAGE 15], and peak aerobic exercise depends on “the synergistic action of pulmonary, cardiovascular and muscle tissue” [PAGE 16]. If oxygen delivery or mitochondrial capacity is limiting, a favorable TGS cannot override the limit. Biomechanical layer: tendon stiffness, joint stability, muscle damage, and force transmission constrain load tolerance; the paper links MLCK and CKM to muscle damage and energy homeostasis [PAGE 3, PAGE 11]. Weather layer: heat, humidity, altitude, wind, and surface alter thermoregulation and oxygen availability; these are not encoded in the TGS. The cardiorespiratory profile itself did not discriminate professional athletes overall: AUC \(=0.545\), \(p=0.152\) [PAGE 8]. Thus cross-layer veto proof is simple: when \(B\lt B_{\min}\) or \(W\gt W_{\max}\), the genetic score has no rescue effect in the model; when constraints are non-vetoing, the genetic effect remains probabilistic and small. The correct adjudication is adjunctive, not deterministic.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
