The core of Theorem B uses Fubini's theorem. For a measurable set \(E\), the product measure is \((\mu \times \nu)(E) = \int_X \nu(E_x) d\mu(x)\). To show \(S\) preserves measure, compute \((\mu \times \nu)(S(E)) = \int_X \nu((S(E))_x) d\mu(x) = \int_X \nu(x E_x) d\mu(x)\). Since \(\nu\) is the right Haar measure on the group, it is right-invariant, so \(\nu(x E_x) = \nu(E_x)\). Hence the integral equals \((\mu \times \nu)(E)\).

Thus, the specific pairing \(\mu \times \nu\) with \(\mu\) left-invariant and \(\nu\) right-invariant is essential for the cancellation. The proof is robust under the assumption that \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are Haar measures of appropriate variance.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
This construction extends Haar's original theorem (1933) on invariant integrals on locally compact groups. Halmos abstracts the structure to measurable groups, focusing on the algebraic manipulations of transformations. The shearing map \(S\) encodes right multiplication, while \(T\) encodes left multiplication via conjugation by \(R\). Historically, such transformations appear in ergodic theory (e.g., von Neumann's work on unitary operators) and representation theory, where \(S\) and \(T\) generate actions of the group on itself. The multi-week trap lies in assuming all groups have a common left-right Haar measure; for non-unimodular groups (e.g., affine group), \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) must be chosen appropriately—here, \(\mu\) left, \(\nu\) right—to ensure measure preservation. The proof technique prefigures modern approaches in harmonic analysis using disintegration of measures.
