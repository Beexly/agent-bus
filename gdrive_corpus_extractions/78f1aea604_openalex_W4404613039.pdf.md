Recursive theory of mind models what players, coaches, and investigators believe about each other’s beliefs under season pressure. Crisis habit regression: as the season approaches the state championship, game stakes rise; under cognitive load and fatigue, players may regress to habitual tackling techniques (head-down contact, leading with the helmet) that increase peak linear acceleration A_k and cumulative load G_k. Coaches observing no diagnosed concussions may infer that full-contact practice is safe and maintain or increase exposure frequency, thereby recursively increasing H_k events precisely when decision-making is most strained [PAGE 2–3, 9].

Counter-trap: the “no concussion diagnosed = cognitively safe” belief is a trap. The study itself found zero clinical concussions yet detected regression signals: maximum impact negatively associated with ImPACT verbal score (B=-0.349 points/g, p=0.015) and cumulative g-force negatively associated with visual memory level and score [PAGE 1, 6–7]. A second counter-trap is the short-form test: because HVMS correlated only moderately with ImPACT visual memory and the authors conclude short-form testing cannot robustly replace ImPACT [PAGE 8], using the novel tile game as a surveillance surrogate would create false reassurance. A third counter-trap is the Stroop improvement: coaches may read faster, more deliberate Stroop performance as neurocognitive resilience, whereas the authors interpret it as conditioning to be slower and more deliberate—possibly a strategy to avoid offsides/false-start penalties rather than genuine improvement [PAGE 8].

Clock: the relevant clock is the season clock (August–December) nested inside a longer recovery clock unknown to the study. Testing occurred within one month of the final game; no off-season or next-pre-season baseline was collected [PAGE 3, 8–9]. Thus the “crisis” endpoint is observationally right-censored: a player showing -1.44 words at postseason may recover by next August or may enter the next season with accumulated deficit. The recursive loop is that without a recovery clock, the staff cannot update the prior that “this season’s hits were safe,” so the same exposure protocol recurs next season with an updated but unmeasured baseline [PAGE 9].

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and environmental veto proofs.

Physical veto: the measurement layer is insufficient to establish intracranial causation. The CUE Sports Sensor logged external helmet kinematics only; no rotational acceleration, no impact directionality, and no finite-element brain-strain output [PAGE 2, 9]. Because concussion and axonal injury are more strongly coupled to rotational and angular kinematics than to linear g-force alone, a veto is triggered: any claim that G_k or A_k “caused” HVMS or IVS decline is physically underdetermined. The study itself concedes accelerometer data did not account for directionality of head impacts [PAGE 9].

Biomechanical veto: the 60 g high-impact threshold is not a deterministic injury boundary. Nine players had at least one impact >60 g and the maximum observed was 97 g, yet zero concussions were diagnosed [PAGE 3, 6–7]. Thus H_k is an exposure proxy, not a diagnostic event; the p=0.063 word-recall contrast between H_k=1 and H_k=0 groups sits below \alpha=0.05 and fails the formal significance rule [PAGE 6]. Additionally, impact magnitude was tightly clustered (mean 16.54 g, SD 0.88 g, \approx 5% coefficient of variation), producing r=0.977 between impact count and cumulative g-force [PAGE 4, 8]. This near-perfect collinearity means the regression cannot identify whether “many subthreshold loads” or “rare peak loads” drives the HVMS/HVML association; a veto applies to any isolated interpretation of cumulative g-force without rotational terms.

Weather/environmental veto: the source records no temperature, humidity, precipitation, or indoor/outdoor practice distribution for an August–December outdoor football season [PAGE 2]. Environmental extremes can alter sensor performance, player hydration, fatigue, and symptom reporting (the ImPACT symptom score includes drowsiness, sleep changes, mental fogginess, and fatigue) [PAGE 8–9]. Because playing both offense and defense was associated with a 6.75-point higher symptom score (p=0.10, non-significant) and slower reaction time percentile, fatigue is a competing biomechanical/environmental layer that cannot be excluded [PAGE 6–8]. Until environmental and load-management covariates are measured, the attribution of reaction-time slowing to head impacts versus two-way fatigue is vetoed.

Adjudication: the only surviving layer is associational within one season, one school, male starters only [PAGE 9]. Physical causality (brain strain), biomechanical specificity (rotation/direction), and environmental control (weather/fatigue) are all absent, so the causal chain from helmet g-force to cognitive change remains open, not closed.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR counterfactual veto scenarios with predictions.

Scenario 1 (multiple-comparison veto). Re-run the analysis with a pre-registered confirmatory protocol and family-wise error control across the 18 dependent variables (e.g., Bonferroni \alpha_{\mathrm{FW}} \approx 0.05/18 = 0.0028 or Holm-Bonferroni), without backward stepwise removal of p_F>0.10 terms. Verifiable condition: report adjusted p-values for the H_k word-contrast and the maximum-impact IVS regression. Prediction: the unadjusted p=0.063 word-recall contrast and p=0.015 IVS maximum-impact coefficient will not survive family-wise correction (adjusted p > 0.0028), vetoing the claim that >60 g exposure reliably reduces verbal recall. If they do survive, the claim gains confirmatory strength.

Scenario 2 (kinematic completeness veto). Replicate with instrumented mouthguard or helmet array capturing six-degree-of-freedom kinematics (linear plus rotational acceleration) and couple to a finite-element head model yielding maximum principal strain \varepsilon_{\max,k} and cumulative strain \Sigma_k=\sum_i \varepsilon_{\max,k,i}\Delta t_i, with units of strain (dimensionless) and strain-seconds. Verifiable condition: fit \widehat{HVMS}_k = \beta_0 + \beta_G G_k + \beta_R R_k + \beta_\varepsilon \Sigma_k + \epsilon_k where R_k is peak rotational acceleration (rad/s^2). Prediction: once rotational peak R_k and cumulative strain \Sigma_k are included, the linear cumulative g-force coefficient \beta_G will become non-significant (p>0.05) or change sign, vetoing the interpretation that linear g-force dose alone drives visual memory decline. If \beta_G remains significant after adjustment, the linear-load interpretation is strengthened.

Scenario 3 (temporal/practice-effect veto). Add mid-season (week 6) and off-season (3 months post) ImPACT and short-form testing, plus actigraphy for sleep and a fatigue scale. Verifiable condition: model each metric Y with mixed effects Y_{k,t}=\gamma_0+\gamma_1 t+\gamma_2 H_k+\gamma_3 (H_k\times t)+u_k+\epsilon_{k,t}, t in weeks, u_k\sim N(0,\sigma_u^2). Prediction: the end-of-season Stroop and ImPACT verbal improvements will be explained by a positive time slope \gamma_1 (practice/maturation) rather than by H_k, and the -1.44 word-recall deficit in the H_k=1 group will partially recover off-season (\gamma_3>0), vetoing the interpretation of a persistent, impact-specific cognitive lesion. If the deficit persists off-season with \gamma_3\approx0 and \gamma_1\approx0, a lasting effect is supported.
