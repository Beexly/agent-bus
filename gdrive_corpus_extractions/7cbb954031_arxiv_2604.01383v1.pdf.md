<THINK_TRACE>
The source’s multi-hop derivation is a sequence of separations, not a single detector score. Hop one is candidate discovery: Grounding DINO proposes player–dummy pairs under hierarchical prompts and relaxed thresholds [PAGE 3]. Hop two is temporal validation: candidates are scored across neighboring frames using IoU, center displacement, and area similarity [PAGE 4]. Hop three is motion ranking: displacement magnitude and directional approach toward the dummy are combined [PAGE 5]. Hop four is backward refinement: the grounding frame is treated as biased later than the onset, so the pipeline searches backward for the first frame with both objects [PAGE 2]. Hop five is pixel verification: SAM2 propagates separate masks, and their intersection is the contact signal [PAGE 5]. Hop six is fallback: if no overlap appears, the next ranked candidate is tried [PAGE 5]. The paper states the design rationale directly: “The fundamental difficulty is the gap between pair discovery and contact confirmation” [PAGE 1], and “Detection confidence reflects appearance similarity to a text prompt, not physical intersection” [PAGE 1].

The gray-zone audit begins with units and clock. At 30 fps, one frame is 33.3 ms. The paper’s ±5, ±10, ±15, and ±20 frame tolerances therefore correspond to 0.167 s, 0.333 s, 0.500 s, and 0.667 s. The annotation uncertainty is “approximately ±3 frames” [PAGE 6], or about 100 ms. Thus the ±5-frame band is already within the combined clock-plus-annotation gray zone. The source reports conditional precision at ±5 frames of 79.1% for GRAZE, against 80.4% for MARS and 80.3% for SOLE [PAGE 7]. This is not a contradiction; it is a trade-off. Backward refinement can step slightly past the onset, moving a near-perfect prediction into the adjacent error band. The compensating result is the error tail: GRAZE’s |err| ≥20 frame rate is 8.4%, versus 14.2% and 14.3% for SOLE and MARS [PAGE 7]. For biomechanical measurement, a two-frame overshoot is less destructive than a twenty-frame miss, because a late localization shifts posture into post-contact deformation. The paper states: “FPOC localization has an asymmetric cost structure: early predictions can be trimmed in post-processing, whereas late ones shift downstream pose measurements into post-contact postures” [PAGE 6].

The deepest physical gray zone is 2D overlap versus physical contact. The paper defines tFPOC as “the exact frame at which the player first physically intersects the dummy” [PAGE 3], yet the operational contact signal is mask intersection in the image plane. The paper admits the gap: “The most common error in correctly processed clips is approach-from-behind geometry: in the final stride, the player’s image-plane silhouette overlaps the dummy before physical contact occurs, typically placing the prediction two to four frames early” [PAGE 7]. That is a verifiable breakdown regime for any system that treats one-pixel overlap as physical proof. A one-pixel intersection can arise from mask drift, motion blur, or silhouette overlap without force transmission. Conversely, true contact can be missed if occlusion removes the overlap. The pipeline therefore has both false-positive and false-negative contact modes.

A second gray zone is visibility. The definition of tFFBO requires both objects to be simultaneously visible [PAGE 3]. If the approach is off-camera, no simultaneous view exists; the paper reports that “Off-camera approaches produce no simultaneous player-dummy view, making grounding impossible regardless of prompt or threshold” [PAGE 7]. In concurrent-player scenes, the directional gate can reject both athletes because neither independently clears the threshold [PAGE 7]. These are not localization errors but definitional and candidate-selection failures. A third gray zone is mathematical: Eq. 5 in the paper has a zero-norm singularity when the mean past center equals the current center, because the unit motion vector is undefined. Eq. 3 also lacks an explicit clip; if the center distance exceeds τdist = 200 px, the displacement term can become negative. These are not cosmetic; they mark where a confidence score stops behaving like a bounded, calibrated probability. The source never claims these scores are probabilities, and the audit must not treat them as such.</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source formulates the event as two temporal quantities. It writes: “the First Frame with Both Objects (tFFBO), the earliest frame in which both P and D are simultaneously visible, and the First Point of Contact (tFPOC), the exact frame at which the player first physically intersects the dummy” [PAGE 3]. Formally, with \(V=\{I_t\}_{t=1}^{T}\) a video of \(T\) frames, define
\[
t_{\mathrm{FFBO}}=\min\{t:\; P\ \text{and}\ D\ \text{are simultaneously visible in}\ I_t\}.
\]
This is a frame index, unit: frames. At 30 fps, a frame index difference \(\Delta t\) maps to seconds by \(\Delta t/30\).

SAM2 propagation is
\[
M_t^{(i)}=\mathrm{SAM2}\left(V,t_{\mathrm{FFBO}},b^{(i)}\right),\qquad i\in\{P,D\},
\]
where
\[
M_t^{(i)}\in\{0,1\}^{H\times W}.
\]
The mask is a binary array with information content of at most \(H\times W\) bits. Contact overlap is
\[
\mathrm{overlap}_t=\sum_{x,y} M_t^{(P)}(x,y)\land M_t^{(D)}(x,y).
\]
The unit of \(\mathrm{overlap}_t\) is pixels, not a probability and not a force. The paper states: “The FPOC is the earliest frame where overlap reaches at least one pixel (τoverlap = 1):” [PAGE 5]. Thus
\[
t_{\mathrm{FPOC}}=\min\{t:\mathrm{overlap}_t\ge \tau_{\mathrm{overlap}}\},\qquad \tau_{\mathrm{overlap}}=1\ \mathrm{pixel}.
\]
The event window ends at
\[
t_{\mathrm{end}}=\min\left(T,t_{\mathrm{FPOC}}+L_{\mathrm{post}}\right),
\]
with \(L_{\mathrm{post}}=20\) frames unless stated otherwise [PAGE 5]. At 30 fps, 20 frames is 0.667 s.

Candidate motion is computed from matched player centers. The displacement score is
\[
m_{\mathrm{disp}}(P)=\min\left(\frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{m\in Q}\frac{\|c_0-c_m\|_2}{200},1\right),
\]
where the numerator is in pixels and the denominator is a 200 px reference, so the result is dimensionless and clipped to \([0,1]\). The directional score is
\[
m_{\mathrm{dir}}(P,D)=\frac{\langle \hat v_{\mathrm{motion}},\hat v_{\mathrm{to-dummy}}\rangle+1}{2},
\]
where \(\hat v_{\mathrm{motion}}=(c_0-\bar c_{\mathrm{past}})/\|c_0-\bar c_{\mathrm{past}}\|\) and \(\hat v_{\mathrm{to-dummy}}=(c_{D^*}-c_0)/\|c_{D^*}-c_0\|\). The cosine is dimensionless; the shifted score is also dimensionless in \([0,1]\). The paper discards candidates with \(m_{\mathrm{disp}}<0.08\) or \(m_{\mathrm{dir}}<0.30\), and ranks survivors by
\[
\mathrm{conf}_{\mathrm{overall}}=0.3\,c_{\mathrm{cons}}+0.3\,m_{\mathrm{disp}}+0.4\,m_{\mathrm{dir}},
\]
a dimensionless weighted score [PAGE 5]. This is not a probability: the weights are design choices, not calibrated likelihoods.

Temporal validation uses
\[
\mathrm{conf}(d_0,d_v)=0.55\frac{\mathrm{IoU}(d_0,d_v)}{\tau_{\mathrm{IoU}}}
+0.30\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{dist}(d_0,d_v)}{\tau_{\mathrm{dist}}}\right)
+0.15\,sr(d_0,d_v),
\]
with \(\tau_{\mathrm{IoU}}=0.20\) dimensionless and \(\tau_{\mathrm{dist}}=200\) px; \(sr\) is a min-over-max area ratio and is dimensionless [PAGE 4]. Important exact breakdown: Eq. 3 has no stated clipping, so if \(\mathrm{dist}(d_0,d_v)>\tau_{\mathrm{dist}}\), the middle term is negative and \(\mathrm{conf}\) need not lie in \([0,1]\). Eq. 5 has a singularity when \(c_0=\bar c_{\mathrm{past}}\) or \(c_{D^*}=c_0\), because a unit vector with zero norm is undefined. Eq. 4 saturates at 1 when displacement exceeds 200 px, so large motion is indistinguishable from extreme motion. Eq. 9 has the threshold singularity: if \(\tau_{\mathrm{overlap}}=0\), then \(\mathrm{overlap}_t\ge 0\) is true for all \(t\), making \(t_{\mathrm{FPOC}}\) degenerate; if \(\tau_{\mathrm{overlap}}\) exceeds the attainable overlap, then \(t_{\mathrm{FPOC}}\) is undefined and the candidate must fall back. The source explicitly says: “If no such frame exists for the current candidate, it is rejected and the next in the ranked list is evaluated” [PAGE 5]. The assumptions are simultaneous visibility, stable mask identity, and image-plane overlap as a necessary contact proxy. The breakdown regimes are off-camera approaches, concurrent-player ambiguity, mask drift, occlusion, and approach-from-behind silhouette overlap [PAGE 7].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is a chain of substitutions. Earlier sports-video work assumed controlled broadcast views: SoccerNet and action-spotting benchmarks pushed temporal precision under curated conditions, while I3D and SlowFast assumed an actor-centered field of view [PAGE 2]. Supervised temporal localization methods such as BMN and ActionFormer “require frame-level annotations unavailable for contact onset in practice footage” [PAGE 3]. The source instead borrows open-vocabulary grounding from GLIP, OWL-ViT, and Grounding DINO, then borrows video segmentation from SAM and SAM2. The paper’s own lineage sentence is precise: “GLIP [9] unified grounding and detection through language-image pretraining; OWL-ViT [13] showed that a contrastively trained Vision Transformer achieves competitive open-set detection without task-specific heads; and Grounding DINO [12] fused a DINO-style detector with cross-modal attention for strong open-vocabulary performance” [PAGE 2]. SAM2 “extends this to video via a streaming memory architecture” [PAGE 3]. Retrocausally, the later success of the full system explains why the earlier components cannot be judged in isolation: detection confidence is not contact, temporal persistence is not direction, and segmentation is not verification unless its mask intersection is explicitly used as evidence.

A multi-week experimental genealogy is visible in the ablation. Week one: single prompt and fixed threshold, the SOLE baseline, reaches 92.0% coverage but 85.8% conditional precision at ±20 frames and a 14.2% error tail [PAGE 7]. Week two: add temporal validation and backward refinement, the TRACE baseline; without directional motion scoring, temporal validation becomes restrictive rather than discriminative, “collapsing coverage to 46.9%” [PAGE 6]. Week three: add motion scoring, the MARS baseline; it reaches 91.9% coverage but conditional precision at ±20 frames is 85.7%, nearly identical to SOLE’s 85.8%, showing that motion scoring in isolation changes ranking but not per-clip accuracy [PAGE 7]. Week four: add multi-prompt progressive search and ranked multi-candidate SAM2 retry; coverage rises to 97.4% [PAGE 7]. Week five: combine all stages; conditional precision at ±20 frames reaches 91.6% and the catastrophic-error tail halves to 8.4% [PAGE 7]. The retrocausal lesson is that the value of directional motion scoring is not visible until it interacts with multi-prompt search and retry; the value of backward refinement is not visible at ±5 frames but is visible in the tail.

The traps are equally retrospective. A prompt-only trap overfits to attire and lighting; the paper uses a three-level hierarchy from specific gear to generic person-running prompts [PAGE 3]. A threshold-only trap assumes lower confidence is always worse; the paper collects all valid candidates because “grounding quality and contact quality do not correlate monotonically” [PAGE 2]. A persistence-only trap confuses a stationary bystander with an active tackler; TRACE demonstrates the coverage collapse [PAGE 6]. A refinement-only trap chases the ±5 band and ignores that refinement can overshoot by one to two frames [PAGE 7]. A retry-less trap routes valid clips to manual review; only 19 of 738 are unprocessed in the full system [PAGE 7]. These are multi-week traps because each looks locally correct and only fails when evaluated across coverage, conditional precision, and error tail simultaneously.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer has four perspectives: the annotator, the coach or biomechanist, the model, and the evaluator. The annotator’s ground truth is not visual overlap alone; the paper says the annotator marked “the first frame in which the player physically collides with and moves the dummy (not merely the first frame of visual overlap/occlusion)” [PAGE 6]. The coach needs that onset because “An incorrect FPOC shifts pose measurements to pre- or post-contact postures, making rubric-based scoring unreliable” [PAGE 1]. The model sees candidate boxes, confidence scores, and mask intersections. The evaluator chooses denominators: end-to-end accuracy over all 738 clips, or conditional precision over the evaluable subset [PAGE 6]. The recursive failure is that each perspective can mistake its own proxy for the others’ target.

Crisis habit regression appears when clutter or occlusion triggers a fallback to the most familiar signal: detection confidence. The source warns that confidence is appearance similarity, not physical intersection [PAGE 1]. A system under stress may accept a high-confidence mid-contact detection and report a late FPOC, because “the grounding frame is systematically biased later than the true onset” [PAGE 2]. The counter-trap is overcorrection: backward refinement can step one to two frames past the onset, which explains GRAZE’s slight deficit at ±5 frames against MARS and SOLE [PAGE 7]. If the evaluator overweights the ±5 band, the system is rewarded for a habit that increases the catastrophic tail. The clock trap is the 30 fps timeline: two to four frames early is 66.7 to 133.3 ms, while annotation uncertainty is about ±3 frames [PAGE 6]. A disagreement of two frames can be within human annotation noise. The correct recursive move is to model the evaluator’s cost asymmetry: early can be trimmed; late contaminates posture. That is why the tail is the more consequential metric.

The coverage/precision recursion is explicit in the counts. Of 738 videos, 681 carry verified ground-truth FPOC labels; 57 lack a simultaneous annotation pass [PAGE 6]. GRAZE outputs 719 clips, but only 666 overlap with the 681 GT-labeled clips; 53 segmented clips lack annotations, and 15 GT-labeled clips fell into manual review [PAGE 7]. A coach who sees 97.4% coverage may trust the system; an evaluator who computes conditional precision excludes the unannotated and manually reviewed clips; a model that is rewarded only for coverage may route hard cases to review. The counter-trap is to treat manual review as success. The clock forces humility: at 30 fps, a 20-frame tail is 0.667 s, and a 2-frame overshoot is 0.067 s. The recursive theory of mind must therefore distinguish “the model believes contact occurred,” “the mask proves image-plane overlap,” “the annotator would call that physical contact,” and “the biomechanist can use that frame for posture.” Only the last is the operational target.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto order is visibility, physical contact, biomechanics, and only then detection confidence. Visibility veto: if both objects are not simultaneously visible, tFFBO is undefined. The source’s off-camera failure is decisive: “Off-camera approaches produce no simultaneous player-dummy view, making grounding impossible regardless of prompt or threshold” [PAGE 7]. Physical veto: define \(V_{\mathrm{contact}}(t)=1[\mathrm{overlap}_t\ge 1\ \mathrm{pixel}]\). This is necessary but not sufficient for physical contact, because a 2D silhouette can overlap before force transfer. The paper’s approach-from-behind error is the proof: “in the final stride, the player’s image-plane silhouette overlaps the dummy before physical contact occurs, typically placing the prediction two to four frames early” [PAGE 7]. A depth-aware or dummy-motion check would be a stronger physical veto; the paper itself proposes “Replacing image-plane mask overlap with depth-aware contact verification” [PAGE 8].

Biomechanical veto: the ground-truth criterion requires that the player “physically collides with and moves the dummy” [PAGE 6]. If the dummy mask is stable and undeformed while the player mask merely grazes it, a biomechanical layer can veto the claim that contact has begun. The paper does not implement dummy-motion verification, so this remains an audit gap, not a claimed result. Weather and illumination veto: the source does not report a rain or snow variable, so a defensible weather-layer claim must be limited to illumination and camera degradation. The paper states: “Recording conditions vary substantially in background, viewing angle, distance to the drill, and illumination” [PAGE 5]. Figure 1 gives the extreme case: “Inverted camera orientation with motion blur and multi-player overlap onto the dummy” [PAGE 2]. Under low contrast, motion blur, or abrupt panning, Grounding DINO can lose one object and SAM2 can drift. The adjudication rule is therefore: if visibility fails, do not infer FPOC; if mask overlap fails, do not accept on confidence; if overlap succeeds but biomechanical evidence is ambiguous, flag for review rather than late-bind to a high detector score. Detection confidence is the weakest veto and should never override a visibility or physical-contact failure.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
