The conceptual lineage of "chain appetites" traces back to Darwin's observations of bee communication and insect nest-building, but gained systematic formulation through Konrad Lorenz and Nikolaas Tinbergen’s work on fixed action patterns (FAPs) in the mid-20th century. Hingston’s 1928 book predates this, positioning him as a precursor who emphasized the sequential, interdependent nature of instinctive acts—what he termed "rhythm." Tolman’s reinterpretation injects a crucial teleological element absent in pure ethology: the idea that each link serves a represented end-state, making the chain flexible rather than rigid.

This synthesis reflects broader scientific shifts: from Watsonian S-R chains (where responses elicit subsequent stimuli mechanically) to Hullian drive-reduction theory (where needs motivate behavior), culminating in Tolman’s purposive behaviorism. Tolman himself drew from Kantian philosophy regarding practical reason and ends, adapting it to sub-human cognition. His emphasis on "means-end readiness" anticipates modern reinforcement learning concepts like policy functions and option architectures.

Historically, debates raged between "instinct school" (Loeb, Woodworth) viewing behavior as automatic tropisms, and "cognitive school" (Tolman, Köhler) insisting on insight and representation. The *Eumenes conica* example became a battleground: FAP proponents cited its stereotypy as evidence against intelligence, while Tolmanians highlighted its adaptability to perturbations as proof of internal goals. This tension persists today in discussions of animal consciousness and AI alignment—whether complex behaviors require explicit models or can emerge from layered reflexes.

Multi-week traps arise when researchers misattribute causality: observing that a wasp repairs a damaged cell before continuing provisioning might be interpreted as "problem-solving," but could equally result from low-level sensory triggers (e.g., exposed silk activating builder programs). Without controlled experiments varying the type and location of damage, one cannot distinguish between genuine re-planning and stimulus-triggered fallback routines. Similarly, assuming all insects share the same chain logic ignores species-specific evolutionary pressures; bees exhibit different rhythms than wasps, suggesting divergent solutions to similar ecological problems.</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
