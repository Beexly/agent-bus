Eulenburg’s *Lehrbuch der Nervenkrankheiten* (1878, cited on [PAGE 1]) supplied the clinical nosology of nervous diseases in which anesthesia and movement sense could be dissociated. *Virchow’s Archiv* (Bd. XLVII, 1869, cited on [PAGE 1]) represents the pathological anatomy tradition linking joint inflammation to excruciating pain, which James imports as evidence of articular sensibility. Earlier in the century, Charles Bell’s distinction between sensory and anterior motor roots (1811) created the very category of “afferent” versus “efferent” that James’s exclusionary logic depends upon, though Bell is not cited on this page.

The multi-week trap in contemporary rehabilitation and sports science is the assumption that recovery of passive range-of-motion under therapist manipulation implies recovery of functional proprioceptive control. James’s own logic shows the opposite: passive discrimination can persist while active control is absent. A second trap is the inflammatory-pain confound: clinicians may interpret a patient’s guarding or pain behavior around a joint as evidence of precise joint-position awareness, when nociception and kinesthesis are separable channels. A third trap is the historical reification of “articular cartilage” as a sensory organ; mature cartilage is aneural, so the 19th-century locus shifted over subsequent decades toward joint capsule, Ruffini endings, Pacinian corpuscles, Golgi tendon organs, and muscle spindle primary/secondary afferents (Sherrington’s proprioception synthesis). Thus James’s page sits at a fork: it correctly rejects efference copy in passive movement, but incorrectly localizes the positive signal to articular cartilage rather than peri-articular connective tissue. The genealogical trap is to treat a 1890 exclusionary inference as an anatomical fact rather than a historically bounded hypothesis.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Model the observer (clinician or experimenter) as recursively modeling the patient’s model of limb position. Let \(M_P(\theta)\) be the patient’s internal estimate of joint angle \(\theta\), and \(M_O(M_P)\) the observer’s belief about that estimate. Under crisis—acute joint inflammation or severe pain—the patient’s habit regresses to a high-weight nociceptive prior: \(M_P(\theta) \approx f(P_{\text{noc}})\), where \(P_{\text{noc}}\) is nociceptive pressure rather than geometric angle. The observer then faces a recursive theory-of-mind problem: does the patient’s verbal report “I know where my limb is” reflect articular kinesthesis \(K(\theta)\) or pain-driven avoidance \(A(P_{\text{noc}})\)?

The counter-trap is to assume that because pain localizes “in or about the joints” [PAGE 1], the joint is encoding spatial geometry. That is a category error: nociception signals threat, not metric. The recursive regress deepens when the observer, aware of this counter-trap, overcorrects and assumes the patient has no joint sense at all, ignoring that passive movement discrimination may genuinely survive deafferentation of skin and muscle [PAGE 1]. The clock variable is temporal: nociceptive inflammation evolves over hours to days, whereas kinesthetic discrimination of passive flexion operates on a timescale of milliseconds to seconds. If the observer samples the patient’s report only during a painful crisis, the recursive model conflates slow affective pain with fast spatial perception. The correct recursion requires separate probes: (1) passive angle discrimination under local articular anesthesia, (2) active movement with efference copy intact, and (3) pain intensity ratings without spatial judgments. Without these, the observer’s model \(M_O\) is systematically mis-calibrated by the patient’s crisis habit regression.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto: mature articular cartilage is avascular and aneural; it cannot itself be the primary seat of discriminative sensibility. Therefore James’s claim that “articular cartilages” are sensitive [PAGE 1] is physically vetoed unless “articular surfaces” is read loosely to include the joint capsule, synovium, subchondral bone, and ligaments, all of which are innervated. Biomechanical veto: passive physician-imposed movement stretches capsule and ligaments in addition to compressing cartilage; thus preservation of passive kinesthesis under muscular anesthesia does not isolate cartilage mechanotransduction. Weather/environmental veto: barometric pressure changes alter intra-articular capsule pressure and can provoke arthritic pain independent of limb angle, introducing a confound \(P_{\text{atm}}(t)\) into any force-or-position judgment made “in or about the joints” [PAGE 1]. Adjudication: the only layer that survives veto is a distributed peri-articular afferent signal \(r_{\text{joint}}\) from capsule and ligament stretch, not articular cartilage proper. The formal veto condition is

\[
\forall t:\; \frac{\partial r_{\text{cartilage}}}{\partial \theta} = 0 \quad \text{(aneural cartilage)}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
I(\Theta; R_{\text{cartilage}}) = 0 \;\text{bits}.
\]

Any model assigning spatial information to cartilage alone is falsified. The surviving model assigns information to the capsule–ligament complex with units of bits per radian, subject to the inflammatory breakdown regime where nociceptor recruitment \(r_{\text{noc}}(P_{\text{cap}}, P_{\text{atm}})\) dominates and \(d'\) for angle discrimination collapses despite preserved pain localization.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Intra-articular anesthetic blockade. If the articular-surface hypothesis on [PAGE 1] is true, selectively anesthetizing the joint capsule and synovium while preserving cutaneous and muscular afferents should abolish accurate discrimination of passive limb flexion/extension. Prediction: if passive angle discrimination persists with \(d'\ge 1\) under confirmed articular anesthesia, the articular-surface hypothesis is vetoed; the signal must arise from extra-articular tissues (skin at the joint margin, tendon, or proximal muscle remnants).

Scenario 2: Tendon vibration illusion. Applying high-frequency vibration (80–100 Hz) to a tendon induces a powerful illusory sense of limb movement without corresponding joint-angle change or capsule deformation. Prediction: if subjects report veridical-feeling movement and position change while \(\theta\) is held constant and no articular surface is deformed, a purely articular geometric encoding is vetoed; muscle spindle Ia afferents, not joint surfaces, are driving the percept.

Scenario 3: Inflammatory dissociation. In acute monoarthritis, joint pain is excruciating [PAGE 1] yet passive kinesthetic discrimination may remain intact. Prediction: if pain intensity \(P_{\text{noc}}\) is high while \(I(\Theta;R)\) for passive angle remains above threshold (bits), then pain and kinesthesis are separate channels, vetoing James’s conflation of inflammatory joint sensitivity with the seat of spatial perception. Conversely, if \(I(\Theta;R)\to 0\) precisely when \(P_{\text{noc}}\) is maximal and recovers as inflammation subsides, a shared nociceptive–kinesthetic channel is supported rather than vetoed.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
