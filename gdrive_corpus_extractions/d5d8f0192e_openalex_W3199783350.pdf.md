Reference-standard neuroimaging (fMRI, PET) detects skull fractures and bleeding in severe TBI, but “only a minority of mTBI such as SRC display distinguishable structural changes immediately post concussion” [PAGE 2], so structural imaging cannot veto or confirm SRC alone.

Biomechanical layer: Balance, gait, and turning are impaired by SRC, but BESS under direct observation is subjective and assessor-dependent [PAGE 3]. Inertial wearables instrument iTUG and BESS with millisecond resolution, and multi-wearable iSCAT (wrists, legs, lower back) segments tandem walk and static balance [PAGE 3]. Turning is more sensitive to mTBI deficits than daily-activity measures [PAGE 7], yet “it is not yet known exactly what clinical gait or turning assessment techniques are sensitive for SRC,” creating a validation barrier [PAGE 3].

Environmental/conditions layer: Digital symptom apps require player attention and regular adherence, which “may not easily lend itself within free-living” settings [PAGE 5]. Eye-tracking outside controlled laboratory conditions “generates many complications,” including lighting, protocol standardization, and big-data analysis complexity [PAGE 4]. Free-living gait characteristics depend on “environment and ambulatory bout length” [PAGE 8], so uncontrolled conditions veto raw wearable outputs as diagnostic endpoints.

Adjudication rule: RTP clearance is vetoed unless (a) baseline \(\mathbf{x}_0\) exists, (b) at least two non-symptom modalities (e.g., inertial motor + objective visual) show concordance with post-injury state, and (c) environmental conditions are controlled or validated for free-living inference. “No single cognitive test/technology has proven capable for standalone use” [PAGE 2]; therefore any clearance resting on a single modality—particularly self-reported PCSS—fails the cross-layer veto.

Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios:

Scenario 1 — Low-resource amateur rugby, single medic, no baseline. Veto: SCAT5-only RTP clearance is invalid because \(\mathbf{x}_0 = \varnothing\); post-concussion scores cannot be decomposed into injury effect versus individual variability [PAGE 2]. The “Recognize & Remove” rule mandates removal, but without baseline or digital follow-up, the athlete re-enters contact sport on subjective judgment alone [PAGE 1]. Prediction: Missed SRC rate remains high; given 48% head-injury prevalence with only 17% self-report [PAGE 3–4], remote app-based symptom logging (CSX/C3) and post-match inertial screening are necessary to detect delayed deterioration [PAGE 4–5].

Scenario 2 — Eye-Sync deployed outside the clinic in free-living conditions. Veto: Although laboratory sensitivity/specificity are 88%/87% [PAGE 3], the absence of “standardized protocols to detect SRC eye-movement impairments” and complications of ambient lighting/adherence invalidate standalone field use [PAGE 4]. Prediction: Deploying Eye-Sync as a sole sideline diagnostic without controlled conditions will produce false positives and false negatives; it must be gated by laboratory confirmation and multimodal symptom/motor correlation.

Scenario 3 — Athlete manipulates PCSS baseline to accelerate RTP. Veto: Symptom-scale-only clearance is vetoed when self-report reliability is compromised by competitive incentive [PAGE 3]. Prediction: Premature RTP re-exposes the athlete to second-impact physiology, with “increased intracranial pressure and in extreme cases, death” [PAGE 1]; objective inertial gait/turning and oculomotor biomarkers must override falsified symptom scores before any RTP decision.
