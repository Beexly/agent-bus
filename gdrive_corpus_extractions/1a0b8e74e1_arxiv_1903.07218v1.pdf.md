The source is a Bayesian survival-and-Gaussian-process model for Test cricket batting careers. The multi-hop derivation begins with the hazard function: if \(X \in \{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}\) is runs scored, then \(H(x)\) is the conditional probability of dismissal on score \(x\), given survival to \(x\). The paper defines \(H(x) \in [0,1]\) as “the probability a batsman gets out on score x” [PAGE 3]. From that, the score distribution is \(P(X=x)=H(x)\prod_{a=0}^{x-1}[1-H(a)]\) [PAGE 3, Equation 1], i.e. survival through all lower scores and then dismissal at \(x\). The likelihood extends this to not-out scores by right-censoring: for a not-out score \(y\), the paper computes \(P(X \geq y)\), not \(P(X=y)\), because the innings ended without dismissal [PAGE 4]. That yields Equation 2 and the log-likelihood in Equation 3 [PAGE 4]. The hazard is then parameterized through an effective batting average \(\mu(x)\): \(H(x)=1/[\mu(x)+1]\) [PAGE 4, Equation 4], with \(\mu(x)=\mu_2+(\mu_1-\mu_2)\exp(-x/L)\) [PAGE 4, Equation 5]. Here \(\mu_1\) is initial ability at the start of an innings, \(\mu_2\) is “eye in” ability after adaptation, and \(L\) is the e-folding time in runs; the paper states \(L\) “signifies the number of runs scored for approximately 63% (formally \(1-1/e\)) of the transition between \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\) to take place” [PAGE 5]. The between-innings extension indexes \(\mu_2\) by career innings \(t\), giving \(\mu_{2t}\), and models \(\log(\mu_{2t})\) with a Gaussian process with mean \(m\), covariance \(K(X_i,X_j)\), scale \(\sigma\), and length \(\ell\) [PAGE 5]. The model is fitted by nested sampling with 1000 particles and 1000 MCMC steps per iteration [PAGE 6], and the posterior predictive function \(\nu(t)\) is the expected batting average in the \(t\)th career innings [PAGE 5].

The gray-zone audit is where the model can be over-read. First, \(x\) is runs, not balls faced, and \(t\) is innings, not calendar weeks. The GP length \(\ell\) is therefore an innings-scale smoothness parameter, not a biological recovery clock. Second, the hazard is score-dependent, not time-dependent: the model has no explicit ball-by-ball survival clock, no bowler quality, and no weather covariate. Third, not-outs are censored, so treating them as dismissals would bias hazard upward and effective average downward. Fourth, the model treats batting form as continuous rather than binary: the authors explicitly say “we can treat batting form as continuous, rather than binary” and “instead of defining players as ‘in’ or ‘out’ of form, we can describe players as improving or deteriorating in terms of batting ability” [PAGE 3]. Fifth, the paper finds that “the model appears to reject the idea of recent performances as having a significant impact on innings in the near future” and that “the effect of recent form varies greatly from player to player” [PAGE 8]. That is a posterior claim conditional on the score-only likelihood and GP prior, not a universal law. Sixth, the model explicitly ignores “the number of balls faced in each innings, as well as the strength of the opposition” and “treats all runs scored equally” [PAGE 8]. Therefore any inference about weather, biomechanics, fatigue, or opposition quality is outside the identified likelihood unless a new covariate model is specified. The singularity audit is also important: \(H(x)=1/[\mu(x)+1]\) is undefined at \(\mu=-1\), but the prior structure keeps \(\mu_1=C\mu_2\) and \(L=D\mu_2\) with positive \(\mu_2\), so the model domain is positive and \(H \in (0,1)\) almost surely. As \(L \to 0\), the within-innings transition collapses to an almost discontinuous jump at score zero; as \(L \to \infty\), the transition is not observed within finite scores and \(\mu_2\) becomes weakly identified. As \(\ell \to 0\), the GP treats innings as independent and \(\nu(t)\) loses career smoothness; as \(\ell \to \infty\), the GP forces all innings to share nearly one ability level and suppresses between-innings variation. As \(\sigma \to 0\), the GP also collapses to the mean \(m\). These are the formal breakdown regimes that a verifier should check before interpreting any “form” or “decline” claim.

P(X=x)=H(x)\prod_{a=0}^{x-1}[1-H(a)],
\]
where \(H(x)\) is “the probability a batsman gets out on score x” [PAGE 3, Equation 1]. For not-out scores, the source states: “in the case of not out scores, we compute \(P(X \geq x)\) as the likelihood, rather than \(P(X = x)\)” [PAGE 4]. With \(I\) total innings and \(N\) not-outs, the likelihood is
\[
p(\{x\},\{y\})=
\prod_{i=1}^{I-N}\left(H(x_i)\prod_{a=0}^{x_i-1}[1-H(a)]\right)
\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(\prod_{a=0}^{y_i-1}[1-H(a)]\right),
\]
and the log-likelihood is
\[
\log L(\theta)=
\sum_{i=1}^{I-N}\log H(x_i)
+\sum_{i=1}^{I-N}\sum_{a=0}^{x_i-1}\log[1-H(a)]
+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{a=0}^{y_i-1}\log[1-H(a)].
\]
These are [PAGE 4, Equations 2 and 3].

The hazard parameterization is
\[
H(x)=\frac{1}{\mu(x)+1},
\]
with
\[
\mu(x)=\mu_2+(\mu_1-\mu_2)\exp\left(-\frac{x}{L}\right),
\]
as given in [PAGE 4, Equations 4 and 5]. The source defines \(\mu_1\) as “a player’s initial batting ability when beginning a new innings,” \(\mu_2\) as the “‘eye in’ batting ability once used to the specific match conditions,” and \(L\) as “the e-folding time” [PAGE 5]. At \(x=0\), \(\mu(0)=\mu_1\); as \(x\to\infty\), \(\mu(x)\to\mu_2\). Therefore \(H(0)=1/(\mu_1+1)\) and \(\lim_{x\to\infty}H(x)=1/(\mu_2+1)\). The between-innings quantity is
\[
\nu(t)=\text{expected batting average in }t\text{th career innings},
\]
as stated in [PAGE 5, Equation 7]. The model places a Gaussian process on the log of the innings-specific eye-in ability:
\[
\log(\mu_{2t})\sim \mathrm{GP}\bigl(m,K(X_i,X_j;\sigma,\ell)\bigr).
\]
The paper says “the set of \(\{\mu_{2t}\}\) terms are modelled using a Gaussian process” and that the covariance function is “the commonly used squared exponential covariance, which contains scale and length parameters \(\sigma\) and \(\ell\)” [PAGE 5]. The priors are
\[
\mu_1\leftarrow C\mu_2,\quad
L\leftarrow D\mu_2,\quad
C\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1,2),\quad
D\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1,5),
\]
\[
\log(\mu_{2t})\sim \mathrm{GP}(m,K),\quad
m\sim\mathrm{Lognormal}(\log(25),0.75^2),\quad
\sigma\sim\mathrm{Exponential}(10),\quad
\ell\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,100),
\]
as listed in [PAGE 6]. These priors keep \(\mu_1\), \(L\), and \(\mu_{2t}\) positive; the paper notes “as we are measuring ability in terms of a batting average (which must be positive), we model \(\log(\mu_{2t})\), rather than just \(\mu_{2t}\), to ensure positivity in our estimates” [PAGE 6].

Breakdown and singularity regimes are explicit. The hazard is undefined at \(\mu=-1\), but that point is outside the prior support because \(\mu_1=C\mu_2\), \(L=D\mu_2\), and the GP is on \(\log(\mu_{2t})\). As \(L\to 0\), \(\exp(-x/L)\to 0\) for every \(x>0\), so the within-innings curve becomes a near-discontinuity: \(\mu(0)=\mu_1\) and \(\mu(x)\approx\mu_2\) immediately thereafter. As \(L\to\infty\), \(\exp(-x/L)\to 1\) over finite scores, so \(\mu(x)\approx\mu_1\) and the eye-in parameter \(\mu_2\) is weakly identified from within-innings scores. As \(\ell\to 0\), the GP covariance between distinct innings vanishes, so \(\mu_{2t}\) behaves as independent innings effects and \(\nu(t)\) has no smooth career trajectory. As \(\ell\to\infty\), all innings become nearly perfectly correlated and the model collapses toward a constant batting-ability level. As \(\sigma\to 0\), the GP collapses to the mean \(m\). Finally, the high-dimensional setting is handled by nested sampling with “1000 particles” and “1000 MCMC steps per nested sampling iteration” [PAGE 6]; failure to converge there would make marginal-likelihood comparisons unreliable.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from geometric-score models to hazard models to Bayesian within-innings survival models to this Gaussian-process career model. The paper cites Elderton and Wood’s 1945 “Cricket scores and geometrical progression” [PAGE 9, reference 10], then notes that later work found geometric assumptions fail because of “the inflated number of scores of 0 that are present in many players’ career records” [PAGE 2], citing Kimber and Hansford and Bracewell and Ruggiero [PAGE 2]. Kimber and Hansford used nonparametric hazard functions, and Cai et al. used parametric hazard functions, to measure how dismissal probabilities change with score [PAGE 2]. Stevenson and Brewer’s 2017 Bayesian survival model estimated a smooth hazard function and found “overwhelming evidence to suggest that players perform with decreased batting abilities early in an innings and improve as they score runs” [PAGE 2]. This paper extends that within-innings model by adding a Gaussian process over innings, so that “we can not only measure and predict how player batting abilities fluctuate during an innings, but also between innings, over the course of entire playing careers” [PAGE 3]. The GP lineage is Rasmussen and Williams [PAGE 5, reference 14], and the computational lineage is Skilling’s nested sampling [PAGE 6, reference 15].

The retrocausal genealogy matters because later modeling choices reinterpret older statistics. The batting average is retained as an intuitive scale, but the paper says “the batting average fails to tell us about variations in a player’s batting ability” [PAGE 2]. Thus the older average is not rejected; it is decomposed into a within-innings effective average, a between-innings effective average \(\nu(t)\), and a GP prior over innings. The hidden-Markov “in form / out of form” model of Koulis et al. is also reinterpreted: the paper notes that the HMM requires “an explicit specification of what constitutes an out of form state” and that the authors define a state with posterior expected median runs less than 25 as out of form [PAGE 3]. This paper replaces that binary state with continuous \(\nu(t)\), saying “we can treat batting form as continuous, rather than binary” [PAGE 3].

The multi-week traps are chiefly clock errors. First, \(\ell\) is uniform on \((0,100)\) in innings, not weeks [PAGE 6]; a “multi-week” dip in scores is not the same as a short \(\ell\) in innings. Second, the paper forecasts “the next 20 innings” [PAGE 7], not the next 20 weeks. Third, the paper reports that public perceptions of form may be overestimated, since for many players “scores able to be modelled using a random sequence” [PAGE 3]; a few low scores over a multi-week interval are weak evidence of a persistent ability change. Fourth, the conclusion says “the model appears to reject the idea of recent performances as having a significant impact on innings in the near future” and that “the effect of recent form varies greatly from player to player” [PAGE 8]; this blocks a universal “form persists for weeks” inference. Fifth, not-out censoring is a trap: ignoring it would make a player’s record look worse and could mimic decline [PAGE 4]. Sixth, the model ignores balls faced and opposition strength [PAGE 8], so a multi-week interpretation that attributes a run of low scores to “better bowlers” or “bad weather” is not identified by this likelihood. The correct genealogy is therefore: geometric score model, hazard model, Bayesian survival model, GP career model, with each step narrowing the inferential target to score-only, innings-indexed ability under censoring.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression is the recursive error in which a player, coach, or selector observes a short run of low scores, infers a persistent loss of ability, and then acts as if the inference were a stable state. In the source, the model’s between-innings term \(\nu(t)\) is a smooth GP-based expected batting average, not a binary “in form / out of form” label. The paper explicitly moves from binary to continuous: “instead of defining players as ‘in’ or ‘out’ of form, we can describe players as improving or deteriorating in terms of batting ability” [PAGE 3]. Crisis habit regression would re-binarize that continuous posterior and overreact to local dips. The source’s warning is direct: public perceptions of form “tend to be overestimated, with many players’ scores able to be modelled using a random sequence” [PAGE 3], and “the effect of recent form varies greatly from player to player” [PAGE 8]. Thus the recursive model should not assume that a player’s self-model, the coach’s model of the player, and the selector’s model of the coach’s model all share the same “form” state. They may share the same score data but impose different priors on persistence.

The counter-trap is to treat the GP posterior predictive \(\nu(t)\) as a verdict rather than a distribution. The paper’s Table 1 gives predicted effective averages for the next innings and notes that the model can make statements such as “we expect Steve Smith to outscore Virat Kohli by 5.1 runs in their next respective innings” and “Steve Smith has a 68.8% chance of outscoring Virat Kohli in their next respective innings” [PAGE 8]. These are probabilistic comparisons under the model, not fixed ability ranks. The counter-trap is to collapse the 68% credible interval into a single rank or to treat a one-innings forecast as a career judgment. A selector’s recursive model should ask: does the posterior for \(\nu(t)\) separate this player from alternatives after accounting for the GP length scale and the 68% interval? If not, the selection decision is not licensed by the model.

The clock is the third recursive constraint. There are two clocks in the source: the within-innings score clock \(x\), with e-folding length \(L\) in runs, and the between-innings career clock \(t\), with GP length \(\ell\) in innings. Neither is a calendar-week clock. The paper says \(L\) “signifies the number of runs scored for approximately 63% ... of the transition between \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\)” [PAGE 5], and the GP prior on \(\ell\) is uniform from 0 to 100 innings [PAGE 6]. Therefore a “multi-week crisis” is not a model object. A player who has three low-score innings in three weeks has not necessarily moved \(t\) enough to update \(\nu(t)\) strongly if \(\ell\) is long. Conversely, if \(\ell\) is short, the GP permits rapid between-innings variation, but that is an innings-scale statement, not a medical or psychological diagnosis. The recursive theory of mind should therefore use the model’s own clocks: within-innings adaptation via \(L\), between-innings trajectory via \(\ell\), and posterior predictive intervals for the next innings. It should resist the crisis habit of turning a local score sequence into a global identity, the counter-trap of turning a posterior comparison into a certainty, and the clock error of replacing innings with weeks.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is proven by the likelihood’s argument list. The source likelihood depends on scores \(\{x\}\), not-out scores \(\{y\}\), and the hazard parameters \(\theta=\{\mu_1,\{\mu_{2t}\},L,m,\sigma,\ell\}\) [PAGE 5]. It contains no weather vector \(w\), no biomechanical vector \(b\), no ball-by-ball count \(n\), and no opposition-strength vector \(s\). Therefore, by construction,
\[
\frac{\partial \log L(\theta)}{\partial w}=0,\qquad
\frac{\partial \log L(\theta)}{\partial b}=0
\]
for the model as specified. Weather is mentioned only as an informal within-innings factor: the paper refers to “short-term changes in ability that are observed during or within a single innings, due to factors such as adapting to the local pitch and weather conditions” [PAGE 2]. That sentence motivates the within-innings hazard shape, but it does not introduce a weather covariate into Equations 1 through 5. Likewise, physical and biomechanical variables are absent from the parameterization. The paper’s future-work sentence is an explicit admission: “we have ignored important variables, such as the number of balls faced in each innings, as well as the strength of the opposition. Currently, the model treats all runs scored equally” [PAGE 8].

The veto adjudication is therefore three-layered. At the statistical layer, the model can speak to score-indexed hazard and innings-indexed effective average. At the biomechanical layer, it cannot identify fatigue, injury, technique change, or load, because no such variables enter the likelihood; any claim that a player declined because of a biomechanical cause is a post hoc narrative, not a model estimate. At the weather layer, it cannot identify pitch or atmospheric effects, because the only weather language is qualitative [PAGE 2] and no weather parameter appears in \(\theta\). A cross-layer claim is admissible only if it is reformulated as a testable extension: add a covariate to \(H(x)\) or to \(\log(\mu_{2t})\), refit by nested sampling, and compare marginal likelihoods. Until then, the correct adjudication is veto: the source model neither proves nor disproves weather or biomechanical causation. It only estimates how batting ability, measured as an effective average, varies with score and innings under a censored score-only likelihood.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Not-out recoding counterfactual. Verifier check: inspect Equation 2 and Equation 3 and confirm that not-out innings contribute a survival product, not a dismissal term. The source states “in the case of not out scores, we compute \(P(X \geq x)\) as the likelihood, rather than \(P(X = x)\)” [PAGE 4]. Prediction: if a counterfactual model rewrites every not-out \(y\) as an observed dismissal at \(y\), the estimated hazard at \(y\) will rise and the posterior effective average \(\nu(t)\) will fall, especially for players with many not-outs. Veto: any claim that ignores censoring is not source-faithful and should be rejected by the verifier.

Scenario 2: Binary-form counterfactual. Verifier check: confirm that the source uses a Gaussian process on \(\log(\mu_{2t})\) and that the paper says “we can treat batting form as continuous, rather than binary” [PAGE 3]. The HMM alternative required an explicit out-of-form threshold, with median runs less than 25 defined as out of form [PAGE 3]. Prediction: replacing the continuous GP with a two-state “in/out” model will produce stepwise posterior trajectories and overstate switching; if the smooth GP has better marginal likelihood under nested sampling, the binary model is vetoed. The source’s nested-sampling setup [PAGE 6] makes this a verifiable model-comparison test, not a rhetorical preference.

Scenario 3: Covariate-omission counterfactual. Verifier check: confirm the source’s admission that it ignores “the number of balls faced in each innings, as well as the strength of the opposition” and “treats all runs scored equally” [PAGE 8]. Prediction: adding balls-faced and opposition-strength covariates will revise \(\nu(t)\) downward for players whose high averages were built on slow scoring or weak attacks, and upward for players who scored quickly against strong attacks. A second prediction is that a weather or biomechanical covariate will have no identified coefficient in the current likelihood because those variables are absent from \(\theta\); any apparent effect must come from a new model, not from the source. Veto: any comparative claim that “player A is better than player B” based only on raw runs, without these covariates, is a source-limited claim and should be flagged as such.
