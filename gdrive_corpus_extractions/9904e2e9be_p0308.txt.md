### 1. Carathéodory’s Measurability Criterion
From entry [12]: “C. CARATHÉODORY, Vorlesungen über reelle Funktionen, Leipzig-Berlin, 1927.”
This work lays the foundation for modern measure theory. The central operator is an **outer measure** \( m^* \) defined on the power set of a set \( X \). A subset \( E \subseteq X \) is declared **measurable** if for every test set \( A \subseteq X \):
\[
m^*(A) = m^*(A \cap E) + m^*(A \setminus E).
\]
**Units**: Outer measure \( m^* \) inherits units from the context (e.g., length in meters, mass in kilograms). In pure mathematics, it is often treated as dimensionless, representing "size" or "count".
**Assumptions**: \( m^* \) is countably subadditive and monotone. The space \( X \) is arbitrary.
**Breakdown Regime**: If \( m^* \) is not regular (i.e., not approximable by open/closed sets), the resulting σ-algebra may not contain all Borel sets. Non-measurable sets exist under the Axiom of Choice, leading to paradoxes like Vitali sets.

### 2. Haar Measure on Locally Compact Groups
From entries [3], [4], [13]: 
- “W. AMBROSE, Measures on locally compact topological groups, Trans. A.M.S. 61 (1947) 106–121.”
- “H. CARTAN, Sur la mesure de Haar, C. R. Acad. Sci. Paris 211 (1940) 759–762.”

Let \( G \) be a locally compact Hausdorff group. There exists a non-zero regular Borel measure \( \mu \), called **left Haar measure**, satisfying:
\[
\int_G f(xg) \, d\mu(x) = \int_G f(x) \, d\mu(x) \quad \forall f \in C_c(G), \forall g \in G,
\]
where \( C_c(G) \) denotes continuous functions with compact support. Right Haar measure is defined analogously.
**Units**: The measure \( \mu \) is typically normalized so that \( \mu(K) = 1 \) for some compact set \( K \), making it dimensionless. In applications (e.g., physics), it may carry units of "group volume".
**Assumptions**: \( G \) must be locally compact and Hausdorff. Uniqueness holds up to a positive scalar multiple.
**Breakdown Regime**: For non-locally compact groups (e.g., infinite-dimensional Lie groups), Haar measure may not exist or be unique. Also, if \( G \) is not unimodular, left and right Haar measures differ by a modular function \( \Delta: G \to \mathbb{R}^+ \), where \( d\mu_{right}(x) = \Delta(x)^{-1} d\mu_{left}(x) \).

**Units**: If \( f \) represents a physical density (e.g., kg/m³), then \( \|f\|_p \) has units of \( [\text{density}] \cdot [\text{volume}]^{1/p} \). In information theory, for \( p=1 \), it yields entropy in nats or bits.

Georg Cantor’s work in the 1870s–1890s on sets, cardinality, and derived sets provided tools to classify discontinuities. ### Borel and the Birth of Measure Theory
Émile Borel (1898) defined what we now call Borel sets—the smallest σ-algebra containing intervals—and constructed a measure on them (Borel measure) for subsets of ℝ. His motivation was probability: he wanted to assign probabilities to complex events (like limsup of sequences) in a consistent way. Borel’s normal number theorem (1909, cited as [9]) used measure theory to show that almost all real numbers are normal, illustrating the power of “almost everywhere” statements.

### Lebesgue and the Integral Reborn
Henri Lebesgue (1901–1904) revolutionized integration by measuring the range instead of the domain. For a function \( f \), he partitioned the y-axis, measured the preimage sets (now measurable sets), and summed \( y_i \cdot \mu(f^{-1}(y_i)) \). This allowed integration of highly discontinuous functions and yielded powerful convergence theorems (Monotone, Dominated). Crucially, Lebesgue measure is complete (contains all subsets of null sets), unlike Borel measure.

### Carathéodory’s Axiomatization
Constantin Carathéodory (1914, expanded 1927 [12]) generalized Lebesgue’s construction by starting with an outer measure \( m^* \) on any set \( X \). His measurability condition (discussed earlier) ensured that measurable sets form a σ-algebra and that \( m^* \) restricted to them is countably additive. This abstraction liberated measure theory from Euclidean space, enabling applications to abstract spaces (e.g., function spaces, groups).

Specifically, if \(\mu_L\) is left-invariant, then \(\mu_R(E) = \mu_L(E^{-1})\) is right-invariant, and \(d\mu_R(x) = \Delta(x)^{-1} d\mu_L(x)\). Using \(\mu_L\) as if it were bi-invariant leads to incorrect formulas.

Example: On \(G = \{(a,b) : a>0, b\in\mathbb{R}\}\) with multiplication \((a,b)(c,d) = (ac, ad+b)\), left Haar measure is \(d\mu_L = \frac{da\,db}{a^2}\), right Haar measure is \(d\mu_R = \frac{da\,db}{a}\). The modular function is \(\Delta(a,b) = a\).

**Prediction**: If one computes the integral of a function that is not central (not constant on conjugacy classes) using the wrong measure, results will be inconsistent under inversion or adjoint actions. Specifically, \(\int f(gx) d\mu_L(x) = \int f(x) d\mu_L(x)\) but \(\int f(xg) d\mu_L(x) = \Delta(g) \int f(x) d\mu_L(x) \neq \int f(x) d\mu_L(x)\) unless \(\Delta(g)=1\).

**Verification Method**: Calculate the modular function explicitly for the group. Test invariance numerically: pick a test function φ and group element g, compare \(\int φ(xg) dμ(x)\) versus \(\int φ(x) dμ(x)\). Discrepancy indicates non-unimodularity. Algebraically, derive Δ from the determinant of the adjoint representation.

### Scenario 3: Assuming All Groups Admit Haar Measure
**Reasoning Chain**: Encountering a topological group \(G\) (possibly infinite-dimensional), one assumes there exists a non-trivial regular Borel measure invariant under translations, citing “Haar measure”.

**Failure Condition**: Haar measure exists and is unique (up to scaling) **only** for locally compact Hausdorff groups. Moreover, even if a translation-invariant measure exists, it may not be regular or σ-finite.
