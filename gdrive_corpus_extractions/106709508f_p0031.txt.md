Formally, this is expressed as:
$$ \overline{E} := \limsup_{n \to \infty} E_n = \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty \bigcup_{k=n}^\infty E_k $$
**Units:** The output is a subset of $X$. If $\mu$ is a probability measure, $\mu(\overline{E}) \in [0,1]$ represents the probability that the event occurs infinitely often (i.o.).

Formally:
$$ \underline{E} := \liminf_{n \to \infty} E_n = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \bigcap_{k=n}^\infty E_k $$
**Units:** Similarly, $\mu(\underline{E})$ is the probability that the event occurs eventually (for all sufficiently large $n$).

**3. Limit Existence and Monotonicity:**
The text states: "If it so happens that $\overline{E} = \underline{E}$, we shall use the notation $\lim_{n \to \infty} E_n$ for this set." [PAGE 1]
Furthermore: "It is easy to verify that if $\{E_n\}$ is a monotone sequence, then $\lim_{n \to \infty} E_n$ exists and is equal to $\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n$ or $\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty E_n$ according as the sequence is increasing or decreasing." [PAGE 1]
*   Increasing ($E_1 \subset E_2 \subset \dots$): $\lim_{n \to \infty} E_n = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n$.
*   Decreasing ($E_1 \supset E_2 \supset \dots$): $\lim_{n \to \infty} E_n = \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty E_n$.

**4. Complement Algebra:**
The text introduces the complement: "The complement of a subset $E$ of $X$ is the set of all those points of $X$ which do not belong to $E$; it will be denoted by $E'$." [PAGE 1]
De Morgan's laws provide the critical link between limits and complements:
$$ (\overline{E})' = \left( \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty \bigcup_{k=n}^\infty E_k \right)' = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \bigcap_{k=n}^\infty E_k' = \underline{E'} $$
Thus, $\limsup E_n = (\liminf E_n')'$. This duality is essential for proving results about one limit via the other.

**5. Indicator Function Representation:**
To analyze convergence, we map sets to functions. Define the indicator function $\mathbf{1}_E: X \to \{0,1\}$ by $\mathbf{1}_E(x) = 1$ if $x \in E$, else $0$. Then:
$$ \limsup_{n \to \infty} \mathbf{1}_{E_n}(x) = \mathbf{1}_{\overline{E}}(x) \quad \text{and} \quad \liminf_{n \to \infty} \mathbf{1}_{E_n}(x) = \mathbf{1}_{\underline{E}}(x) $$
Convergence of sets $\lim_{n \to \infty} E_n = E$ is equivalent to pointwise convergence of indicators $\mathbf{1}_{E_n} \to \mathbf{1}_E$ everywhere.

The notions of $\limsup$ and $\liminf$ for numeric sequences were developed to handle cases where the ordinary limit does not exist. These numeric limits describe the largest and smallest accumulation points of a sequence.

2.  **Cantor Set Theory (1870s-1880s):** Georg Cantor’s work on infinite sets provided the language of subsets, unions, and intersections. He introduced derived sets and perfect sets, laying the groundwork for discussing points that appear "infinitely often" within a constructed set hierarchy.

3.  **Borel and Lebesgue Measure (Early 20th Century):** Émile Borel, in his study of geometric probability, needed to classify subsets of the real line. He defined the Borel $\sigma$-algebra as the smallest class containing intervals and closed under countable unions and intersections. Within this framework, he encountered sets like the lim sup of a sequence of intervals. His famous first Borel-Cantelli lemma (1909) directly uses the set-theoretic lim sup: if $\sum \mu(E_n) < \infty$, then $\mu(\overline{E}) = 0$. This connected the abstract set operation to a concrete probabilistic conclusion about recurrence.

4.  **Halmos’s Synthesis (1950s):** Paul Halmos’s "Measure Theory" [PAGE 1] systematized these ideas. By placing the definitions of $\overline{E}$ and $\underline{E}$ early in the text (§3), he highlights their role as the fundamental building blocks for continuity of measure, convergence theorems (Monotone Convergence, Dominated Convergence), and the construction of integrals. The treatment of monotone sequences as a sufficient condition for limit existence is a direct generalization of the monotone convergence theorem for functions.

**Multi-Week Traps in Learning:**
Students often encounter several persistent traps when mastering these concepts:
*   **Trap 1: Confusion with Numeric Limits.** The intuition from $\limsup a_n$ (the largest subsequential limit) does not perfectly map to $\limsup E_n$ (the set of points in infinitely many $E_n$). One might incorrectly assume that if $\mu(E_n) \to L$, then $\mu(\overline{E}) = L$. This is false; consider $E_n = [0,1]$ for even $n$ and $E_n = [2,3]$ for odd $n$. *   **Trap 4: Ignoring Measurability of the Limit.** While the $\sigma$-algebra property ensures $\overline{E}$ and $\underline{E}$ are measurable if each $E_n$ is, students sometimes apply these operations in contexts (e.g., arbitrary filters) where the resulting set may not belong to the original collection of sets under consideration.
