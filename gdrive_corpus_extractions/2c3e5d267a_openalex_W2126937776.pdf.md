\]
with the conventional conversion $1\ \mathrm{MET} = 3.5\ \mathrm{ml\ O_2 \cdot kg^{-1} \cdot min^{-1}}$. Mechanical power is approximated by
\[
P_g = \dot{V}O_{2,g} \cdot m \cdot \epsilon_{O_2} \quad [\mathrm{W}],
\]
where $\epsilon_{O_2} \approx 20.1\ \mathrm{J/ml\ O_2}$ is the caloric equivalent of oxygen.

\[
D^{(p)} = \sum_{g=1}^{N_p} f_{exo,g}^{(p)} \cdot t_{exo,g}^{(p)} \cdot \mathrm{MET}_g \quad [\mathrm{MET \cdot min \cdot week^{-1}}].
\]

T_{WHO} = 30\ \mathrm{min \cdot day^{-1}} \times 5\ \mathrm{days \cdot week^{-1}} = 150\ \mathrm{min \cdot week^{-1}}

\[

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock:

Crisis habit regression: Profile B respondents (2, 4) began exercising actively only in adulthood after previously negative attitudes toward physical activity [PAGE 75]. Respondent 4 had not exercised for more than 40 years; the group-exercise threshold was psychologically prohibitive (“leader is more than eager busybody”). Wii Fit Plus provided a private, low-threshold entry: “you did not have to take it so seriously” [PAGE 67–68]. This is a habit-regression intervention: exergames collapse the social-evaluation threat that blocks group exercise, allowing self-efficacy to accrue through solo play [PAGE 90–92].

Counter-trap: Profile A respondents (3, 5, 6) are already heavy sports users (5–6 times/week) for whom digital sports games do not replace real-world physical activity and have no impact on real-world behavior [PAGE 74]. The counter-trap is substitution: an already-active user may replace outdoor MVPA with lower-intensity exergame play, reducing net energy expenditure. Owen et al.’s interpretation—active people replace MVPA with exergames—is the formal version of this counter-trap [PAGE 18]. Profile A users expect “true sports performance” and reject under-challenging games [PAGE 74, 78], so they use exergames as variety and maintenance, not substitution; however, if challenge is insufficient, they disengage rather than regress.

Clock: The temporal operator is multi-week adherence. Women report time management as a barrier to both gaming and real-world exercise [PAGE 90]; respondents note games are slow to start, with excessive clicking and breaks between activities [PAGE 78–79]. Playing for entertainment lasts longer per session than playing for fitness [PAGE 95], creating a duration clock: entertainment sessions (1–2 hours) can exceed fitness sessions (0.5–1 hour) [PAGE 74–76]. The recursive loop is: novelty $\rightarrow$ initial adherence $\rightarrow$ challenge ceiling $\rightarrow$ boredom $\rightarrow$ attrition (Barnett et al.) [PAGE 17, 91]. The clock resets only if game design adds levels, feedback, interactivity, and hardware integration (heart-rate monitor, Balance Board + Kinect fusion) as respondents requested [PAGE 84–85].

Theory-of-mind nesting: The researcher attributes to respondents a second-degree interpretation that games are “only a factor that makes a change” (respondent 2) or “real exercise” if developed for exercise (respondent 5) [PAGE 64, 70]. Respondents model the game as a personal trainer (respondent 2, 5) [PAGE 63, 69], while the researcher models respondents as self-interpreting subjects whose first-degree accounts must be transformed into theoretical interpretations [PAGE 60–61].

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof:

Physical layer (metabolic): The absolute veto is intensity ceiling. Miyachi et al.: 0% of 46 Wii activities exceeded 6 METs; 67% were below 3 METs [PAGE 18–19]. Barnett et al.: none reached more than 6 METs [PAGE 17–18]. Therefore, for users whose fitness requires vigorous activity, exergames alone are physically insufficient to meet ACSM/AHA vigorous thresholds (20 minutes, 3 times/week) [PAGE 12]. Veto condition: $\forall g \in \mathcal{L}, \mathrm{MET}_g < 6 \implies \neg \text{VigorousThreshold}$.

Biomechanical layer: The Wiimote is described as clumsy, stiff, difficult to hold, and interfering with gaming [PAGE 77–78]. Respondent 8: “Nintendo Wii can be played only with a small wrist movement but PlayStation Move makes the player really move” [PAGE 78]. Kinect offers whole-body freedom but its motion sensor is disturbed when someone else walks near the play area; user identification sometimes fails and movements are ignored [PAGE 78]. Wii Fit yoga does not reach the challenge of real yoga; movements are hard to do efficiently compared to real-world yoga [PAGE 81]. Veto condition: sensor occlusion or controller mismatch $\implies$ broken proprioceptive feedback loop $\implies$ adherence decay.

Environmental/weather layer: Respondent 8 values indoor play: “It is important that I do not always have to go out to exercise, instead, I can do it in my living room, rain or shine” [PAGE 72]. This removes the weather veto for exercise but introduces a space veto: sufficient room for whole-body movement is required, and Kinect fails under occlusion [PAGE 78]. The weather veto is thus transferred from atmospheric conditions to domestic spatial constraints.

Avatar/psychophysical layer: Respondents with high self-image enjoy seeing themselves on screen; those lacking self-confidence may find it “terrible, disgusting,” stopping play altogether [PAGE 83–84]. Song et al.: digital sport game playing works positively for low body-image players with avatars, but willingness to play decreases if a person is unsatisfied with body image and sees themselves (Kinect) [PAGE 18–19]. Veto condition: body dissatisfaction $\times$ self-realistic avatar $\implies$ play rejection.

Adjudication hierarchy: Physical (MET) veto is absolute for performance outcomes; biomechanical veto is conditional on hardware (Wiimote vs Kinect vs Move); weather veto is inverted indoors but replaced by spatial veto; avatar veto operates on the motivational layer and can nullify even biomechanically adequate systems.
