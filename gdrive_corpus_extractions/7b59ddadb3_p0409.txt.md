<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The genealogy of this formalism traces back to the early 20th-century behaviorist movement, particularly through Clark L. Hull's efforts to create an axiomatic system for psychology. His work was influenced by earlier figures such as Pavlov (classical conditioning) and Thorndike (law of effect), but Hull sought to introduce mathematical rigor akin to physics. The use of differential equations and algebraic symbols was unprecedented in psychology at the time, aiming to predict behavior based on quantifiable variables like drive, habit strength, and incentive motivation.

Historically, this approach dominated mid-century experimental psychology, shaping research paradigms in learning theory. However, it faced challenges from cognitive revolutions in the 1950s-60s, which emphasized internal mental states over observable behaviors. Despite this, Hullian concepts persist in modern neuroscience (e.g., habit formation circuits) and computational modeling (reinforcement learning algorithms). Multi-week traps might arise from overgeneralizing laboratory findings to naturalistic settings or ignoring individual differences in learning rates. The legacy includes both rigorous methodological standards and cautionary tales about reductionism in complex systems.
