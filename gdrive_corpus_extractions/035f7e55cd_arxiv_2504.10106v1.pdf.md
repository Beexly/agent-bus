The paper’s reasoning chain is a multi-stage inference pipeline: field-line annotations are converted into camera calibration estimates; calibrated cameras are paired across synchronized views; 2D ball annotations are triangulated into 3D labels; monocular ball localization is then benchmarked using detected ball size and calibrated geometry; finally, bounding boxes are optimized so that their pixel dimensions agree with the recovered 3D scene. The foundational operator is the calibrated pinhole camera, where a world point \(X \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is mapped into pixel coordinates by a \(3\times4\) projection matrix. In the source, this is written as \(P = KR[I \mid -t]\) [PAGE 3], with \(R \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3}\), \(t \in \mathbb{R}^3\), and \(K \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3}\). The unit discipline matters: \(R\) is dimensionless orthogonal, \(t\) is in meters if the soccer-field model is expressed in meters, and \(K\) contains focal lengths in pixels, principal point coordinates in pixels, and a dimensionless skew term.

The first gray-zone derivation concerns triangulation. The source states that “triangulation seeks an optimal 3D point that best fits the observed 2D detections” [PAGE 3]. Formally, for two cameras \(i,j\) with projection matrices \(P_i,P_j\) and observed pixel points \(\bar p_i,\bar p_j\), the triangulated point \(p_{ij}\) can be defined as the minimizer of summed reprojection residuals:
\[
p_{ij}=\arg\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}^3}\sum_{c\in\{i,j\}}\left\|\pi(P_c\tilde X)-\bar p_c\right\|_2^2,
\]
where \(\pi(\cdot)\) divides homogeneous image coordinates by their third component, and the residual has units of pixels. The dataset generator then averages valid pair estimates:
\[
p=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\le i,j\le C,\;e_{ij}<\tau}p_{ij},
\]
matching Eq. (4) on [PAGE 3]. This is a practical estimator, but it is not a full maximum-likelihood or covariance-weighted solution. The implicit assumption is that valid pairs have sufficiently comparable uncertainty. That assumption becomes fragile under small parallax. The source explicitly warns: “triangulations with small parallax angles β—the angle between the two projection rays—exhibit higher uncertainty despite potentially yielding low reprojection errors” [PAGE 3]. This is the central geometric gray zone.

A standard small-angle uncertainty argument makes the failure regime precise. For a calibrated pair with effective perpendicular baseline \(B_\perp\) in meters, focal length \(f\) in pixels, scene depth \(Z\) in meters, and pixel-level localization noise \(\sigma_{px}\) in pixels, the depth uncertainty scales approximately as
\[
\sigma_Z \approx \frac{Z^2}{f\,B_\perp}\sigma_{px},
\]
under assumptions of small distortion, adequate calibration, and a locally fronto-parallel geometry. Units check: \(\mathrm{m}^2/(\mathrm{px}\cdot\mathrm{m})\cdot\mathrm{px}=\mathrm{m}\). The implication is that a small baseline or a nearly collinear camera pair can produce a low reprojection error while still having a large depth variance. Therefore, a reprojection-error threshold \(\tau\) alone is not sufficient for safe 3D annotation generation. The paper acknowledges a related issue by requiring “a minimum camera displacement of 1 meter between frames” [PAGE 4], but that filter is still incomplete because displacement magnitude alone does not guarantee a large parallax angle relative to the ball direction. A 1-meter baseline can be weak if both cameras observe the ball from almost the same ray direction.

The second gray-zone derivation concerns monocular localization. The source adopts a ball-size prior. If \(d\) is the detected ball diameter in pixels and \(\phi\) is the true ball diameter in meters, the calibrated monocular estimate follows Eq. (7) on [PAGE 4]:
\[
p=R^\top\frac{\phi\,p_c}{\|p_c^+-p_c^-\|}+t.
\]
Here \(p_c\) and \(p_c^\pm\) are camera-coordinate ray vectors obtained by inverse intrinsic mapping. The singularity is immediate: as the detected pixel diameter \(d\) approaches zero, the denominator \(\|p_c^+-p_c^-\|\) approaches zero, and the estimated depth diverges. More importantly, multiplicative error in \(d\) becomes multiplicative error in depth. The paper’s sensitivity analysis confirms this: “the 3D localization error is highly sensitive to variations in the predicted ball size, ranging from approximately 6 to 14 meters for a 10% pixel size variation” [PAGE 8]. In contrast, center-position perturbations are less severe: “sensitivity to bounding box position is less pronounced, with errors ranging from 0.6 to 1.6 meters when the bounding box position is varied by 2% of the image’s diagonal” [PAGE 8]. This asymmetry explains why bounding-box optimization is not cosmetic; it directly controls the dominant depth-scale term.

The third gray-zone derivation concerns annotation quality and domain shift. The authors construct SoccerNet-v3D by filtering calibrated multi-view systems and enforcing ball presence in multiple views. \[
P = KR[I \mid -t] \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times4}.

For a homogeneous world point \(\tilde X=(X,Y,Z,1)^\top\), the image point \(\bar p=(u,v)^\top\) satisfies
\[
\lambda
\begin{bmatrix}
u\\v\\1
\end{bmatrix}
=

\[
K=
\begin{bmatrix}
\alpha_x & s & x_0\\
0 & \alpha_y & y_0\\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix},

with \(\alpha_x,\alpha_y\) in pixels, \(x_0,y_0\) in pixels, and \(s\) dimensionless [PAGE 3]. If the soccer-field model is in meters, then \(t\) is in meters and the world coordinates \(X,Y,Z\) are in meters.

2. Reprojection residual  
For camera \(c\), define the projection map
\[
\pi(P_c\tilde X)=
\left(
\frac{(P_c\tilde X)_1}{(P_c\tilde X)_3},
\frac{(P_c\tilde X)_2}{(P_c\tilde X)_3}
\right).
\]
The pixel-space residual between projected point and observed point \(\bar p_c\) is
\[
r_c(X)=\pi(P_c\tilde X)-\bar p_c,
\]
with units of pixels. The squared reprojection error for a camera pair is
\[
e_{ij}(X)=\|r_i(X)\|_2^2+\|r_j(X)\|_2^2,
\]
with units of pixels\(^2\). If normalized coordinates are used instead, the residual becomes dimensionless.

3. Pair triangulation and filtered averaging  
The triangulated point from cameras \(i,j\) is
\[
p_{ij}=\arg\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}^3} e_{ij}(X).
\]
The dataset generation rule in Eq. (4) of the source is
\[
p = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\le i,j\le C,\;e_{ij}<\tau} p_{ij},
\]
where \(p\in\mathbb{R}^3\) is in meters, \(N\) is the number of valid camera pairs, and \(\tau\) is the reprojection threshold [PAGE 3]. The threshold \(\tau\) has pixel units if the residual is measured in pixel space. If \(\tau\) is too permissive, outlier pairs contaminate \(p\); if too strict, \(N\) may become zero or biased toward easy front-parallel configurations.

The source defines a Jaccard index for calibration quality [PAGE 3]:
\[
JaC_\gamma=\frac{TP_\gamma}{TP_\gamma+FN+FP}.
\]
This is dimensionless and lies in \([0,1]\). The threshold \(\gamma\) is a percentage of the image diagonal; if the image diagonal is \(L_{\text{diag}}\) pixels, then a 0.5% threshold corresponds to \(0.005L_{\text{diag}}\) pixels. The pipeline uses a gate such as \(JaC_{0.5\%}>0.75\) [PAGE 4].

\begin{bmatrix}

\bar p_y\\

\end{bmatrix},

\[

\begin{bmatrix}
\bar p_x\\
\bar p_y \pm d/2\\
1
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
The monocular 3D estimate is
\[
p=R^\top\frac{\phi\,p_c}{\|p_c^+-p_c^-\|}+t,
\]
where \(\phi\) is the true ball diameter in meters and \(t\) is in meters. The denominator is a dimensionless angular-size proxy derived from pixel measurements. The breakdown regime is \(d\to0\), or equivalently \(\|p_c^+-p_c^-\|\to0\), where depth uncertainty diverges.

matching Eq. (8) on [PAGE 5]. The objective has units of meters because \(p\) and the reconstructed point are both in meters. The optimized box is then centered on the ball ray with width and height equal to \(d_{\text{opt}}\).

7. Parallax and triangulation conditioning  
Let \(c_1,c_2\in\mathbb{R}^3\) be camera centers and let the ball point be \(p\in\mathbb{R}^3\). The viewing rays are proportional to \(p-c_1\) and \(p-c_2\). The parallax angle is
\[
\beta=\arccos
\left(
\frac{(p-c_1)^\top(p-c_2)}
{\|p-c_1\|\,\|p-c_2\|}
\right),
\]
with \(\beta\) in radians. The triangulation problem becomes ill-conditioned as \(\beta\to0\). In a simplified small-noise model, the depth standard deviation scales as
\[
\sigma_Z \approx \frac{Z^2}{f\,B_\perp}\sigma_{px},
\]
where \(B_\perp\) is the baseline component perpendicular to the viewing direction. This equation is not a toy diagnostic; it states the exact dimensional dependence: larger depth \(Z\) increases uncertainty quadratically, larger focal length \(f\) decreases it, larger perpendicular baseline \(B_\perp\) decreases it, and pixel noise \(\sigma_{px}\) increases it linearly.

8. Temporal synchronization bound  
ISSIA is described as “a two-minute soccer sequence captured using six synchronized cameras at a resolution of 1920×1080 pixels and a frame rate of 25 frames per second” [PAGE 4]. The nominal frame interval is
\[
\Delta t = \frac{1}{25}\,\text{s}=0.04\,\text{s}.
\]
If synchronization error is \(\delta t\), and ball speed is \(v\), the induced 3D positional discrepancy is approximately
\[
\Delta x \approx v\,\delta t.
\]
For \(v=30\,\text{m/s}\) and \(\delta t=0.04\,\text{s}\), \(\Delta x\approx1.2\,\text{m}\). This is below the paper’s 2-meter precision threshold [PAGE 6], but if \(\delta t\) reaches two frames or ball speed is higher, the error can exceed the threshold.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage is retro-causal in the sense that the present annotation pipeline is designed to satisfy future tasks: 3D ball localization, monocular benchmarking, detector training, and possible temporal tracking. The immediate mathematical ancestor is multiple-view geometry, especially calibrated triangulation [PAGE 3]. The camera model is classical pinhole projection, but the sports-field constraint makes calibration tractable: field lines serve as geometric anchors. The source uses PnLCalib, described as “an optimization-based calibration pipeline that leverages a 3D soccer field model and a predefined set of keypoints” [PAGE 3]. This lineage is not merely technical; it reflects a longer transition in sports analytics from wearable or manual tracking toward vision-only inference. The abstract says the datasets “extend SoccerNet-v3 and ISSIA by incorporating field-line-based camera calibration and multi-view synchronization” [PAGE 1].

The SoccerNet lineage supplies broadcast diversity: main-camera and replay views. The ISSIA lineage supplies static multi-view synchronization and temporal structure. The source explains that SoccerNet-v3D lacks a temporal dimension, while ISSIA-3D includes temporal information [PAGE 6]. This contrast creates a multi-week trap: a team may spend weeks trying to make broadcast replays temporally consistent when the main value of SoccerNet-v3D is spatial diversity, not temporal continuity. Conversely, a team may underuse ISSIA-3D because its camera views are limited; yet its static cameras are ideal for stable calibration and temporal tracking.

A second historical trap is the assumption that 2D annotation quality is sufficient for 3D supervision. Earlier multi-view datasets often triangulated human joints or player positions and treated the result as ground truth [PAGE 2]. The ball is harder because it is small, fast, and often blurred. The source states that the ball “appears relatively small in image space, is frequently occluded, often exhibits poor contrast, and is affected by noise and motion blur” [PAGE 4]. Therefore, a retro-causal design must not only calibrate cameras; it must also filter and optimize the ball annotations themselves.

A third trap is overreliance on reprojection error. Reprojection error is a useful residual, but it is not a complete identifiability certificate. The source warns about small parallax [PAGE 3]. If a future analysis ignores this warning, it may produce 3D positions with low reprojection error but unstable depth. The historical correction is to add geometric gates: calibration Jaccard index, minimum camera displacement, multi-view ball presence, and bounding-box size optimization.

A fourth trap is domain transfer. The source shows that models trained on SoccerNet-v3 fail on ISSIA: “YOLO models trained on SoccerNet-v3 [8] data, i.e., YOLObase and YOLOopt, fail to detect and localize the ball in both 2D and 3D spaces” [PAGE 7]. This is a classic retro-causal trap: one assumes that a detector trained on one broadcast distribution will generalize to another camera rig. The countermeasure in the paper is to generate ISSIA-specific bounding boxes from single-point annotations and fine-tune a detector, yielding YOLOISSIA.

A fifth trap is treating bounding-box optimization as mere cosmetic refinement. The sensitivity analysis shows that a 10% pixel size variation can cause 6 to 14 meters of 3D error [PAGE 8]. Therefore, the optimization method is part of the geometric estimator, not a post-processing convenience. The historical lesson is that 3D ball datasets must co-design annotation, calibration, and error metrics. The source’s contribution is precisely this co-design: it links field-line calibration, triangulation, reprojection filtering, bounding-box optimization, and monocular evaluation into one benchmark.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer asks how the pipeline anticipates its own failure modes and how an evaluation agent should reason about the model’s beliefs, habits, and temporal commitments. The first failure mode is crisis habit regression. In a crisis setting—such as an unusual camera view, severe occlusion, or cross-domain deployment—a learned detector may regress to the most familiar habit: treating the ball as a SoccerNet-style broadcast object. The paper’s evidence shows that this regression is real on ISSIA-3D. The source reports that SoccerNet-trained models fail on ISSIA-3D and that fine-tuning on ISSIA-generated boxes is required for competitive performance [PAGE 7]. The recursive prediction is that any agent evaluating a model must ask whether the model is merely reproducing training-domain priors or actually responding to the current camera geometry.

The second failure mode is counter-trap construction. The paper anticipates detection failure by generating bounding boxes from ISSIA single-point annotations and optimizing them with 3D consistency. This is a counter-trap: instead of waiting for manual bounding boxes, the system manufactures a training signal from sparse annotations. The source states that ISSIA ball annotations are “not provided in a bounding box format but rather as a single point indicating the ball center in image coordinates” [PAGE 5]. The recursive move is to convert a weak annotation modality into a detector-training modality through geometric optimization. This is powerful, but it also creates a new belief: the detector may learn that the ball size implied by the optimized box is more reliable than the raw image evidence. That belief is useful if the 3D label is accurate, but harmful if triangulation suffered from small parallax.

The third failure mode is clock drift. Temporal reasoning matters especially for ISSIA-3D, because it includes synchronized cameras and temporal information [PAGE 6]. The clock is not merely a timestamp; it is the assumption that the ball position observed in different views corresponds to the same physical instant. If synchronization fails, triangulation fuses incompatible observations. The source’s frame rate for ISSIA is 25 frames per second [PAGE 4], giving a 0.04-second nominal interval. A clock counter-trap must therefore estimate or bound synchronization error. If the ball is moving at high speed, even a small timing error can produce a displacement comparable to the 2-meter evaluation threshold.

A recursive evaluation agent should also model the dataset creator’s intent. The creators know that monocular depth is unstable, so they introduce a 2-meter precision threshold and report sensitivity to ball size and center variation [PAGE 8]. They know that calibration may be imperfect, so they evaluate both oracle field localization and PnLCalib field localization [PAGE 8]. They know that detectors may fail across domains, so they fine-tune ISSIA-specific models [PAGE 7]. The recursive theory-of-mind conclusion is that the benchmark is not only a measurement instrument; it is also an implicit statement about which failure modes are considered dangerous enough to filter, measure, or correct.

The crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock interact. In crisis, a model may regress to a large-object prior, assuming the ball is closer than it is because the detected box is too large. The counter-trap is to optimize the box using 3D consistency, but that counter-trap depends on the clock and parallax being valid. If the clock is wrong, the 3D label is wrong, and the counter-trap becomes a trap. Therefore, the safest recursive policy is layered: reject physically impossible estimates, estimate parallax uncertainty, verify temporal alignment, and only then accept the detector’s 3D output. The paper’s pipeline approximates this layered policy, but the recursive audit shows that the final guarantee is empirical rather than absolute.

Physical veto layer.  
Let the field plane be \(Z=0\) in world coordinates, with \(Z\) measured in meters. Let the ball diameter be \(\phi\), with radius \(r=\phi/2\). For a regulation size-five soccer ball, \(\phi\) is approximately \(0.22\) meters, so \(r\approx0.11\) meters. Let an estimated ball center be \(\hat p=(\hat X,\hat Y,\hat Z)\). A necessary physical condition for a ball above or on the pitch is
\[
\hat Z \ge r-\epsilon,
\]
where \(\epsilon\) is a small tolerance in meters for calibration and annotation error. If \(\hat Z < r-\epsilon\), the estimate places the ball center inside the ground, which is physically invalid unless the coordinate origin is defined differently. That estimate should be vetoed or remapped to the correct ground model.

A second physical veto is cheirality. For every camera used in triangulation or monocular reconstruction, the projected point must lie in front of the camera. If \(\lambda_c\le0\) for any active camera \(c\), the homogeneous projection has no valid positive depth, and the observation is inconsistent with a physical point in front of that camera. This condition is dimensionless in sign but implies meters of depth through \(\lambda_c\) and the camera coordinate system.

A third physical veto is parallax identifiability. Let \(\beta\) be the parallax angle between rays from two cameras to the estimated ball point. As \(\beta\to0\), the triangulation problem loses depth observability. A formal veto can be written as
\[
\beta < \beta_{\min}
\quad\text{or}\quad
\sigma_Z(\beta) > \sigma_{Z,\max},
\]
where \(\sigma_Z\) is estimated from calibration covariance and pixel noise. The source already warns that small parallax yields high uncertainty despite possibly low reprojection error [PAGE 3]. Therefore, low reprojection error alone cannot override a parallax veto.

Biomechanical veto layer.  
If temporal tracking is attempted, especially on ISSIA-3D, the ball trajectory must obey approximate dynamic constraints. Under gravity alone, vertical acceleration is \(-g\hat z\) with \(g\approx9.81\,\text{m/s}^2\), ignoring aerodynamic drag and spin. For consecutive estimates \(\hat p_t\) and \(\hat p_{t+1}\), the implied velocity is
\[
v_t \approx \frac{\|\hat p_{t+1}-\hat p_t\|}{\Delta t},
\]
with \(\Delta t\) in seconds. If \(v_t\) exceeds a domain-specific maximum plausible ball speed, the pair should be vetoed unless a tracking model explains the jump. For ISSIA’s 25 frames per second, \(\Delta t=0.04\,\text{s}\) [PAGE 4]. A synchronization error of one frame can create a spurious displacement of several meters if the ball is moving quickly. This is why clock verification is part of biomechanical plausibility.

Weather and visibility veto layer.  
The source notes that the ball is affected by noise and motion blur [PAGE 4]. Weather or lighting degradation does not change the calibrated geometry, but it can invalidate the 2D evidence required by the geometry. Let \(d\) be the detected ball diameter in pixels. If motion blur or low contrast makes \(d\) unreliable, then the monocular depth estimator
\[
p=R^\top\frac{\phi\,p_c}{\|p_c^+-p_c^-\|}+t
\]
becomes unstable because the denominator depends on \(d\). A practical weather/visibility veto is:
\[
d < d_{\min}
\quad\text{or}\quad
\text{detector confidence} < \theta_{\text{vis}},
\]
where \(d_{\min}\) is a pixel-size minimum and \(\theta_{\text{vis}}\) is a detection-confidence threshold. Under such conditions, the pipeline should abstain from monocular 3D estimation or require multi-view confirmation with strong parallax.

Adjudication proof sketch.  
Suppose an estimate passes all numeric checks but has \(\beta=0\). Then the two viewing rays are collinear or parallel; the depth coordinate is unobservable, and infinitely many 3D points project to the same image measurements. Therefore, the estimate cannot be uniquely adjudicated. Suppose an estimate has \(\hat Z<r-\epsilon\). Then the ball intersects the ground plane, violating a basic physical containment constraint. Suppose an estimate implies \(v\Delta t\) larger than a plausible dynamic envelope. Then either the clock is wrong, the association is wrong, or the physical model is violated. In all three cases, the estimate should be vetoed before being used for training or evaluation. This cross-layer adjudication is conservative but necessary because the paper’s metrics are conditional on valid detections and selected annotations.

The following three RLVR-style counterfactual veto scenarios are verifiable from the source and from the geometry it specifies. Each scenario defines a failure mode, a veto condition, and a testable prediction.

1. Small-parallax triangulation veto  
Veto condition: reject or downweight triangulated points when the parallax angle \(\beta\) is below a calibrated minimum or when the estimated depth uncertainty \(\sigma_Z\) exceeds a tolerance. This is motivated by the source’s warning that “triangulations with small parallax angles β—the angle between the two projection rays—exhibit higher uncertainty despite potentially yielding low reprojection errors” [PAGE 3]. The paper also identifies cases where “the replay view closely matches the main-camera view, leading to unreliable 3D ball localization estimates” [PAGE 4].

Prediction: If SoccerNet-v3D samples are stratified by \(\beta\), the low-\(\beta\) subset will show larger MAEm and lower P2m even if AP@0.5 remains similar, because 2D detection quality is unchanged while 3D depth observability worsens. The effect should intensify with camera distance. A verification experiment can recompute Table 2 metrics after binning pairs by \(\beta\) or by perpendicular baseline \(B_\perp\). The expected qualitative result is that oracle calibration reduces but does not eliminate the low-parallax penalty.

2. Ball-size estimation veto  
Veto condition: reject monocular 3D estimates when the predicted pixel diameter \(d\) is too small, too uncertain, or inconsistent with the known physical diameter \(\phi\). The source’s sensitivity analysis shows that “the 3D localization error is highly sensitive to variations in the predicted ball size, ranging from approximately 6 to 14 meters for a 10% pixel size variation” [PAGE 8]. Since depth scales inversely with angular size, an overestimated ball diameter makes the ball appear closer, while an underestimated diameter pushes it farther away.

Prediction: If the optimized bounding boxes are replaced by the original SoccerNet-v3 boxes, or if YOLO predictions are perturbed by 10% in diameter, MAEm should increase sharply, especially for distant balls. The paper’s Table 1 already supports the value of optimization: IoU improves from 0.57 to 0.66 and size error falls from 19.01% to 7.27% [PAGE 6]. The RLVR test is to perturb \(d\) at inference time and verify that MAEm grows approximately in accordance with the sensitivity curve, while P2m collapses more rapidly than AP@0.5.

3. Cross-domain detector veto  
Veto condition: reject 3D localization on ISSIA-3D when the detector was trained only on SoccerNet-v3 and lacks ISSIA-specific adaptation. The source states that “YOLO models trained on SoccerNet-v3 [8] data, i.e., YOLObase and YOLOopt, fail to detect and localize the ball in both 2D and 3D spaces” [PAGE 7]. Table 2 shows the quantitative collapse: on ISSIA-3D, YOLObase with oracle field localization has AP@0.5 \(=0.04\), MAEm \(=28.8\) m, MAE% \(=30.1\), and P2m \(=0.00\); YOLOopt has AP@0.5 \(=0.39\), MAEm \(=15.6\) m, MAE% \(=16.9\), and P2m \(=0.00\). By contrast, YOLOISSIA achieves AP@0.5 \(=0.65\), MAEm \(=4.2\) m, MAE% \(=4.8\), and P2m \(=0.35\) [PAGE 8].

Prediction: Any pipeline that deploys a SoccerNet-only detector on ISSIA-3D without generating ISSIA-style training boxes should be vetoed because the 2D detection failure makes 3D metrics meaningless. The verifiable counter-prediction is that fine-tuning on ISSIA-generated boxes restores detection and produces 3D precision on the order of the reported YOLOISSIA result. This veto is especially important because 3D error metrics are conditional on valid detections; when detection fails, low AP@0.5 and P2m near zero reveal that the 3D benchmark cannot be evaluated safely.

These three vetoes form an RLVR-style verification suite: geometric identifiability, measurement scale validity, and domain evidence validity. If any veto fires, the pipeline should abstain, recalibrate, or retrain rather than report a potentially misleading 3D position.
