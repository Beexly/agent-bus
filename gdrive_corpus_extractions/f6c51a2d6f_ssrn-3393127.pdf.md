<THINK_TRACE>
This is a methodological audit of the supplied DFS portfolio-construction paper, not a recommendation to participate in any contest. The core multi-hop derivation is: choose a binary roster \(w\in\{0,1\}^P\); realize athlete performance \(\delta\); score \(F=w^\top\delta\); compare \(F\) to an opponent order statistic \(G_{(r')}(W_{\mathrm{op}},\delta)\); integrate a rank-dependent step reward over uncertainty in \(\delta\) and \(W_{\mathrm{op}}\); then approximate the resulting probability by a mean-variance surrogate that is solvable as a binary quadratic program (BQP). The paper states this chain explicitly: “Our formulation maximizes the expected reward subject to feasibility constraints” [PAGE 1], and the double-up problem is written as \(\max_{w\in W}\mathbf P\{w^\top\delta>G_{(r')}(W_{\mathrm{op}},\delta)\}\) [PAGE 5]. The top-heavy extension replaces a single cutoff with rank bands and payoff differences, leading to the sum over \(d\) in equation (2) [PAGE 6].

The gray-zone audit starts with the approximation. The authors connect the probability objective to mean-variance optimization via a benchmark-outperformance result: “If we assume \(R_w-R_b\) has a normal distribution” then maximizing the probability is equivalent to maximizing \(1-\Phi(-\mu_w/\sigma_w)\) [PAGE 10]. Proposition 4.1 then splits the problem into a high-mean/high-variance branch when \(\mu_w<0\) and a mean-variance-efficient branch otherwise [PAGE 10]. The paper is candid that normality is not essential for the intuition: “if \(\mu_{Y_w}<0\) for all \(w\in W\), then it seems intuitively clear that we need to select a team \(w\) that simultaneously has a high mean and a high variance” [PAGE 11]. The operational risk is not the algebra; it is the input estimation. The moments \(\mu_{G_{(r')}}\), \(\sigma^2_{G_{(r')}}\), and \(\sigma_{\delta,G_{(r')}}\) must be simulated, and the top-heavy extension requires Assumption 5.2, namely that \(\mathrm{Cov}(\delta_p,G_{(r'_d)})\) is effectively independent of \(d\) [PAGE 12]. The source supports this by saying “we found these covariance terms to be very close to each other” for top percentiles [PAGE 12], and Proposition 5.1 gives an \(O\to\infty\) justification [PAGE 12]. That is a finite-sample gray zone: \(O\approx 200{,}000\) is large but not infinite, and the paper itself notes that the number of feasible lineups is vastly larger [PAGE 35].

The second gray zone is the payoff-shape assumption. Theorem 5.1 says “Under Assumption 5.3, \(R(W)\) is monotone submodular in \(W\)” [PAGE 14], and Assumption 5.3 is a convexity condition \(\Delta_k\ge\Delta_{k+1}\) on payoff decrements [PAGE 14]. But the paper admits a real violation: ranks 999 and 1000 both paid \$10 while rank 1001 paid \$8, so \(\Delta_{999}=0<2=\Delta_{1000}\) [PAGE 36]. The submodularity guarantee therefore holds best for the top few ranks, where most of the reward mass sits, but not uniformly. The \(1-1/e\) greedy guarantee is also only for the idealized greedy algorithm; the implemented Algorithm 3 does not enjoy that guarantee because finding the true highest-value-add entry is hard [PAGE 14–15]. The paper supplies a weaker certificate \(\nu_W=R(W)/(N R(w^*))\) [PAGE 15], with observed values “lowest ... 48.95%, highest ... 80.21%, average ... 62.27%” [PAGE 15]. This is a critical audit point: the empirical result is within-model and lower-bound based, not a proof of global optimality.

The third gray zone is opponent modeling. The Dirichlet-multinomial construction assumes \(p_{QB}\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha_{QB})\), then a random opponent selects a QB by \(\mathrm{Mult}(1,p_{QB})\) [PAGE 7], with \(\alpha_{QB}=\exp(X_{QB}\beta_{QB})\) [PAGE 8]. The copula is a mixture of independence and stacking: \((1-q)C_{\mathrm{ind}}+qC_{\mathrm{stack}}\) [PAGE 9, PAGE 30]. This is reasonable but not game-theoretic. The paper explicitly says fully strategic opponents create a game that “would most likely be impossible to solve” and that mean-field refinements are likely intractable [PAGE 25]. Thus, any claim that the model “solves” strategic DFS must be vetoed unless it is restricted to the paper’s assumption that opponents are generated by the fitted Dirichlet-multinomial-copula process.

The empirical audit is also gray. The authors report a strategic top-heavy cumulative profit of \$280.74 on \$75.76, “a return of over 350% in just 17 weeks” [PAGE 16], but they also warn that “model performance in DFS contests based on a single NFL season has an inherently high variance” [PAGE 3]. The source records stale-input failures: Fournette/Ivory late news was missed, and “around 60% of them ... picked Ivory” [PAGE 17]; Funchess/Byrd was missed, and “52 of our entries picked Funchess” [PAGE 17]; week 5 injuries affected 56, 70, 4, 44, and 25 entries [PAGE 18]. Those are not side notes; they are physical-state vetoes on the optimization inputs. The correct conclusion is narrow: under the paper’s fitted model, in this one NFL season, the strategic portfolio outperformed a benchmark; it is not a universal law of DFS, and it is especially not robust to stale \(\mu_\delta\), stale \(\Sigma_\delta\), or changing opponent habits.

and additional linear positional/diversity constraints collected into \(W\) [PAGE 4–5]. 2. Double-up objective. With \(G_{(r)}\) the rth order statistic of opponent scores and \(r'=O+1-r\),
\[
\max_{w\in W}\ \mathbf P\!\left\{w^\top\delta>G_{(r')}(W_{\mathrm{op}},\delta)\right\}.
\]
This is equation (1) [PAGE 5]. Units: \(w^\top\delta\) and \(G_{(r')}\) are fp; the probability is dimensionless. Breakdown: if \(\sigma_{Y_w}=0\), the normal-CDF surrogate \(1-\Phi(-\mu_{Y_w}/\sigma_{Y_w})\) is undefined at the ratio level, and the exact objective becomes a deterministic indicator. If all entries have identical deterministic scores, ties dominate and the source’s strict inequality requires the tie-handling caveat [PAGE 5, footnote 4].

\[
\max_{W\in W^N}\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{d=1}^{D}
(R_d-R_{d+1})\,
\mathbf P\!\left\{w_i^\top\delta>
G^{(r'_d)}_{-i}(W_{-i},W_{\mathrm{op}},\delta)\right\},
\]
with \(r'_d=O+N-r_d\) [PAGE 6, equation (2)]. Units: \(R_d-R_{d+1}\) is USD; the probability is dimensionless. Breakdown: if ranks are not distinct or if ties are not prorated, the step payoff changes; the paper notes ties are negligible except under replication [PAGE 5, footnote 4].

4. Mean and variance of the comparison variable. The source defines \(Y_w:=w^\top\delta-G_{(r')}\) and
\[
\mu_{Y_w}=w^\top\mu_\delta-\mu_{G_{(r')}},
\]
\[
\sigma^2_{Y_w}=w^\top\Sigma_\delta w+\sigma^2_{G_{(r')}}-2w^\top\sigma_{\delta,G_{(r')}},
\]
where \(\sigma_{\delta,G_{(r')}}\) is a \(P\times1\) covariance vector [PAGE 10, equation (11)]. Units: \(\mu_{Y_w}\) is fp; \(\sigma^2_{Y_w}\) is fp\(^2\); \(\sigma_{\delta,G}\) entries are fp\(^2\). Breakdown: if \(\sigma^2_{Y_w}<0\) due to numerical estimation error, the surrogate is invalid; the covariance vector must be estimated consistently.

5. Mean-variance proposition. Under \(R_w-R_b\sim N(\mu_w,\sigma^2_w)\), the source solves
\[
\max_{w\in W}\ 1-\Phi\!\left(-\frac{\mu_w}{\sigma_w}\right)
\]
[PAGE 10, equation (8)]. Proposition 4.1 states that if \(\mu_w<0\) for all feasible \(w\), the optimum lies in
\[
\{w(\lambda):w(\lambda)\in\arg\max_{w\in W}(\mu_w+\lambda\sigma^2_w),\ \lambda\ge0\},
\]
and if some \(\mu_w\ge0\), the optimum lies in
\[
\{w(\lambda):w(\lambda)\in\arg\max_{w\in W,\ \mu_w\ge0}(\mu_w-\lambda\sigma^2_w),\ \lambda\ge0\}
\]
[PAGE 10, equations (9)–(10)]. Units: \(\lambda\) must have units fp\(^{-1}\) so that \(\lambda\sigma^2_w\) is fp and can be added to \(\mu_w\). Breakdown: the branch is undefined at \(\mu_w=0\); the source itself notes that in quintuple-up contests “we often obtained an estimate of \(\mu_w\) that was close to zero” and may err in the variance direction [PAGE 17]. The source also notes \(\lambda^*\approx0\) for double-up and quintuple-up, meaning the opponent-modeling value is much smaller there [PAGE 19, PAGE 47–48].

6. Dirichlet-multinomial opponent generator. For the QB position,
\[
p_{QB}\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha_{QB}),\qquad
\text{selected QB}\sim\mathrm{Mult}(1,p_{QB}),
\]
with \(\alpha_{QB}=\exp(X_{QB}\beta_{QB})\) [PAGE 7–8]. The source estimates \(\beta_{QB}\) by Bayesian Dirichlet regression:
\[
p(\beta_{QB}\mid\{p_{QB,t}\}_{t=1}^{T-1})
\propto\prod_{t=1}^{T-1}
\mathrm{Dir}\!\left(p_{QB,t}\mid e^{X_{QB,t}\beta_{QB}}\right),
\]
with a flat prior [PAGE 8, equation (6)]. Units: \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are dimensionless after feature scaling; \(p_{QB}\) is a probability vector on the simplex. Breakdown: if the feature matrix \(X\) is stale or omits “momentum,” the fitted marginals can be badly wrong; the source documents Ezekiel Elliott being picked by about 80% of opponents in double-up while the 95% interval predicted 0% to 10% [PAGE 50], and Brady/Wilson misprediction in week 12 [PAGE 51].

7. Copula. By Sklar’s theorem,
\[
F_{w_o}(w_o^{QB},\dots,w_o^D)
=C\!\left(F_{QB}(w_o^{QB}),\dots,F_D(w_o^D)\right),
\]
with \(C=(1-q)C_{\mathrm{ind}}+qC_{\mathrm{stack}}\) [PAGE 7, PAGE 9, PAGE 30]. Units: copula arguments are probabilities in \([0,1]\); \(q\) is dimensionless. Breakdown: \(q=0\) gives the independence copula; \(q=1\) gives pure stacking; \(q\) estimated from a small sample of 300 entries is subject to sampling error [PAGE 45, footnote 39].

8. Order-statistic asymptotics. The source uses
\[
G_{(\lfloor qO\rfloor)}\mid(\delta,p)\xrightarrow{p}F^{-1}_{G\mid(\delta,p)}(q)
\quad\text{as }O\to\infty
\]
for \(q\in(0,1)\) [PAGE 32, equation (21)]. Units: the quantile is in fp. Breakdown: for extreme ranks such as \(q=1-10^{-5}\), the source states: “We cannot hope to estimate \(\hat F^{-1}_{G\mid(\delta,p)}(1-10^{-5})\) with just a moderate number of samples” [PAGE 31]. It therefore uses a normal tail approximation matched to the 99.0th and 99.5th percentiles [PAGE 31]. That is a clear singularity/breakdown regime: deep-tail order statistics are not nonparametrically identified from moderate Monte Carlo samples.

9. Submodularity and greedy bound. Under Assumption 5.3, the source proves “\(R(W)\) is monotone submodular in \(W\)” [PAGE 14]. For monotone submodular maximization under a cardinality constraint, the idealized greedy algorithm is within \(1-1/e\approx63.2\%\) of optimal [PAGE 14]. The implemented Algorithm 3 instead uses a diversification constraint \(w^\top w_{\lambda^*}\le\gamma\) and sets \(\gamma=C-3=6\) in the experiments; the paper reports that this “expected reward almost doubled” [PAGE 15]. The suboptimality certificate is
\[
\nu_W:=\frac{R(W)}{N R(w^*)},\qquad
\nu_W\le\frac{R(W)}{R(W^\sharp)}\le1,
\]
where \(W^\sharp\) is the optimal N-entry portfolio [PAGE 15, Proposition 5.2]. Breakdown: Assumption 5.3 fails at nonconvex payout steps; the source gives the rank 999/1000/1001 example where \(\Delta_{999}=0<2=\Delta_{1000}\) [PAGE 36]. Also, Algorithm 3’s \(\gamma<C\) can make the feasible set empty for large \(N\), and the \(1-1/e\) guarantee does not apply to Algorithm 3 because the true value-add entry is not found [PAGE 14–15].

10. Parimutuel reward-sharing analogy. In the parimutuel construction, if the current allocation on a horse is \(n_h=k\), the expected gain from assigning the next unit to horse \(h\) is
\[
\mu_h^{k+1}
=(k+1)q_h\frac{O+N}{O_h+k+1}
-kq_h\frac{O+N}{O_h+k}
=q_h(O+N)\frac{O_h}{(O_h+k)(O_h+k+1)}
\]
[PAGE 40, equation (31)]. Units: \(\mu_h^{k+1}\) is USD; \(q_h\) is dimensionless; \(O_h,k,N,O\) are counts. This yields a saturation effect: \(\mu_h^k\) is strictly decreasing in \(k\) [PAGE 40]. Breakdown: the paper explicitly notes that DFS lacks reward independence because the prize is awarded to the best submitted entry, not annulled if the ex-post best feasible team received no entry [PAGE 43]. Hence the parimutuel optimality proof does not transfer verbatim to DFS; it motivates diversification rather than proving it.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage is unusually explicit. It descends from four strands. First, copula modeling: Sklar’s theorem is used to factor the joint distribution of positional selections into marginals and a copula [PAGE 7], citing Sklar 1959 [PAGE 29]. Second, order-statistics theory: the paper relies on David and Nagaraja’s order-statistics results for convergence of sample quantiles [PAGE 31–32], and it uses ranked opponent scores as the stochastic benchmark. Third, mean-variance optimization for outperforming a stochastic benchmark: the mean-variance reduction is adapted from Morton et al. [PAGE 9–10], and the authors explicitly connect the DFS problem to that finance literature [PAGE 3]. Fourth, submodular maximization: the greedy heuristic is licensed by Nemhauser et al.’s \(1-1/e\) result [PAGE 14, PAGE 28]. A fifth, more applied strand is the integer-programming DFS work of Hunter, Vielma, and Zaman, which the paper positions as the closest predecessor [PAGE 2]. The parimutuel lineage is also explicit: the authors connect their allocation analysis to horse-race parimutuel markets and to the economics literature on information aggregation [PAGE 3, PAGE 39–43].

The retrocausal lesson is that later empirical claims are constrained by earlier modeling choices. The Dirichlet-multinomial-copula model is not a game-theoretic equilibrium model; it is a statistical opponent generator. The paper says fully strategic opponents create a game that is “most likely ... impossible to solve” and that mean-field refinements would “still likely be intractable” [PAGE 25]. So the genealogy explains both the power and the ceiling: the method inherits the tractability of mean-variance and submodular optimization, but it also inherits the stationarity assumptions of Dirichlet regression and the non-strategic assumption of the opponent generator.

The multi-week traps are visible in the source’s own audit. Trap 1: one-season overfitting. The authors warn that “model performance in DFS contests based on a single NFL season has an inherently high variance” [PAGE 3], and they note that 17 NFL weeks are a short sample relative to baseball, basketball, or hockey seasons [PAGE 17–18]. Trap 2: stale \(\mu_\delta\). The Fournette/Ivory and Funchess/Byrd cases show that a one-day-old estimate can be decisively wrong before roster lock [PAGE 17]. Trap 3: missing momentum features. Ezekiel Elliott’s ownership was about 80% in double-up while the 95% interval predicted 0% to 10% [PAGE 50]. Trap 4: playoff/weather/venue priors. The Brady-in-Miami example shows that a public feature provider may miss a well-known contextual effect, and the regression “followed suit” [PAGE 51]. Trap 5: payout-shape nonconvexity. The rank 999/1000/1001 payout step violates the convexity used for submodularity [PAGE 36]. Trap 6: contest-specific stacking. The estimated \(q\) is 0.35, 0.25, and 0.20 for top-heavy, quintuple-up, and double-up contests respectively [PAGE 45], so a single \(q\) carried across contest types is a drift trap.

The deeper retrocausal point is that the paper’s strongest empirical result, the top-heavy cumulative profit of \$280.74 on \$75.76 [PAGE 16], is not a stable causal estimate. It is the product of a model, a single season, a particular contest mix, and a benchmark that does not model opponents. The source’s own language—“we are confident that the realized P&L to-date for each contest series is actually conservative” [PAGE 17]—is a belief statement, not an identified effect. A valid genealogy must therefore treat the result as an existence demonstration under calibrated assumptions, not as a transferable law.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The source’s recursive theory of mind is deliberately shallow: it models opponents as draws from a fitted distribution, not as agents who reason about our reasoning. The generative model is \(p\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha)\), \(w_o\sim\mathrm{Mult}(\cdot,p)\), with a mixture copula \(C=(1-q)C_{\mathrm{ind}}+qC_{\mathrm{stack}}\) [PAGE 7–9]. The paper’s justification is empirical: some athletes are more popular, and “there is some predictability in the team selections of DFS players who may be responding to weekly developments that contain more noise than genuine information” [PAGE 6]. That is a first-order theory of mind: we predict their habits, not their counter-reasoning.

Crisis habit regression is the first failure mode. Under late-breaking news, opponents do not smoothly update a Dirichlet posterior; they regress to salient habits—popular names, stacking, and recent scoring. The Fournette/Ivory case is a clean example: the authors’ stale estimate projected 17.27 fp for Fournette and 6.78 fp for Ivory, but “around 60% of them ... picked Ivory” in double-up [PAGE 17]. The model’s \(p\) vector was not merely noisy; it was behaviorally displaced by a public injury signal. A recursive model would need a crisis-state variable \(S_t\in\{\text{normal},\text{late-news},\text{injury-shock}\}\) that shifts both \(\alpha\) and \(q\). The source’s own week 7 “before” and “after” plot shows that updating the Ivory estimate would have moved the predicted ownership interval substantially [PAGE 51].

Counter-trap is the second failure mode. If the strategic player systematically avoids popular athletes, the remaining field may also pivot, or the public feature that everyone uses may already encode the pivot. The paper’s benchmark model is not strategic, but the paper’s strategic model picks less popular QBs on average: “an average \(p_{QB}\) of 6.88% for the strategic model versus an average of 12.74% for the benchmark model” [PAGE 48]. That is a real counter-crowd behavior, but it is not a guarantee of edge. If opponents anticipate the same public signal, the “contrarian” pick becomes crowded. The Brady/Wilson week 12 example is the mirror image: FantasyPros overestimated Brady and underestimated Wilson, and the regression followed the feature [PAGE 51]. The counter-trap is therefore not “always fade the crowd”; it is “fade the crowd only when the crowd’s signal is stale and the opponent model’s \(q,\alpha\) have not already absorbed the pivot.”

Clock is the third failure mode. The DFS decision has a hard submission deadline, and the source repeatedly emphasizes late information: “such late-breaking developments are common-place and in order to extract the full benefit of the modeling framework ... it is important that such developments be reflected in updated parameter estimates” [PAGE 24–25]. But the optimization pipeline is expensive: the paper reports Monte Carlo estimation, BQP solves, and a need for fast re-optimization just before lock [PAGE 24–25]. The clock creates a recursive mismatch: as \(t\to t_{\text{lock}}\), the value of new information rises, but the time available to re-solve the BQP and re-estimate the opponent distribution falls. A rational opponent model must therefore include a latency variable, not just a probability vector.

A formal recursive extension would be:
\[
p_{t}\sim\mathrm{Dir}\!\left(\alpha_t(X_t,\beta)\right),\qquad
q_t=g(S_t,\text{contest type}),\qquad
S_t=\phi(\text{news}_t,\text{public picks}_t),
\]
where \(S_t\) is a latent crisis/habit state and \(\phi\) is a monitored news-and-crowd signal. The source does not estimate such a state. Its own evidence—Elliott at about 80% versus a 0%–10% interval [PAGE 50], Ivory at about 15% versus “below 5%” [PAGE 50], and the week 5 injury cascade [PAGE 18]—shows that the omitted state is not a minor perturbation. The correct recursive conclusion is: the opponent model is a habit model, and habit models fail precisely when the clock is shortest and the crisis is largest.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical-layer veto is the strongest. The optimization takes \(\mu_\delta\) and \(\Sigma_\delta\) as inputs, but the true performance distribution is generated by human bodies, roles, injuries, and weather. The source provides direct counterexamples. In week 7, the authors’ estimate “did not reflect this new information” about Leonard Fournette and Chris Ivory, and the opponent field responded: “around 60% of them (in double-up) picked Ivory” [PAGE 17]. In week 16, the authors missed the Funchess/Byrd role change, and “52 of our entries picked Funchess” [PAGE 17]. In week 5, injuries hit many submitted portfolios: “Devante Parker ... was picked by 56 of our entries. Charles Clay ... and Sterling Shepard ... affecting 70 and 4 entries, respectively. Bilal Powell ... and Travis Kelce ... impacting 44 and 25 entries” [PAGE 18]. These are not modeling residuals; they are state changes that invalidate the input distribution.

A formal veto proof is straightforward. Let \(\Theta\) be the true physical state and \(\hat\Theta\) the model input. The objective is
\[
R(W;\Theta)=\mathbb E_{\delta,W_{\mathrm{op}}\mid\Theta}\left[\mathrm{Reward}(W,\delta,W_{\mathrm{op}})\right].
\]
Because \(\mathrm{Reward}\) is a step function of the rank of \(w_i^\top\delta\) relative to order statistics \(G^{(r'_d)}_{-i}\), it is not continuous in \(\delta\) at rank boundaries. Therefore a shift \(\Theta\to\hat\Theta\) that changes the ordering of athletes can change \(R(W;\Theta)\) discontinuously. Hence
\[
W^*(\hat\Theta)\in\arg\max_W R(W;\hat\Theta)
\ \not\Rightarrow\
W^*(\hat\Theta)\in\arg\max_W R(W;\Theta).
\]
No amount of BQP optimality closes that gap. The source’s own stale-input failures are empirical witnesses to this non-invariance.

Biomechanical vetoes include confirmed inactivity, snap-count changes, target-share changes, and injury probability spikes. The paper notes that NFL player performance “will have quite a high variance due to the large roster size as well as the relatively high probability of injury” [PAGE 17]. It also notes that “there are more than 45 athletes on a roster but only 11 on the field at any one time” [PAGE 17]. That is a biomechanical admission: the mapping from athlete identity to fantasy points is mediated by playing time and role, not just by a mean vector. If a starting running back is questionable and his backup is underpriced, the opponent distribution \(p\) and the performance distribution \(\delta\) both move. The source’s Fournette/Ivory case is exactly that.

Weather vetoes are less explicit in the source but no less real. The paper does not model weather as a separate covariate; it models \(\mu_\delta\) and \(\Sigma_\delta\) from external estimates and then notes that late developments should be incorporated [PAGE 24–25]. The Brady-in-Miami anecdote is a venue/context effect: “Tom Brady ... and the New England Patriots generally perform very poorly in Miami” and “FantasyPros did not account for this” [PAGE 51]. Weather can act through the same channel: wind, precipitation, and temperature can shift passing volume, kicking accuracy, and game script. Because the source has no explicit weather operator, any claim that its portfolios are weather-robust is unsupported. The correct cross-layer adjudication is: if a credible weather signal arrives after the last parameter update, the optimization must be treated as stale, and the safe action is to freeze, re-estimate, or veto the affected athletes rather than trust the previous BQP solution.

The cross-layer veto rule should be explicit. Let \(\mathcal V_{\mathrm{phys}}\) be the set of athletes with confirmed or high-probability role/injury/weather changes. A portfolio \(W\) is vetoed if
\[
\left|\{w_i\in W:w_i^\top \mathbf 1_{\mathcal V_{\mathrm{phys}}}>0\}\right|
>
\tau_{\mathrm{phys}},
\]
or if the updated \(\mu_\delta,\Sigma_\delta\) change the sign of \(\mu_{Y_{w_i}}\) or the ordering of the top candidate entries. The source’s week 5 injury cascade would have triggered such a veto: several entries held multiple affected athletes at once. The source’s own advice points the same way: “it would be desirable to re-optimize the entire portfolio in such circumstances but given time constraints, it may be necessary to make do with simple but fast heuristic updates” [PAGE 24–25]. That is a concession that the physical layer can veto the optimization layer.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto scenario 1: posterior-predictive coverage veto for the Dirichlet regression. The source reports that the three model variations have marginal posterior predictive summary statistics between 93% and 97% [PAGE 56, Table 7], but the “most-picked athlete” check shows extreme Bayesian p-values about 12.5% of the time in top-heavy contests and 19.5% of the time in double-up contests, even though roughly 5% would be expected under a correct model [PAGE 56–57]. Verifiable prediction: if a new week’s 95% posterior interval coverage falls below 90%, or if the most-picked-athlete p-value falls below 2.5% or above 97.5% in more than 15% of position-weeks, the opponent model should be vetoed for that week. The source’s Ezekiel Elliott case—about 80% realized ownership versus a 0%–10% interval [PAGE 50]—predicts exactly this kind of failure when a momentum feature is omitted. A second prediction: adding a momentum feature and late-news updates should reduce the extreme-p-value rate toward 5%; if it does not, the Dirichlet specification itself is suspect.

Veto scenario 2: branch-condition veto for Assumption 5.1 and the \(\mu_{Y_w}\) sign. Algorithm 2 assumes \(\mu_{Y^d_w}<0\) for all d and feasible w, so that the optimization seeks high variance for a fixed mean [PAGE 12]. The source admits the assumption can break down “if payoffs extend beyond the top 20% of entries” [PAGE 34]. Verifiable prediction: for each week, compute the estimated \(\mu_{Y^d_w}\) for the selected top-heavy entry. If \(\mu_{Y^d_w}\ge0\) for the top payoff band d, then the high-variance branch is mis-specified and the BQP solution should be vetoed or rerun with Algorithm 5. The source’s own tables predict that top-heavy contests will have positive \(\lambda^*\) while double-up and quintuple-up will have \(\lambda^*\) near zero [PAGE 47–48]. A second prediction: the veto will fire more often in quintuple-up than in top-heavy, because the source says quintuple-up “often obtained an estimate of \(\mu_w\) that was close to zero” [PAGE 17].

Veto scenario 3: payout-convexity veto for Assumption 5.3 and submodularity. Theorem 5.1 requires \(\Delta_k\ge\Delta_{k+1}\) [PAGE 14]. The source itself reports a violation: rank 999 and rank 1000 paid \$10 each, while rank 1001 paid \$8, so \(\Delta_{999}=0<2=\Delta_{1000}\) [PAGE 36]. Verifiable prediction: if the top \(D\) payoff bands that carry most of the expected reward satisfy the convexity condition, Algorithm 3’s diversification heuristic should retain its empirical advantage; if the violation enters the top few percent of ranks, the submodularity justification fails and the greedy portfolio should be compared against an exhaustive or mixed-integer benchmark. The source predicts that the violation is mostly in the tail: “Assumption 5.3 holds for the top few ranks where most of the action ... lies” [PAGE 36]. A second prediction: a policy that uses the idealized greedy guarantee as a performance claim will overstate the implemented Algorithm 3’s optimality, because the source explicitly says Algorithm 3 does not enjoy the \(1-1/e\) guarantee [PAGE 14–15].
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
