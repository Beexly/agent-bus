James’s 1890 text sits at the convergence of several nineteenth-century lineages. The concept of “mono-ideism” descends from the hypnotism debates of the Salpêtrière and the Nancy school: Charcot’s view of hysteria and hypnosis as a neuropathic state, versus Bernheim and Liébeault’s emphasis on suggestion in ordinary subjects [PAGE 600 context: James contrasts hypnotic phenomena with “simple waking credulity and ‘suggestibility’,” directly engaging the Nancy position]. The term “hypnotic trance” itself echoes Braid’s 1840s neuro-hypnology, which rebranded mesmerism as a physiological state rather than animal magnetism. James’s appeal to “automatism, double consciousness, or second ‘personality’” [PAGE 600] anticipates Pierre Janet’s dissociation theory and the later diagnostic category of dissociative identity disorder; it also connects to Flournoy’s work on mediums and “second personalities” in spiritualist séances, where hypnotic-like trance produced alternate identities with amnesic boundaries. The hypnagogic/hypnopompic imagery lineage runs through Alfred Maury’s *Le sommeil et les rêves* (1861), which James knew well, and into modern sleep-onset research. The footnote’s insistence that trance “is not identical with sleep” [PAGE 600, footnote] marks a deliberate separation from the somnolist school that had treated hypnosis as artificial sleep.

Multi-week traps. In longitudinal hypnosis research, three traps recur. First, the state-dependency trap: a subject’s amnesia for trance events may hold within a single week but break down across weeks as retrieval cues generalize and the encoding context becomes less distinctive; researchers who test only once per week may falsely conclude that amnesia is stable. Second, the susceptibility drift trap: \(\chi\) is not a trait but a state contingent on sleep debt, stress, medication, and circadian phase; a subject screened as “highly susceptible” in week one may test as insusceptible in week three, producing false negatives if re-screening is omitted. Third, the rapport expectancy trap: repeated sessions with the same operator create demand characteristics that inflate motor suggestibility \(I(V;M\mid h)\) without any corresponding change in \(C_t\); the “propitious phase” becomes easier to induce not because the cerebral change is more accessible, but because the subject has learned the role. James’s observation that “even the best hypnotic subjects pass through life without any one suspecting them” until deliberate experiment [PAGE 600] implies that susceptibility is latent and context-gated, which exacerbates all three traps in multi-week designs.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Crisis habit regression. When an operator or subject enters a crisis—failed induction, unexpected motor compulsion, or emergence anxiety—each party tends to regress to habitual control strategies. The operator, per James’s protocol, fixes the subject’s eyes or attention and “holds them in it by his talk” [PAGE 600]. Under crisis, the operator may regress to authoritarian fixation, prolonged staring, or increasingly direct commands, which can break rapport and push \(\chi\) below \(\chi_c\), aborting the transition across \(\mathcal{M}_{w\to h}\). The subject, meanwhile, may regress to waking suggestibility (ordinary compliance) or to active resistance; either regression collapses the mono-ideism condition because background ideas “rally” to monitor the operator, raising \(H_t\) above \(H_c\).

Counter-trap. The primary counter-trap is simulated trance: the subject produces motor effects and reports anesthesia or amnesia to satisfy demand characteristics, while \(I(V;M\mid h)\) is high only because \(M\) is under voluntary control, not because \(V\) drives \(M\) through a dissociative cerebral change. The counter-counter-trap is to require the Jamesian signature cluster jointly: motor effects plus post-trance oblivion plus recall in a subsequent trance plus anesthesia/hyperesthesia [PAGE 600]. A simulator can typically fake one channel, but maintaining state-dependent memory failure across normal waking and subsequent trance—i.e., \(P(\text{recall}\mid w) \ll P(\text{recall}\mid h)\)—is difficult without genuine state-dependent encoding.

Clock. James’s induction is explicitly time-structured: a short fixation period develops the “propitious phase,” talk maintains it, and “abrupt awakening” terminates it [PAGE 600]. The propitious phase is a time-locked window in \(C_t\)-space; missing it means the bifurcation is not crossed. Circadian and ultradian rhythms modulate \(\chi\): sleep deprivation lowers the threshold for somnolescence-like mono-ideism but may blur the trance/sleep boundary the footnote seeks to preserve [PAGE 600, footnote]. The “subsequent trance” recall test reintroduces a longer clock: state-dependent memory must persist across the inter-trance interval, which can range from minutes to weeks. If the interval is too short, short-term memory confounds the test; if too long, ordinary forgetting inflates \(\epsilon\). The recursive theory of mind therefore nests three clocks: the seconds-scale induction clock, the minutes-scale trance maintenance clock, and the days-to-weeks consolidation/recall clock.

Physical layer veto. No suggestion, however potent, can override hard homeostatic reflexes (airway protection, nociceptive withdrawal, cardiac autoregulation). Conversely, true neurological lesion (stroke, spinal cord injury) vetoes any hypnotic explanation for paralysis: the biomechanical layer trumps the psychological layer. Formal: \(V_{\text{bio}} = (\text{EMG normal under distraction}) \lor (\text{structural lesion present}) \implies \neg \text{hypnotic paralysis}\).

Weather layer veto. Sleep onset and hypnagogic imagery are sensitive to thermoregulatory and environmental arousal load. The weather layer does not veto an already-established trance directly, but it vetoes the induction attempt: if environmental arousal is high, \(\chi\) is effectively driven below \(\chi_c\) regardless of operator skill. Formal: \(V_{\text{weather}} = (\text{arousal stimulus} > \theta_{\text{env}}) \land (\text{induction fails within standard fixation}) \implies \neg \text{trance onset}\). This is consistent with James’s requirement for a controlled, short fixation period to develop the propitious phase [PAGE 600]; uncontrolled environments violate the boundary conditions of the procedure.

Adjudication rule. A phenomenon is admitted as Jamesian hypnotic trance only if all layer vetoes are negative: physical reflexes are appropriately modulated, biomechanical signs are functional not structural, and environmental arousal was controlled during induction. If any veto fires, the case falls back to somnolescence, ordinary sleep, role enactment, or neurological pathology, per James’s own boundary work [PAGE 600, footnote].

Deep multi-hop derivation and gray-zone audit.

The source text (printed page 600; source file p0610.txt) presents William James’s comparison between somnolescence and the hypnotic trance. The derivation proceeds in several hops. First, James describes somnolescence as a condition in which background ideas “inertly into the background of the mind,” allowing “incongruous images” to “reign alone” [PAGE 600]. These images acquire “exceptional vivacity,” progressing from hypnagogic hallucinations into dreams as sleep deepens [PAGE 600]. The key psychological signature is “mono-ideism,” defined as the “impotency and failure to ‘rally’ on the part of the background-ideas” [PAGE 600]. James attributes this to a “special physiological change occurring in the brain at that time” [PAGE 600]. Hop two extends the same logic to the hypnotic consciousness: “similar mono-ideism, or dissociation of the reigning fancy from those other thoughts which might possibly act as its ‘reductives,’” must “equally be due to a special cerebral change” [PAGE 600]. Hop three notes that the label “hypnotic trance” is explicitly phenomenological: it “tells us nothing of what the change is, but it marks the fact that it exists” [PAGE 600]. Hop four lists the behavioral markers that separate hypnotic phenomena from “simple waking credulity and ‘suggestibility’”: the great vivacity of hypnotic images “as gauged by their motor effects,” oblivion upon resumption of normal life, abrupt awakening, recall in subsequent trances, and frequent anesthesia/hyperesthesia [PAGE 600]. These point toward “sleep and dreaming, or towards those deeper alterations of the personality known as automatism, double consciousness, or second ‘personality’” as the true analogues [PAGE 600]. Hop five describes the induction procedure: the operator fixes eyes or attention and “holds them in it by his talk,” developing a “propitious phase,” after which the subject becomes “the puppets of all his suggestions,” a control “no ordinary suggestions of waking life ever took” [PAGE 600]. The footnote adds a crucial boundary condition: the trance state “is not identical with sleep,” lighter stages show “muscular inabilities and compulsions” absent in ordinary somnolescence, and whereas the somnolescent mind is confused, the lighter trance mind “may be quite clearly conscious” [PAGE 600, footnote].

Gray-zone audit. James never specifies the “special physiological/cerebral change,” leaving it an unparameterized black box. The notion of “reductives” is undefined at the mechanistic level—it could correspond to inhibitory competition in a global-workspace framework, to suppression of counter-schemas, or to failure of inhibitory interneuronal coordination, but the text does not decide among these. “Vivacity” is operationally indexed only by motor effects, which is a behavioral proxy, not a direct neural measure, and it risks circularity: trance is inferred from suggestibility, and suggestibility is evidenced by motor response to trance-induced suggestions. The footnote creates a three-way boundary problem among somnolescence, lighter trance, and sleep; James asserts non-identity with sleep while admitting analogy, but he does not provide a crisp criterion that distinguishes hypnotic trance from deep absorption or from stage-1 sleep onset in all cases. State-dependent memory is implied by “oblivion … when normal life is resumed” and “recollection … in subsequent trances” [PAGE 600], but James offers no retrieval-cue model explaining why the amnesia barrier selectively drops in the trance context. Finally, the claim that “no ordinary suggestions of waking life ever took such control” [PAGE 600] is a strong within-subject contrast, but it does not rule out role-enactment, demand characteristics, or motivated compliance as alternative accounts of the “puppet” phenomenon.

Multi-hop formal sketch (to be formalized in the next section): Let a latent cerebral state variable C_t range over wake (w), somnolescence (s), hypnotic trance (h), and sleep (l). James’s physiological change is a crossing of a bifurcation manifold M_{w→h}. Mono-ideism corresponds to a collapse in the Shannon entropy of the ideational distribution P_t(i) over ideas i. “Reductives” form a counter-idea set R; their dissociation from the reigning fancy F means conditional mutual information I(R;F | h) ≈ 0. Vivacity is indexed by mutual information between image content and motor output, I(V;M | h). Oblivion and later recall are state-dependent memory signatures: recall probability is low in normal waking but high in a subsequent trance. Breakdown occurs when susceptibility χ falls below a critical value χ_c, preventing the induction operator T̂_op from driving C_t across M; or when retrieval context matches encoding context, collapsing the amnesia barrier. These regimes are stated explicitly as assumptions and singularity conditions rather than as causal claims licensed by James alone.

Formal operators, units, page citations, and breakdown regimes.

Define the state space S = {w, s, h, l} for wake, somnolescence, hypnotic trance, and sleep. Let C_t ∈ R^n denote the latent cerebral state at time t. James’s “special physiological change” [PAGE 600] is modeled as a transition of C_t across a bifurcation manifold M_{w→h} (analogously M_{w→s} for somnolescence). Assumption: the macroscopic psychological states are identifiable with regions or attractors in this neural state space; breakdown regime: James provides no equation for M, so any specific coordinate assignment is an extra-theoretical invention, not a deduction from the text.

Let P_t(i) be the probability of idea i ∈ I being active at time t. Ideational entropy, in bits:
H_t = -Σ_{i∈I} P_t(i) log_2 P_t(i)   [bits].
Mono-ideism is the regime H_t < H_c for a critical entropy H_c. In wake, H_w is comparatively high because background ideas can “rally” [PAGE 600]; in somnolescence and trance, H_s, H_h < H_c. Breakdown: H_c is not specified by James; choosing it arbitrarily would be a toy parameter.

Let R denote reductive counter-ideas and F the reigning fancy. Dissociation in trance:
I(R; F | h) ≈ 0   [bits],
whereas in ordinary wake I(R; F | w) > 0. This is the information-theoretic expression of James’s “dissociation of the reigning fancy from those other thoughts which might possibly act as its ‘reductives’” [PAGE 600]. Breakdown: if I(R; F | h) remains high, the mono-ideism hypothesis is falsified for that subject.

Vividness gauge via motor effects [PAGE 600]:
I(V; M | h) >> I(V; M | w)   [bits],
where V is image vividness and M is motor output. High mutual information means the image reliably drives action, matching James’s observation that hypnotic images are gauged by their motor effects. Breakdown: motor compliance can occur without subjective vividness (role enactment), so I(V;M|h) is necessary but not sufficient for the Jamesian trance.

State-dependent memory and oblivion [PAGE 600]:
P(recall | state=w) = ε << 1,
P(recall | state=h_subsequent) = 1 - δ,
with probabilities in [0,1]. The divergence between conditions is
D_KL(P_recall|w || P_recall|h) >> 0   [bits].
This captures “oblivion of them when normal life is resumed” and “recollection of them again in subsequent trances” [PAGE 600]. Breakdown: if recall is equally poor in both states, the amnesia is ordinary forgetting, not state-dependent trance amnesia.

Sensory gain modulation for anesthesia/hyperesthesia [PAGE 600]:
g → 0   (anesthesia),    g >> g_wake   (hyperesthesia),
where g is the gain of sensory afferent transmission. Breakdown: subjective report of anesthesia without corresponding suppression of nociceptive reflexes or cortical evoked responses invalidates the gain claim.

Footnote boundary layer [PAGE 600, footnote]: Let consciousness clarity C ∈ [0,1] and muscular inhibition/compulsion μ ≥ 0. Ordinary somnolescence: C low, μ ≈ 0. Lighter trance: C high, μ > 0. Non-identity with sleep: h ≠ l, though the sets overlap in phenomenology. Breakdown: if a subject shows μ > 0 with C low and slow-wave EEG, the state is sleep, not trance, and the hypnotic label must be vetoed.

Induction operator T̂_op acts on C_t via fixation and talk to develop the “propitious phase” [PAGE 600]. Susceptibility χ ∈ R_{≥0}. Successful induction requires T̂_op C_t to cross M_{w→h}. Singularity/breakdown: when χ < χ_c, the operator fails to drive the transition regardless of talk duration; when χ is high but environmental arousal is excessive, the system is knocked back to w before consolidation.

Historical and scientific lineage, plus multi-week traps.

James’s 1890 text sits at the convergence of several nineteenth-century lineages. The concept of “mono-ideism” descends from the hypnotism debates of the Salpêtrière and the Nancy school: Charcot’s view of hysteria and hypnosis as a neuropathic state, versus Bernheim and Liébeault’s emphasis on suggestion in ordinary subjects [PAGE 600 context: James contrasts hypnotic phenomena with “simple waking credulity and ‘suggestibility’,” directly engaging the Nancy position]. The term “hypnotic trance” itself echoes Braid’s 1840s neuro-hypnology, which rebranded mesmerism as a physiological state rather than animal magnetism. James’s appeal to “automatism, double consciousness, or second ‘personality’” [PAGE 600] anticipates Pierre Janet’s dissociation theory and the later diagnostic category of dissociative identity disorder; it also connects to Flournoy’s work on mediums and “second personalities” in spiritualist séances, where hypnotic-like trance produced alternate identities with amnesic boundaries. The hypnagogic/hypnopompic imagery lineage runs through Alfred Maury’s Le sommeil et les rêves (1861), which James knew well, and into modern sleep-onset research. The footnote’s insistence that trance “is not identical with sleep” [PAGE 600, footnote] marks a deliberate separation from the somnolist school that had treated hypnosis as artificial sleep.
