<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The core operator is a contrast-based network meta-analysis. For study \(i\) and arm \(k\), the generic model is

\[
y_{i,k}\sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\theta_{i,k},\,s_{i,k}^{2}\right),
\qquad
\theta_{i,k}=\mu_{t_k}+\delta_{i,t_k},
\qquad
\delta_{i,t_k}\sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(d_{t_k,u_k},\,\tau^{2}\right),
\]

where \(y_{i,k}\) is the post-intervention outcome in native units: seconds (s) for 5–40 m sprint, centimeters (cm) for SJ and CMJ, meters per second (m/s) for COD, and kilograms (kg) for 1RM [PAGE 4]. Therefore \(s_{i,k}^{2}\) and \(\tau^{2}\) carry squared units: \(\mathrm{s}^2\), \(\mathrm{cm}^2\), \(\mathrm{m}^2/\mathrm{s}^2\), or \(\mathrm{kg}^2\). The authors state they assumed “a common between-study variance (τ²) across treatment comparisons within each network” and estimated it by REML [PAGE 5]. Assumptions: consistency, transitivity, connected network, and exchangeability of studies within each comparison. Breakdown regimes: a disconnected network makes some \(d_{t_k,u_k}\) non-identifiable; a sparse loop makes the indirect estimate effectively singular, as in the 5 m table where an indirect SE is 29.506 s [PAGE 22]; and \(\tau^2\to 0\) collapses the model toward a fixed-effect summary, which can overstate precision when between-study heterogeneity is real.

SUCRA is a rank-transform, not a posterior probability of superiority:

\[
S_j=\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{r=1}^{K-1}\Pr\!\left(\operatorname{rank}_j\le r\right),
\qquad S_j\in[0,1]\ \text{or}\ 0\text{–}100\%.
\]

Here \(K\) is the number of interventions in that outcome network. The paper reports \(S_j\) as percentages, e.g. 99.8% for Traditional Strength Training at 5 m and 74.2% for Speed Training at 30 m [PAGE 1]. Units are probability; the value is not an information-theoretic certainty and not a direct effect size. Breakdown regimes: if all treatments are nearly equal, or if \(\tau^2\) is large relative to the contrasts, SUCRA values drift toward the uniform \(1/K\) pattern; if a node has no direct evidence and wide intervals, its SUCRA can be high while its contrast with the reference remains non-significant. That is exactly the 30 m case: Speed Training ranks first by SUCRA, but MD = -0.16 s (95% CI: -0.34 to 0.01) versus Regular Training [PAGE 1].

For future-study uncertainty, the relevant operator is the prediction interval:

\[
\widehat{MD}\ \pm\ t_{\nu,0.975}\sqrt{\widehat{SE}^{2}+\widehat{\tau}^{2}},
\]

with the same native units as the outcome. Table S31 gives key examples: 5 m Traditional Strength vs Regular MD = -0.09 s, 95% PI -0.13 to -0.05; 20 m MD = -0.13 s, PI -0.26 to 0.00; 30 m Speed vs Regular MD = -0.16 s, PI -0.42 to 0.10; 40 m Endurance vs Regular MD = -0.21 s, PI -0.56 to 0.14 [PAGE 37]. The 20 m PI touches zero at its upper bound; the 30 m and 40 m PIs cross zero. Breakdown regime: when \(\tau^2\) is large relative to \(\widehat{MD}\), the PI becomes so wide that it cannot adjudicate practical superiority even if the mean estimate looks favorable.

Mechanistically, the paper’s short-sprint interpretation is compatible with impulse-momentum and force-velocity operators:

\[
m\Delta v=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}F_{\text{net}}(t)\,dt,
\qquad
F_{\text{net}}=F_{\text{prop}}-F_{\text{drag}}-F_{\text{friction}},
\qquad
F_{\text{prop}}\le \mu_s F_N,
\]

where \(m\Delta v\) is momentum change in \(\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}\), \(F\) is force in newtons, and impulse is \(\mathrm{N\cdot s}\). \(F_{\text{prop}}\le \mu_s F_N\) is the traction constraint: if the required horizontal impulse exceeds the available friction impulse over the contact time, the athlete slips. This is a physical breakdown regime for sprint acceleration, especially on wet or loose surfaces.

For vertical jump, the simplest ballistic relation is

\[
h=\frac{v_{\text{to}}^{2}}{2g},
\]

with \(h\) in meters, \(v_{\text{to}}\) in m/s, and \(g=9.80665\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\). The SJ/CMJ distinction is that CMJ uses stretch-shortening-cycle storage and return, whereas SJ reduces countermovement contribution; the paper reports Traditional Strength Training MD = 4.40 cm for SJ (95% CI: 2.07 to 6.74) and 3.58 cm for CMJ (95% CI: 2.00 to 5.15) versus Regular Training [PAGE 4]. The rate-of-force-development operator is

\[
\mathrm{RFD}(t)=\frac{dF}{dt},
\]

in \(\mathrm{N/s}\); it is relevant to the explosive push-off phase, but it cannot be inferred directly from the NMA because RFD was not a synthesized outcome. Breakdown regimes for \(h=v_{\text{to}}^2/(2g)\): non-vertical takeoff, air resistance, and landing asymmetry invalidate the simple ballistic interpretation.

Finally, the bias operators are probability statements: Egger’s test reports a dimensionless \(P\)-value, and the 1RM network has Egger \(P=0.009\) with a “Significant Bias” label [PAGE 28]. That is a publication/small-study signal, not a treatment effect. The CINeMA domains—within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, incoherence—are ordinal certainty judgments, not continuous measurements [PAGE 28–PAGE 33]. The exact breakdown regime for certainty is when a high SUCRA is paired with “low” or “very low” CINeMA, because then the ranking is a model artifact rather than decision-grade evidence.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from mechanical strength and impulse production to sprint acceleration, then to the stretch-shortening cycle and vertical jump, and finally to network meta-analysis as a decision-synthesis tool. The paper’s introduction frames soccer as a high-intensity intermittent sport in which sprinting, jumping, and COD are decisive [PAGE 1], and its mechanistic discussion links short-sprint acceleration to maximal force production and rate of force development [PAGE 12]. That is a classic retrocausal chain: observed short-sprint benefit is explained backward through force capacity, neural recruitment, and ground-reaction-force impulse. The lineage is plausible, but it is not a direct measurement of those mechanisms in the NMA; the NMA measures post-intervention sprint and jump outcomes.

The methodological genealogy is also explicit: Cochrane Handbook, PRISMA 2020, and PROSPERO registration CRD42024608868 [PAGE 1–PAGE 2]; search from January 2000 to 30 September 2025 [PAGE 2]; 31 RCTs after screening 6,546 records [PAGE 5]. This is a modern evidence-synthesis lineage, but it inherits older pairwise-review problems: node definitions, sparse loops, and outcome non-commensurability. The authors refine the taxonomy with a “dominant training stimulus” rule: a study arm was assigned to a single node only when one modality accounted for at least 60% of prescribed additional content [PAGE 3]. That improves within-node homogeneity but cannot eliminate residual heterogeneity from concurrent soccer training.

The multi-week traps are the most important genealogical weakness. The main text says intervention durations ranged from 1 to 16 weeks [PAGE 6], but Table S2 includes a 40-week Keiner study [PAGE 20]. That discrepancy matters because a 1-week flexibility study and a 40-week strength study are not exchangeable in a common-\(\tau^2\) model. A second trap is the immediate-post design: “No longer-term follow-up data were available in the included trials; therefore, the present network meta-analysis was restricted to immediate post-intervention effects” [PAGE 4]. Thus the genealogy cannot answer durability, detraining, or periodization. A third trap is concurrent training: the paper says the modalities are typically embedded within concurrent training weeks, and the observed effects should be interpreted as the added contribution of a training emphasis rather than stand-alone prescriptions [PAGE 1, PAGE 14]. A fourth trap is sequencing: the paper explicitly says the network “does not directly inform optimal sequencing or periodization within longer-term training plans” [PAGE 15]. So the historical lineage supports a narrow retrocausal claim—strength-oriented loading plausibly improves short-sprint acceleration and vertical jump over weeks—but not a universal “strength first” law, and not a long-sprint speed law.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive decision problem has three layers: the practitioner’s crisis habit, the analyst’s counter-trap, and the clock. Crisis habit regression occurs when a coach under congested schedules defaults to the modality with the most legible short-term signal. The paper gives that signal: Traditional Strength Training has SUCRA = 99.8% at 5 m and 89.9% at 20 m, with significant MDs versus Regular Training [PAGE 1]. Under time pressure, the coach reads “99.8%” as “almost certain” and regresses to heavy strength work. That habit is reinforced because the same modality also leads SJ (SUCRA = 86.2%) and CMJ (SUCRA = 73.0%) [PAGE 4]. The recursive belief is: “My athletes need to be faster over the first steps; strength improves force; therefore strength is the priority.” That is not irrational, but it is a habit formed under a decision clock, not a full synthesis of the evidence.

The counter-trap is overfitting to the ranking. SUCRA is a probability of rank order under the fitted network, not a probability that the intervention is best in a future population. The paper itself says the 40 m rankings “should be interpreted with caution” and that “these rankings reflect substantial statistical uncertainty rather than a definitive hierarchy of effectiveness” [PAGE 7]. The analyst’s counter-trap is to treat the 30 m Speed Training SUCRA of 74.2% as proof despite the non-significant contrast (MD = -0.16 s, 95% CI: -0.34 to 0.01) [PAGE 1]. A recursive theory of mind must model the practitioner’s meta-belief: the coach believes the analyst has already accounted for uncertainty; the analyst believes the coach will read the CI; both are wrong if the output foregrounds SUCRA. The correct counter-trap is a decision rule: use the contrast and prediction interval first, SUCRA second, and CINeMA certainty third.

The clock layer is both biological and institutional. Biological clock: adaptations occur over weeks, but the network synthesizes immediate post-intervention effects only [PAGE 4]. Institutional clock: the competitive season and concurrent training load shape what can be emphasized. The paper’s meta-regression found that baseline ability and maturation modified several outcomes (\(P<0.05\)), while playing standard and season phase did not significantly modify 5 m and 20 m effects [PAGE 6]. But for 40 m, season phase had \(P=0.043\) in the meta-regression table [PAGE 34], which is a warning that the long-sprint ranking may be clock-dependent. A coach in preseason, a coach in congested midseason, and a coach in off-season are not drawing from the same causal graph. The recursive failure mode is “clock collapse”: treating a 1-week flexibility trial, an 8-week strength trial, and a 40-week strength trial as if they sit on the same training calendar. The paper’s dosage meta-regression found no significant interaction for frequency, session duration, or intervention duration across the eight outcomes (all \(P\ge 0.05\)) [PAGE 35], but that is a sparse-network null, not proof of clock invariance. The safe mental model is: short-sprint strength emphasis is the default under a short-horizon clock; long-sprint speed emphasis is a hypothesis under a longer-horizon clock; and any claim beyond immediate post-intervention is outside the evidence.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer vetoes generalization more strongly than it vetoes the short-sprint signal. The evidence base is predominantly male professional or academy-level players, with women’s soccer and other levels not meaningfully represented [PAGE 6]. Baseline ability and maturation modified several outcomes [PAGE 6], and the meta-regression table shows baseline ability significant for 5 m (\(P=0.041\)), 20 m (\(P=0.020\)), SJ (\(P=0.015\)), CMJ (\(P=0.023\)), COD (\(P=0.019\)), and 1RM (\(P=0.024\)), while playing standard was significant for COD (\(P=0.041\)) and 1RM (\(P=0.013\)) [PAGE 34]. Therefore a physical-layer veto applies to claims about lower-level, female, or highly mature populations unless the same contrast is re-estimated. The short-sprint effect is not automatically transferable across maturation or baseline force capacity.

The biomechanical layer is governed by the traction and impulse constraints. For sprint acceleration,

\[
m\Delta v=\int F_{\text{net}}\,dt,
\qquad
F_{\text{net}}=F_{\text{prop}}-F_{\text{drag}}-F_{\text{friction}},
\qquad
F_{\text{prop}}\le \mu_s F_N.
\]

If surface friction \(\mu_s\) is reduced by rain, mud, or loose turf, the available horizontal impulse falls even if the athlete’s force capacity is unchanged. The paper’s sprint-testing table records grass, artificial turf, indoor courts, and indoor tracks, plus varied start procedures and timing methods [PAGE 38]. That creates a measurement-bias decomposition:

\[
\widehat{MD}=MD_{\text{true}}+B_{\text{timing}}+B_{\text{start}}+B_{\text{surface}}+B_{\text{weather}}+\varepsilon.
\]

A cross-layer veto is warranted when \(|B_{\text{timing}}+B_{\text{start}}+B_{\text{surface}}+B_{\text{weather}}|\) is of the same order as \(|\widehat{MD}|\). For 5 m, the estimate is -0.09 s and the timing sensitivity analysis is unchanged at -0.09 s (-0.11 to -0.07) after excluding video/app-based timing studies [PAGE 39], so the measurement-bias veto is weak for the short-sprint conclusion. For 20 m, the sensitivity estimate is -0.13 s (-0.19 to -0.05) [PAGE 39], also robust. For 30 m, the sensitivity estimate is -0.14 s (-0.31 to 0.03); for 40 m, -0.18 s (-0.41 to 0.05) [PAGE 39]. Those intervals cross zero, so the biomechanical/measurement veto is strong for long-sprint superiority claims.

The weather layer is the least measured and therefore the most speculative. The paper does not report wind, temperature, humidity, or precipitation as covariates. Wind acts through aerodynamic drag,

\[
F_{\text{drag}}=\frac{1}{2}\rho C_d A v^2,
\]

in newtons, where \(\rho\) is air density in \(\mathrm{kg/m^3}\), \(C_d\) is dimensionless, \(A\) is frontal area in \(\mathrm{m^2}\), and \(v\) is speed in \(\mathrm{m/s}\). Rain and mud act through \(\mu_s\) in the traction constraint. Because these are unmeasured, the correct adjudication is not “weather explains the effect”; it is “weather and surface are uneliminated confounders that can veto external validity, especially at 30–40 m where the intervals already cross zero.” The only defensible cross-layer proof is therefore asymmetric: the 5 m and 20 m strength signals survive timing-method sensitivity; the 30 m and 40 m speed signals do not survive imprecision; and the 1RM signal is independently vetoed by possible small-study effects, Egger \(P=0.009\) [PAGE 28], plus less stable leave-one-out behavior [PAGE 12].

Define a verifiable reward for a counterfactual trial as

\[
R_m=\mathbf{1}\!\left[\text{preregistered, complete-data RCT meets the stated threshold}\right],
\]

where \(\mathbf{1}[\cdot]\) is an indicator in \(\{0,1\}\). The three veto scenarios below are falsifiable because each has a pre-declared threshold and a predicted outcome from the source paper.

Scenario 1 — veto the 5 m Traditional Strength claim. New RCT: soccer players, Traditional Strength Training vs Regular Training, 5 m sprint, photoelectric timing, standardized standing start, same surface, adequate power. The paper predicts MD = -0.09 s (95% CI: -0.11 to -0.07) and PI -0.13 to -0.05 [PAGE 1, PAGE 37]. Veto rule: if the new trial’s TST-vs-Regular MD is \(\ge 0\) s or its 95% CI includes 0, the claim “Traditional Strength Training is most effective for 5 m” is vetoed for that population. Prediction: because the timing-method sensitivity analysis is unchanged at -0.09 s (-0.11 to -0.07) [PAGE 39], this veto should fail to trigger if the population and protocol match; the expected result is a small negative MD with a CI excluding zero.

Scenario 2 — veto the 30 m Speed Training ranking. New RCT: Speed Training vs Regular Training, 30 m sprint, same start and surface, with a sample large enough to estimate the contrast precisely. The paper’s estimate is MD = -0.16 s (95% CI: -0.34 to 0.01), PI -0.42 to 0.10 [PAGE 1, PAGE 37]. Veto rule: if the new trial’s 95% CI includes 0 or its point estimate is \(\ge 0\) s, the SUCRA-based “Speed Training is highest for 30 m” claim is vetoed as a superiority claim. Prediction: the paper’s own interval includes zero; the timing sensitivity estimate is -0.14 s (-0.31 to 0.03) [PAGE 39]. Therefore this veto has a meaningful chance of triggering, and the correct predicted outcome is “direction may persist, but superiority is not established.”

Scenario 3 — veto the supportive 1RM claim. New RCT: Traditional Strength Training vs Regular Training, 1RM in kg, preregistered with complete data and no small-study selection. The paper reports MD = 20.86 kg (95% CI: 10.73 to 30.98) and PI 1.84 to 39.88 [PAGE 4, PAGE 37], but also Egger \(P=0.009\) and less stable leave-one-out behavior [PAGE 28, PAGE 12]. Veto rule: if the new trial’s 1RM MD is \(<10\) kg or its 95% CI includes 0, the “supportive 1RM benefit” is vetoed as a robust claim. Prediction: because the 1RM network is secondary, has possible small-study effects, and a wide PI, this veto has a non-trivial chance of triggering; the expected result is either a smaller positive effect or a null, not a clean replication of 20.86 kg. These three vetoes preserve the paper’s strongest short-sprint/jump signal while blocking the overextended long-sprint and maximal-strength claims.
