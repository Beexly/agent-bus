The first audit point is that “pressure” is redefined operationally: “We define a pressure at the instance in which the area of the space in question falls below a certain threshold” [PAGE 7]. That threshold is not physical contact; it is a percentile of an observed spatial distribution: “we select the value at the 30th percentile of this distribution as our threshold” [PAGE 7]. Therefore every survival result inherits threshold sensitivity, coordinate sensitivity, and sample-selection sensitivity.

The second hop is geometric. The paper says “we define the pocket as a 10x12 yd2 rectangular space, being centered around the initial position of the ball” [PAGE 2]. It also says “the line of scrimmage (initial position of the ball) is where x=0, and the furthermost edge of the pocket is x=-10” [PAGE 2]. Thus the admissible spatial domain is a 120 yd² rectangle, with hash-dependent y offset. A Voronoi cell is the set of pocket points closer to a given player than to any other player [PAGE 3]. The operator is exact, but the interpretation is not: a lineman’s individual cell area can change because a neighboring blocker is beaten, not because that lineman’s own block improved or degraded. The paper itself flags this: “the Voronoi space of an individual linemen is susceptible to variations, either positive or negative, not indicative of their actual performance” [PAGE 4]. This is a gray-zone audit: aggregate Voronoi area can measure line-level compression, but individual Voronoi area is not a clean player-value operator.

The third hop is survival. The paper uses right-censoring because many plays end in a throw before a pressure event: “because much of the data we have is right-censored” [PAGE 7]. The censor horizon is “the first 35 frames (3.5 seconds) of pocket play after the snap” [PAGE 3]. The sample is “310 plays and 7094 total frames” [PAGE 3]. Let raw tracking coordinates be \(\mathbf r=(r_x,r_y)\) in source units, with stated raw ranges \(r_x\in(30,750)\) and \(r_y\in(30,350)\) [PAGE 2]. The implied uniform scale is \(6\) raw units per yard. A normalized field coordinate \(\mathbf q=(q_x,q_y)\) in yards can be written as
\[
q_x=\frac{r_x-30}{6}-10,\qquad
q_y=\frac{r_y-30}{6}-26.67,
\]
with units \(\text{yd}\). The pocket domain at snap is
\[
\mathcal P(t_0)=\{(x,y): -10\le x\le 0,\; y_0-6\le y\le y_0+6\},
\]
where \(y_0\) is the hash-dependent ball y-coordinate, so
\[
\mu(\mathcal P)=10\cdot 12=120\ \text{yd}^2.
\]
This matches the source’s “10x12 yd2” pocket statement [PAGE 2].

For players \(i=1,\ldots,N(t)\) with positions \(\mathbf p_i(t)\in\mathcal P(t_0)\), the Voronoi cell is
\[
V_i(t)=\left\{\mathbf q\in\mathcal P(t_0):\;
\|\mathbf q-\mathbf p_i(t)\|_2\le \|\mathbf q-\mathbf p_j(t)\|_2
\ \forall j\ne i\right\},
\]
with area
\[
A_i(t)=\int_{\mathcal P(t_0)}\mathbf 1_{V_i(t)}(\mathbf q)\,d\mathbf q
\quad [\text{yd}^2].
\]
The offensive aggregate and quarterback areas are
\[
A_O(t)=\sum_{i\in O}A_i(t),\qquad
A_Q(t)=A_{q(t)}(t),
\]
both in \(\text{yd}^2\). The source uses these as spatial summaries [PAGE 3–4].

For the bin method, partition \(\mathcal P\) into 30 bins \(B_k\), each \(2\times 2\ \text{yd}^2\), so \(\sum_k \mu(B_k)=120\ \text{yd}^2\). Bin occupation is
\[
A_{i,k}(t)=\int_{B_k}\mathbf 1_{V_i(t)}(\mathbf q)\,d\mathbf q
\quad [\text{yd}^2],
\]
and the bin-level mean is
\[
\bar A_k(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i,k}(t)
\quad [\text{yd}^2].
\]
The source describes partitioning “into 30 2x2 yd2 bins” [PAGE 4] and comparing bin profiles against league average [PAGE 5–6].

For survival, let \(A(t)\) be the area process in question. Let \(F_{A_{\text{throw}}}\) be the empirical distribution of that area at recorded forward-pass instants. The pressure threshold is
\[

\]
with \(\tau_q\) in \(\text{yd}^2\). The event time in frames is

\]
and the physical time is \(t_s=t_f/10\) seconds because the source states “10 observations recorded each second” [PAGE 2]. The censor time is \(C=35\) frames, equivalent to 3.5 s [PAGE 3]. The Kaplan-Meier estimator is
\[

\]
where \(d_i\) is the number of events at event time \(t_i\) and \(n_i\) is the risk set. \(\hat S(t)\) is dimensionless probability; \(t\) is in frames or seconds depending on indexing. The source uses “the nonparametric Kaplan-Meier estimator” [PAGE 7]. The log-rank test has null hypothesis that hazard functions are equivalent; the source states the alternative as hazard functions differ [PAGE 9]. A two-group form can be written
\[
\chi^2_{\text{LR}}
=
\frac{(O_1-E_1)^2}{\sum_i v_i},
\qquad
v_i=
\frac{n_{1i}n_{2i}d_i(n_i-d_i)}
{n_i^2(n_i-1)},
\]
dimensionless under the usual counting-process assumptions.

B_\theta=[150^\circ,300^\circ].

If quarterback orientation \(\phi_Q(t)\) were available, the pose-relative angle would be
\[
\tilde\theta=\theta-\phi_Q(t).
\]
The source explicitly notes “Information about player direction was not provided in the data source” [PAGE 8], so the paper’s operator is field-relative, not pose-relative.

1. Voronoi singularity: if \(\|\mathbf p_i(t)-\mathbf p_j(t)\|_2=0\) for \(i\ne j\), cell membership is non-unique and \(V_i\) requires a tie-breaking perturbation or shared boundary convention. If \(N(t)=0\), \(V_i\) is undefined.

3. Kaplan-Meier breakdown: if a risk set \(n_i=0\), the product is undefined; if all observations are censored before the first event, \(\hat S(t)=1\) for all estimable \(t\); with \(C=35\) frames the tail need not reach zero [PAGE 7].
4. Log-rank breakdown: the chi-square approximation is unreliable when expected event counts are small, when hazards cross, or when non-proportional hazards make a single chi-square summary non-explanatory.
5. Angular breakdown: without \(\phi_Q(t)\), a quarterback rotation can move true blindside pressure outside \(B_\theta\); the field-relative label is then a proxy, not the biomechanical blindside.

The paper’s objection is temporal and spatial: a lineman “conceding a pressure 1 second after the snap is considered equal to that of a lineman who concedes pressure after 2.4 seconds” [PAGE 2]. The spatial lineage includes Voronoi tessellation and the soccer space-creation work of Fernandez and Bornn [PAGE 3, reference 6]. The retrocausal genealogy is therefore: charted pressure → discrete PBWR → survival-of-pressure → spatial survival-of-area. Each step preserves some earlier assumption and adds a new one. The new one here is that area below a percentile is a meaningful pressure event.

Multi-week traps are where this lineage becomes fragile. First, the sample is six games from the 2020 regular season [PAGE 2], with one profiled play from Ravens versus Chiefs in Week 3 [PAGE 3]. A single-week diagram can generate a plausible narrative about a left-side flank and central ingress [PAGE 4], but that narrative is not a multi-week law. Fifth, the paper’s own caveat is decisive: “compounding this relatively small sample of observations with the data’s occasional noisiness, we’re unable to draw authoritative conclusions” [PAGE 3]. A multi-week trap is therefore treating a sample-level survival separation as a stable team trait without re-estimating threshold, censor horizon, handedness, and pocket filter in each week or block.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive model has three agents: the quarterback, the offensive line, and the defense. The quarterback holds a pressure belief \(b_Q(t)\) and a hazard estimate; the defense holds a model of that belief, \(b_D(b_Q(t))\); the line holds a sustain policy. Crisis habit regression occurs when \(b_Q(t)\) crosses an internal threshold and the quarterback reverts to a prior policy, such as leaving the pocket or throwing earlier. The paper’s sampling rule excludes many such plays, because it keeps only quarterbacks who remain inside the pocket until release [PAGE 2–3]. That exclusion is not neutral: it censors the behavioral regression that high pressure is supposed to provoke. The paper acknowledges the difficulty: it deemed it “too difficult to control for the fact that different quarterbacks have different proclivities to exit the pocket at different times, sensing pressure differently” [PAGE 3]. Thus any recursive claim about quarterback response is conditioned on a quarterback who did not exit.

Counter-trap dynamics appear in the bin and angular results. The defense can concede space in one region while compressing the quarterback’s immediate neighborhood. The paper’s own example is a diagonal underperformance from the top-left of the pocket toward the center, “especially in bins 13 and 18, indicating pressure to the quarterback’s center” [PAGE 6]. A naive aggregate Voronoi reading can show acceptable space at the top of the pocket while the space underneath is collapsing. The bin method is the counter-trap detector because it distinguishes where the space is, not just how much space exists. The clock is the survival axis. The paper observes that “after 30 frames, the probability of a pressure in the subsequent frame becomes exceedingly likely” [PAGE 8], and that “within 22 frames (2.2 seconds) of the snap, the periphery of the pocket is largely dominated by the defense” [PAGE 5]. The recursive interaction is therefore: defense induces a spatial feint, quarterback updates pressure belief, line tries to sustain the quarterback’s neighborhood, and the clock converts spatial loss into hazard. Crisis habit regression is the quarterback’s fallback under rising hazard; counter-trap is the defense’s exploitation of that fallback; clock is the censor-bounded test of whether the fallback occurred before or after the spatial threshold.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
