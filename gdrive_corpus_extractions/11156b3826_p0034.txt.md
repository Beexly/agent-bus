<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage plus multi-week traps.

The [PAGE 25] ballots are artifacts of the pre-AP, pre-UPI consensus era. Walter Camp's influence had not yet fully expired (Camp died in 1925), and the All-America idea was still a journalist/coach editorial construct rather than an institutionalized award; the first major wire-service poll (AP) would not appear until 1936. The selector population is geographically and institutionally narrow: Harvard, Princeton, Yale, Dartmouth, and Army dominate every ballot, with Notre Dame (Eichenlaub, LeGore, Spiegel, Cruikshank, Fleming), Pittsburgh (Peck), Pennsylvania (Journeay), Cornell (Barrett, Bradlee, O'Hearn), and Washington & Jefferson (Spiegel, LeGore, Cruikshank, Fleming) as the only non-Ivy/Army intruders. This is an Eastern establishment canon, not a nationally representative sample.

A retrocausal trap is embedded in any attempt to project these thirteen names forward. The 1914 season operated under rules materially different from 1915 and from the modern game: the forward pass existed but was heavily restricted, the end zone had only been introduced in 1912, the four-down system was recent, and there was no two-platoon specialization—players like Mahan and Barrett appear at multiple positions precisely because single-platoon football required two-way participation. Graduation in spring 1915 would strip the consensus team of its seniors; World War I would suspend or curtail college football in 1917–1918; and equipment (leather helmets without face masks, minimal padding) imposed a completely different injury and performance physics. Treating Hardwick, Ballin, or Pennock as "predictive" of 1915 All-America status, let alone modern NFL projection, is a retrocausal fallacy: it conditions a later outcome on an earlier honor roll while ignoring attrition, rule-regime shifts, and the absence of film or biomechanical data. The multi-week trap is similarly acute: these ballots were compiled after the season's final games (including late November Army–Navy and intersectional clashes), so late-season recency and rivalry visibility overweight December reputation and underweight September–October form. A selector who saw Harvard in October but not November would be systematically underrepresented relative to a New York editor reading telegraphic reports of the season's finale.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Each selector was not observing players in a vacuum but modeling the expectations of other selectors and of their readership. The dominant habit is crisis regression to the familiar: when a selector faced uncertainty about a fringe tackle or guard, the ballot regresses to Ivy League staples—Pennock (Harvard) appears 13/13, Hardwick 13/13, Ballin 12/13—because these names carried established reputational capital and minimized the risk of an idiosyncratic, embarrassing omission. This is a Bayesian prior weighted toward prestige programs (Harvard, Princeton, Yale, Army), which is itself a recursive expectation: selectors picked Pennock because they expected other selectors to pick Pennock.

The counter-trap operates at the margin. Frank G. Menke's INS ballot substitutes T. J. Coolidge (Harvard) for Merillat at end and Weyand (Army) for Ballin at tackle; the Boston American substitutes Ghee (Dartmouth) at quarter-back and Shenk (Princeton) for Spears at guard; L. H. Bankhart inserts Abell (Colgate) and Brann (Yale). These are small deviations—usually one position per ballot—consistent with a selector attempting to signal independent judgment without forfeiting the safety of the core consensus. Donald G. Herring's Daily Princetonian ballot prints the OCR variant "Hardwiek" but otherwise tracks the Ivy/Army core, suggesting a house style tuned to Princeton-adjacent readers while still anchoring on Pennock and Ballin.

The clock operates through publication deadline. Ballots were assembled post-season from newspaper coverage and personal observation; selectors who relied on late-season wire reports overweighted November games (e.g., Army's late schedule, Harvard–Yale). A player who faded in October but starred in November—Barrett at Cornell, for instance—gains votes relative to a consistently strong October performer who was quiet in November. Recursively, New York City selectors (Evening News, Sun, Globe, Post, Herald, Mail, Press, American, plus Menke's INS) share a media ecosystem that permits copying or mutual reinforcement, so the apparent 13-selector consensus partially collapses under a model of correlated observation: the "unanimity" of Pennock and Hardwick may reflect a single information cascade rather than thirteen independent estimates. Crisis habit regression, counter-trap differentiation, and clock-driven recency jointly explain why the distribution is bimodal: a near-unanimous Ivy/Army core surrounded by a long tail of one- and two-vote regional alternatives.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather vetoes.

Physical/biomechanical veto: Any attempt to compare the [PAGE 25] All-America players to modern college or professional athletes must be vetoed. In 1914 linemen typically ranged roughly 190–220 lb with no modern strength-and-conditioning infrastructure, no face masks, no helmets comparable to contemporary polycarbonate shells, and no two-platoon substitution; momentum, collision energy, and injury kinetics were therefore in a different mechanical regime. A formal veto condition is: if comparing kinetic energy at contact E_k = ½mv², the mass m and velocity v distributions are non-overlapping between eras, rendering any head-to-head "better/worse" judgment physically meaningless.

Weather veto: The selections on [PAGE 25] were retrospective editorial judgments made indoors after the season; no meteorological variable (temperature, precipitation, wind) enters any ballot B_{s,p}. Therefore weather cannot veto the selection act itself. However, weather vetoes any use of this list as a predictive lineup for a specific contest: a November 1914 game played in freezing rain would differentially affect a pass-oriented backfield versus a mass-formation line, but the All-America roster contains no game-condition conditioning variable.

Injury/availability layer: No injury reports appear on [PAGE 25]; a player could be named All-America while carrying a late-season injury unknown to the selector. This vetoes any inference that the consensus eleven were simultaneously available and healthy for a hypothetical all-star contest.

Cross-layer adjudication: Because the object of analysis is an honor roll, not a game-day lineup, in-game physical and weather vetoes do not invalidate the historical frequencies f_{i,p}. They do, however, impose a hard boundary: the output of this page is a ranked list of reputational selections, not a biophysically valid team sheet. Any downstream model that consumes f_{i,p} as if it were a probability of in-game availability or modern performance must be rejected at the veto layer.
