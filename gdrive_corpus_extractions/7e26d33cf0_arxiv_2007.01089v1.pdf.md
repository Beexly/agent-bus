First, the cognitive premise: “People instinctively suppress blinks during attention-grabbing events and synchronously generate blinks at attention break points in videos” [PAGE 1]. That makes a population blink rate a candidate temporal signal. Second, the measurement premise: blink onsets are extracted from pupil diameter using a near-infrared eye tracker at 120 Hz, and “the percentage of people blinking was analyzed in each video frame” [PAGE 3]. Third, the learning premise: 18 OpenPose joints over 90 frames are converted into a 90 × 36 input and mapped by a three-layer 1D-CNN to the blink probability rate at the last frame [PAGE 2]. The inference chain is therefore: viewer attention → blink suppression/release → aggregate blink rate R(t) → pose-sequence regression \hat R_t = f_\theta(X_t) → threshold rule for highlights. The gray-zone audit begins here: the paper’s “94%” is not classification accuracy. It is the fraction of clips with a significant positive correlation between estimated and actual blink rate: “45 of the 48 video clips (94%) had a significant positive correlation” [PAGE 4]. The “72%” jump result is likewise a fraction of 272 jump events with significant positive correlation, not event-detection accuracy [PAGE 4]. Those distinctions matter because a model can have high clip-level correlation yet poor frame-level localization, especially with overlapping 3 s windows and 1 s stride [PAGE 3]. The surrogate test is the paper’s defense against long-series pseudo-correlation: predicted blink-rate time series are randomly shuffled while preserving the distribution, 1000 random correlation coefficients are generated, and a t-test is applied with Bonferroni correction at alpha = 0.05/48 [PAGE 4]. This is a temporal-structure null, but it is not a causal identification proof. It does not rule out pose nuisance cues such as camera cuts, audience contamination, replay edits, or skater identity; it only tests whether the predicted sequence’s time ordering carries information about the actual sequence. The highlight rule is also a local-threshold operator: “at least two standard deviations below the mean across 5 video frames” [PAGE 5]. If the local standard deviation is zero, the threshold degenerates; if the blink rate is near zero or constant, no highlight can be defined. The jump-event average is physically plausible: both estimated and actual blink rates “started to decrease gradually from 0.5 s just before the jump, showed a sharp decline with the jump and increased 1 s after the jump” [PAGE 4]. But 28% of jumps fail the significance test, so the correct claim is a population-average temporal coupling, not universal per-event detection. Finally, the paper’s own generalization boundary is explicit: “we need to verify that this method can be applied to other sports videos and daily life videos” [PAGE 6]. The multi-hop audit therefore rejects any overclaim that the model directly measures interest; it measures a pose-to-population-blink-rate mapping validated on 48 figure-skating clips.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(t\) index video frames at 30 FPS, so one frame is \(\Delta t = 1/30\ \mathrm{s} = 33.3\ \mathrm{ms}\). Let \(p=1,\dots,N(t)\) index viewers with valid eye-tracker data. Define the blink indicator
\[
b_p(t)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{blink onset for viewer }p\text{ at frame }t,\\0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
\]
and the instantaneous blink rate
\[
R(t)=\frac{1}{N(t)}\sum_{p=1}^{N(t)} b_p(t).
\]
\(R(t)\) is dimensionless probability in \([0,1]\), or percent when multiplied by 100. The source says “the percentage of people blinking was analyzed in each video frame” [PAGE 3], so percent is the paper’s natural reporting scale. The eye tracker samples at 120 Hz, i.e. \(1/120\ \mathrm{s}=8.33\ \mathrm{ms}\) [PAGE 3], so the tracker-to-frame resampling is an assumption not fully specified in the source. The blink-onset detector is qualitative in the paper: “detecting a combination of rapid increase in pupil diameter followed by a rapid decrease within 0.5 s” [PAGE 3]. The source does not report numerical pupil-diameter thresholds, so an exact detector equation cannot be reconstructed without invention.

For the pose input, let \(x_i\in\mathbb{R}^{36}\) be the 18-joint \(X,Y\) coordinate vector at frame \(i\). The input tensor is
\[
X_t=\big[x_{t-89},x_{t-90+1},\dots,x_t\big]\in\mathbb{R}^{90\times 36},
\]
where the 90 frames span 3 s and the sliding window advances by 1 s [PAGE 2]. Coordinate units are image-coordinate units: pixels if unnormalized, or normalized image coordinates if preprocessed; the source only states “the X and Y coordinate positions across 3 seconds (90 frames) were converted into a 90 × 36 image” [PAGE 2]. The CNN estimator is
\[
\hat R_t=f_\theta(X_t),
\]
with three convolution layers, kernel size 8, filters 64-128-64, followed by average pooling and a fully connected layer; the output is the blink probability rate at the 90th frame [PAGE 2–3]. The training loss is RMSE:
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(R_{t_m}-\hat R_{t_m}\right)^2},
\]
where \(M\) is the number of samples and the loss units are the same as \(R\) (dimensionless probability or percentage points). The source states optimization used “Adam optimizer with learning rate of 0.001,” maximum 100 epochs, and batch size 4,096 [PAGE 3]. For each clip \(c\), the observed correlation is \(r_c=\operatorname{corr}(R_c,\hat R_c)\); 1000 surrogate correlations are formed by shuffling the predicted series in time, \(r_{c,s}=\operatorname{corr}(R_c,\operatorname{shuffle}_s(\hat R_c))\), \(s=1,\dots,1000\) [PAGE 4]. The paper then uses the surrogate mean and variance in a t-test with Bonferroni correction at \(\alpha=0.05/48\) [PAGE 4].

The highlight operator is, under the assumption that the 5-frame window is centered at \(t\),
\[
m_t=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{k=-2}^{2}\hat R_{t+k},\qquad
s_t=\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=-2}^{2}\left(\hat R_{t+k}-m_t\right)^2},
\]
\[
H_t=\mathbf{1}\{\hat R_t < m_t - 2s_t\}.
\]
\(H_t\) is a Boolean highlight indicator; \(m_t\) and \(s_t\) have blink-rate units. The source says “at least two standard deviations below the mean across 5 video frames” [PAGE 5]. Breakdown regimes: (i) \(N(t)=0\) or invalid eye data makes \(R(t)\) undefined; (ii) \(s_t=0\) makes the threshold non-strict and can suppress all detections; (iii) OpenPose confidence below 0.7 triggers exclusion and interpolation [PAGE 3], so severe occlusion or identity swap invalidates \(X_t\); (iv) clock misalignment beyond the 0.2 s synchronization tolerance [PAGE 1] weakens \(r_c\); (v) out-of-domain sports or camera styles exceed the paper’s validated scope [PAGE 6].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from endogenous-eyeblink physiology to video summarization and pose-based neural regression. The paper cites Stern et al. for the claim that “spontaneous blinks are controlled directly by the central nervous system according to visual attention” [PAGE 6], and Nakano et al. for blink synchronization: “spontaneous blinks were synchronized within 0.2 s among people viewing the same video” [PAGE 1]. The interest link is stated as “the higher the interest in the video, the higher the blink synchronization among people” [PAGE 1]. That lineage is retrocausal in the sense that a late-stage machine-learning result—pose-only blink-rate estimation—is justified by older psychophysiological findings about attention and blinking. The video-side lineage is the standard three-branch summarization taxonomy in Section 2: unsupervised video-feature methods [1–9], human-reaction supervised methods [10–14], and neural-network methods [28–31] [PAGE 2]. Figure-skating scoring and action-quality work [21, 25] supply the domain-specific contrast: prior methods detect athletic events such as jumps and spins but “have failed to detect highlights related to artistic expression” [PAGE 5]. The proposed method claims to detect both because blink rate reflects “not only human cognition but also emotion” [PAGE 6].

Multi-week traps are numerous. First, the “94%” and “72%” figures are correlation-significance rates, not accuracy; a team that reports them as accuracy has misread the evaluation [PAGE 4]. Second, leakage is a serious trap because the 3 s windows overlap with 1 s stride [PAGE 3]; the paper correctly uses leave-one-out by video clip: “The dataset extracted from 47 out of 48 video clips was used as training data, and the remaining one video clip was used as test data” [PAGE 3]. A random frame split would leak same-clip pose and blink structure. Third, the surrogate test must shuffle the predicted time series while preserving its distribution; the paper says “randomly shuffling the time-series of the predicted blink rate” [PAGE 4]. Shuffling labels across clips or destroying the marginal distribution would invalidate the null. Fourth, multiple-comparison correction is across 48 clips at \(\alpha=0.05/48\) [PAGE 4], not across every frame; per-frame testing would require a different correction. Fifth, eye-tracker and video clocks differ: 120 Hz tracker versus 30 FPS video [PAGE 3], and the paper’s synchronization tolerance is 0.2 s [PAGE 1]. A multi-week implementation that ignores resampling and lag alignment can fabricate or erase correlations. Sixth, generalization is unproven beyond figure skating; the conclusion explicitly says future work must verify “other sports videos and daily life videos” [PAGE 6].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind problem is that the model never observes interest directly. It observes skater pose and learns to predict a population’s blink rate, which is a behavioral proxy for the viewers’ latent attention. This creates three failure modes. First, crisis habit regression: the learned habit is “low blink rate means highlight,” but low blink can also arise from fatigue, dry eye, cognitive load, or staring at empty ice. The paper itself reports that 8% of detected scenes were “just sliding” [PAGE 5], which is exactly the gray zone where a low-blink artifact could be misread as interest. Counter-trap: if the model is truly tracking attention rather than a pose-blink nuisance correlation, then time-shuffling the pose input or swapping the skater’s joints should destroy the correlation; if performance survives, the model has learned a confound. Clock: the synchronization window is 0.2 s [PAGE 1], the video frame is 33.3 ms, and the tracker sample is 8.33 ms [PAGE 3]; an offset of six video frames already saturates the stated tolerance. A verifiable clock audit is to compute the lag \(\tau^\*=\arg\max_\tau \operatorname{corr}(R(t),\hat R(t+\tau))\); if \(|\tau^\*|>0.2\ \mathrm{s}\), the temporal claim is vetoed. A second recursive trap is population shift: 24 clips used 38 participants and 24 clips used 12 participants [PAGE 3], so the mapping from pose to aggregate blink rate depends on audience composition. The model’s “theory of mind” is therefore not a universal viewer model; it is a dataset-specific coupling between figure-skating motion and a particular viewer pool’s blink behavior. Counter-trap for that shift is to hold out entire clips and, ideally, entire viewer groups; the paper’s leave-one-out clip protocol controls clip leakage but not viewer-pool shift. The correct conclusion is conditional: the model estimates a population blink-rate trajectory when the viewers, sport, camera grammar, and clock alignment match the training regime.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical/video layer: the pose front end is vetoed when OpenPose confidence is low or identity selection fails. The paper says “the joint data with a low confidence score of estimation (< 0.7) was excluded, and interpolated it with the data before and after” and selects “the person whose body area was the largest in each video frame” [PAGE 3]. Thus a physical veto condition is \(V_{\text{pose}}(t)=1\) if any critical joint has confidence \(<0.7\), if the largest-body-area subject is not the skater, or if occlusion/camera cut makes interpolation semantically invalid; then \(\hat R_t\) should not be treated as a valid highlight estimate. Biomechanical layer: the validated event is the jump, with 272 jump events and a significant positive correlation in 72% [PAGE 4]. The average temporal signature is specific: decrease beginning 0.5 s before the jump, sharp decline with the jump, and increase 1 s after [PAGE 4]. A biomechanical veto for an individual event is therefore \(V_{\text{bio}}(t)=1\) when the event is outside the trained action vocabulary or when the pose trajectory is too occluded to resolve the jump/spin signature; the paper’s 28% nonsignificant jump fraction is direct evidence that per-event claims need this veto. Weather/environment layer: the provided pages contain no weather, temperature, humidity, lighting, or venue variables; the dataset is “competition videos for broadcast in the Olympics or formal international figure skating matches” [PAGE 3]. Therefore no weather-based veto can be proven from the source. The only environmental confounds that can be adjudicated are broadcast-camera edits, lighting/occlusion, audience contamination, and eye-tracker validity. Combined adjudication is a logical AND, not a ratio: a frame is a valid highlight only if \(H_t=1\) and \(V_{\text{pose}}(t)=0\) and \(V_{\text{clock}}(t)=0\) and \(V_{\text{domain}}(t)=0\), where \(V_{\text{clock}}\) vetoes lag beyond 0.2 s and \(V_{\text{domain}}\) vetoes out-of-domain sport/camera conditions [PAGE 1, PAGE 6]. This preserves the paper’s physical claim while refusing unsupported weather causation.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Temporal-shuffle veto. For each held-out clip, compute \(r_{\text{obs}}=\operatorname{corr}(R,\hat R)\). Generate 1000 surrogates by shuffling the predicted time series, as in “randomly shuffling the time-series of the predicted blink rate” [PAGE 4], and apply the stated Bonferroni level \(\alpha=0.05/48\) [PAGE 4]. Veto predicate: fail the clip if \(r_{\text{obs}}\) does not exceed the surrogate-based critical value. Prediction: the paper’s protocol should pass 45/48 clips (94%) and fail 3/48; an implementation reporting 48/48 should be audited for leakage or an incorrectly constructed null.

2. Pose-identity ablation veto. Replace the skater’s 18-joint trajectory with (a) the second-largest-body-area person’s joints, (b) time-shuffled skater joints, or (c) random joint permutations, then retrain and re-evaluate. Veto predicate: if the ablated model still achieves the surrogate-significant correlation or preserves the jump-decline signature at 0.5 s before to 1 s after [PAGE 4], the original model is relying on nuisance cues rather than the skater’s biomechanics. Prediction: valid pose information should collapse under these ablations; if it does not, the “blink-rate” mapping is confounded by camera/audience/identity structure.

3. Leave-one-out leakage veto. Compare the paper’s clip-level leave-one-out protocol—“The dataset extracted from 47 out of 48 video clips was used as training data, and the remaining one video clip was used as test data” [PAGE 3]—against a random frame split within clips. Veto predicate: reject the random-frame result if it inflates the significant-clip fraction or jump-event correlation above the leave-one-out value, because overlapping 3 s windows with 1 s stride [PAGE 3] leak same-clip temporal structure. Prediction: the random-frame split should overestimate performance; the leave-one-out split should reproduce the reported 94% clip and 72% jump significance levels, and any large deviation is a verifiable leakage flag.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
