The source’s detection pipeline is a multi-stage physical-to-statistical derivation, not a single threshold test. The causal chain is: player-to-player or player-to-ground collision → skull linear acceleration and angular velocity → instrumented mouthguard signal at “1000 Hz sampling rate for 100 ms (10 ms pre-trigger and 90 ms post-trigger with 10 g trigger)” [PAGE 2] → anatomical-frame rotation and filtering → 411 time-domain, PSD, wavelet, and biomechanical-model features [PAGE 4–5] → infrared placement gating → RBF-SVM classification → leave-one-out cross-validation and independent youth testing. The key gray-zone claim is that peak kinematics are not sufficient: the paper reports that impact and nonimpact peak linear acceleration distributions overlap, while low-frequency PSD/WT features are more discriminative [PAGE 6–7]. A simple acceleration-threshold classifier “did not perform much better than randomly assigning classes with a probability of impact of 50% (with area under the ROC curve close to 0.5)” [PAGE 9]. Thus the classifier’s validity rests on frequency-domain structure linked to mass/inertia differences: “head impacts involve more massive collisions between players and may thus exhibit lower frequency dynamics with higher power” [PAGE 11].

The first audit hop is physical validity. The mouthguard measures skull motion via the upper dentition, with “approximately 10% error in measuring peak head linear acceleration, angular acceleration, and angular velocity in dummy head validation” [PAGE 2]. That is a bounded calibration uncertainty; it does not validate off-teeth recordings. Hence the IR gate is not optional. The paper states “73% of the nonimpacts and 23% of the recordings during helmet contact periods were low IR, or off-teeth” [PAGE 11], and “about 71% were captured on the mouthguard, and only around 77% of the mouthguard recordings had high IR readings” [PAGE 12]. Any inference that ignores IR gating conflates sensor-placement noise with head-impact dynamics.

The second hop is temporal labeling. Video review at “1080 p 30 fps” with two or more views provides ground-truth labels, but synchronization uses a “±2 second time window” because “the mouthguard timestamp had a resolution of 1 second” [PAGE 4]. Inter-rater reliability for helmet contact is “88%” [PAGE 3]. Therefore labels are probabilistic and windowed, not exact event-time stamps. The classifier is trained only where video can support a confident label; the authors excluded no-view and uncertain-contact periods [PAGE 3–4]. This selection creates a gray zone: the classifier is validated on clearly visible activity, while “almost all recordings from when the player was buried under a pile were excluded from training” [PAGE 12]. That exclusion is a generalization boundary, not a failure of the reported cross-validation.

The third hop is statistical. There are 411 features but only “156 impacts and 231 nonimpacts” [PAGE 6], and PCA shows “90% of the variance could be explained by the first principal component” [PAGE 9]. Feature selection therefore must be aggressive; “fewer than ten features optimized classifier performance” [PAGE 1]. The paper reports 87.2% sensitivity and 93.2% precision in collegiate cross-validation and over 90% sensitivity and precision on an independent youth dataset [PAGE 1]. But the F-measure-optimized classifier had excellent collegiate cross-validation yet poorer youth specificity and precision (68.5% and 75.0%) [PAGE 10], and the authors conclude “the F-measure classifier likely overfit to the collegiate football training and validation data” [PAGE 11]. The valid claim is therefore not “the classifier generalizes universally”; it is “the AUC-optimized classifier generalized to a small youth set under this mouthguard design.” The paper itself limits scope: “The classifier is only evaluated to be used with the specific instrumented mouthguard design used in this study for classifying collegiate American football helmet impacts” [PAGE 12], and “Retraining is likely required for any device that does not have the same mechanical and electrical design” [PAGE 12]. The gray-zone audit thus yields a narrow warrant: impact-detection performance is established for this sensor family, this labeling protocol, and these player samples, with strong but bounded evidence.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(TP,TN,FP,FN\in\mathbb{N}_0\) be the counts of true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives. The source defines them as: \(TP\) = a head impact classified as a head impact; \(TN\) = a nonimpact classified as a nonimpact; \(FP\) = a nonimpact classified as a head impact; \(FN\) = a head impact classified as a nonimpact [PAGE 6]. The reported performance operators are:

\[
\mathrm{Sensitivity}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad
\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},
\]
\[
\mathrm{Specificity}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP},\qquad
\mathrm{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+FP+TN+FN},
\]
\[
F_1=\frac{2\cdot \mathrm{Sensitivity}\cdot \mathrm{Precision}}{\mathrm{Sensitivity}+\mathrm{Precision}}.
\]

These are the paper’s Equations 1–5 [PAGE 6]. Counts are dimensionless; sensitivity, precision, specificity, accuracy, and \(F_1\) are proportions in \([0,1]\). The ROC AUC is a dimensionless area in \([0,1]\):
\[
\mathrm{AUC}_{ROC}=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{TPR}(s)\,d\mathrm{FPR}(s),
\]
with \(\mathrm{TPR}(s)=\mathrm{Sensitivity}(s)\) and \(\mathrm{FPR}(s)=1-\mathrm{Specificity}(s)\) over threshold \(s\). The paper reports \(\mathrm{AUC}_{ROC}=0.96\) for AUC optimization and \(0.98\) for F-measure optimization in collegiate cross-validation [PAGE 10]. The PR AUC is likewise dimensionless; the paper reports \(0.95\) and \(0.94\), respectively [PAGE 10]. These metrics assume a fixed ground-truth label set and a fixed decision threshold; they are not physical invariants.

The classifier operator is an SVM with a radial basis function kernel: “We trained an SVM classifier with a radial basis function kernel using the MATLAB fitcsvm function” [PAGE 5]. The standard kernel form is
\[
K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')=\exp\!\left(-\gamma\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\|_2^2\right),\qquad \gamma>0,
\]
with decision function \(f(\mathbf{x})=\operatorname{sign}\!\left(\sum_i \alpha_i y_i K(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x})+b\right)\). The paper does not report \(\gamma\), \(C\), or feature-standardization details for the SVM; that omission is a reproducibility gray zone because mixed-unit features (g, rad/s, rad/s², PSD amplitudes, WT amplitudes) require scaling for a Euclidean RBF kernel to be physically interpretable.

Feature units and sampling assumptions: linear acceleration is reported in \(g\), where \(1\,g=9.80665\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\); angular velocity in \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\); angular acceleration in \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}\). The sensor records 6-DOF kinematics at 1000 Hz for 100 ms [PAGE 2]. Accelerometer cutoff is 500 Hz and gyroscope cutoff is 184 Hz, filtered with a fourth-order Butterworth filter [PAGE 4]. PSD features are extracted from 10–200 Hz for linear acceleration and 10–180 Hz for angular acceleration [PAGE 5]. Under a periodogram convention, linear-acceleration PSD has units \(g^2\,\mathrm{Hz^{-1}}\) or \((\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}})^2\,\mathrm{Hz^{-1}}\); angular-acceleration PSD has units \((\mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}})^2\,\mathrm{Hz^{-1}}\). The paper says “PSD amplitude” and “WT amplitude” without stating normalization [PAGE 5], so cross-study WT amplitude comparison is not warranted unless normalization is matched. A 100 ms window gives nominal frequency resolution on the order of \(1/0.1\,\mathrm{s}=10\,\mathrm{Hz}\), which is consistent with the paper’s lowest PSD/WT bins at 10 Hz [PAGE 5].

Breakdown regimes are exact and must be stated. Sensitivity is undefined when \(TP+FN=0\); precision is undefined when \(TP+FP=0\); specificity is undefined when \(TN+FP=0\); accuracy is undefined when the total count is zero; \(F_1\) is undefined when sensitivity plus precision is zero. AUC is undefined if only one class is present. The feature ratios used in the biomechanical model — ratios between angular acceleration about an anatomical axis and linear acceleration along the remaining normal axes [PAGE 5] — have a singularity when the linear-acceleration denominator is zero or near zero. The PSD/WT upper bounds break down for signals above the sensor bandwidth: with a 1000 Hz sample rate, the paper chooses 200 Hz for linear acceleration and 180 Hz for angular acceleration because the gyroscope bandwidth is 184 Hz and because “sample rate is suggested to be six times the signal bandwidth” under SAE J211 guidance [PAGE 5]. High-frequency content above these cutoffs is not evidence of absence; it is outside the validated measurement band.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage runs from simple threshold sensors to feature-based machine learning. Earlier systems included the Head Impact Telemetry System, G-Force Tracker, skin-adhered patches, and xPatch. The source reports that in a HITS validation paper “19% of dummy impacts were incorrectly removed” [PAGE 2]; in cadaver drop tests HITS detected 79.6%, GFT 87.0%, and skin patches 86.1% and 75.7% [PAGE 2]; a field validation of xPatch found “more than 30% of the recordings to be spurious” [PAGE 2]; and video verification found only 65% verifiable for GFT and 32% for xPatch [PAGE 2]. This lineage explains why the current paper does not treat peak acceleration as sufficient. It inherits the lesson that sensor placement, false positives, and false negatives are first-order problems.

The methodological lineage also includes fall detection and human-activity recognition using Fourier and wavelet features [PAGE 2]. The paper explicitly cites prior work where “Fourier Transform features (frequency-domain) and Wavelet Transform (WT) features (time and frequency-domain) have proven useful in detecting falls and other human activities” [PAGE 2]. The immediate precursor is the authors’ pilot work: “PSD features combined with an SVM classifier in differentiating dummy head impacts and laboratory-simulated nonimpacts with 98% sensitivity and 99.98% precision” [PAGE 2]. The current field study is a retrocausal correction to that laboratory result: field conditions are more complex, labels are video-derived, and the classifier must reject high-IR nonimpacts such as biting or chewing the mouthguard [PAGE 11].

Multi-week traps are visible in the design. First, device wear and individual variability forced IR thresholds “on a per-subject-event basis (game or practice) because of individual variabilities in the mouthguard and wear and tear over time” [PAGE 4]. A threshold fixed across weeks would drift. Second, the motivating outcome is cumulative: repeated impacts over a season are linked to white matter integrity changes [PAGE 1], and the paper cites long-term exposure concerns including chronic traumatic encephalopathy [PAGE 1]. Thus a single-event classifier is not the same as a season-level exposure monitor; missed low-severity impacts accumulate. Third, retraining is a multi-week trap: the classifier is tied to “the specific instrumented mouthguard design” and “Retraining is likely required for any device that does not have the same mechanical and electrical design” [PAGE 12]. Fourth, the youth test is small: 16 impacts and 16 nonimpacts [PAGE 9]. That is enough to show consistency, not enough to certify a new population across a season. The retrocausal reading is that the later field result “87.2% sensitivity and 93.2% precision” [PAGE 1] is not a universal detector; it is the end of a lineage that has progressively replaced threshold heuristics with frequency-domain and biomechanical features, while inheriting strict device- and label-specific constraints.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis habit regression is the operator’s default under time pressure: use peak linear acceleration because it is familiar, even though the paper shows that “common peak kinematic features did not show as much distinction between impacts and nonimpacts compared to some frequency-domain features” [PAGE 7]. In a crisis, a reviewer or field operator may regress to “10 g trigger” or a single acceleration cutoff. That habit is a trap because the source states that a threshold-only classifier performed near random guessing on ROC [PAGE 9]. The counter-trap is to overcorrect by trusting the most complex model without checking the label and placement layers. The F-measure-optimized classifier is exactly that counter-trap: it had strong collegiate cross-validation but “likely overfit” [PAGE 11], and on youth data its specificity fell to 68.5% and precision to 75.0% [PAGE 10]. The disciplined model of mind must represent three perspectives simultaneously: the field operator wants low false positives during practice; the researcher wants sensitivity for rare concussion events; the reviewer wants reproducible generalization. The paper adjudicates these by recommending sensitivity and precision rather than sensitivity and specificity because “even 90% specificity would lead to 100 false positive detections, which is twice the number of actual head impacts” [PAGE 13].

The clock layer is the temporal contract. Recordings are 100 ms with 10 ms pre-trigger and 90 ms post-trigger [PAGE 2]; video is 30 fps [PAGE 3]; mouthguard timestamps have 1 s resolution and synchronization uses ±2 s [PAGE 4]. Any mental model that treats a “hit” as an instant is wrong. The classifier sees a short window, the labeler sees a video interval, and synchronization can shift the pairing by up to two seconds. Therefore the operator must not infer that a classified impact at one timestamp is the exact video-observed contact at that timestamp; the valid inference is co-occurrence within the stated window. The clock also constrains frequency features: a 100 ms window cannot resolve below about 10 Hz, which matches the lower feature bound [PAGE 5]. The recursive theory-of-mind conclusion is that crisis habits should be replaced by a checklist: confirm IR placement, confirm video-label confidence, confirm sensor bandwidth, confirm classifier variant, and only then interpret the impact count.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto: The measurement layer vetoes any claim that ignores sampling and bandwidth. The system records at 1000 Hz for 100 ms and uses a 10 g trigger [PAGE 2]; accelerometer cutoff is 500 Hz and gyroscope cutoff is 184 Hz [PAGE 4]; feature extraction stops at 200 Hz for linear acceleration and 180 Hz for angular acceleration [PAGE 5]. A claim about high-frequency impact content above these limits is vetoed as under-sampled or filtered, not as a demonstrated head-motion effect. A claim based on low IR is also vetoed: low IR means the device may be off-teeth [PAGE 4], and the paper reports substantial off-teeth contamination [PAGE 11]. A claim that ignores the 10% dummy-head calibration error [PAGE 2] overstates precision.

Biomechanical veto: The source’s neck/torso features come from a two-linkage model with a universal joint, a minimum-distance torso constraint, a 10.8 cm neck length, and a head center-of-gravity 5 cm above the head-neck joint [PAGE 5]. The model assumes “a massive torso that has less motion than the less massive head” [PAGE 5]. Any claim that the model directly measures neck muscle force is vetoed; it estimates kinematics under a rigid-body assumption. The paper also warns that the mouthguard “was susceptible to mandible noise if the lower jaw was not clenched” [PAGE 12]. Therefore a claim that jaw clenching or mandible dynamics are irrelevant is vetoed. A claim that ratios of angular to linear acceleration are always finite is vetoed by the denominator singularity already identified.

Weather veto: No weather operator exists in the source. The recorded channels are 6-DOF kinematics and IR placement [PAGE 2], with video-derived activity labels [PAGE 3–4]. There is no temperature, precipitation, humidity, wind, field-surface, or helmet-temperature variable in the measurement or model description. Therefore any claim that weather changes classifier sensitivity, precision, or impact counts is vetoed as out-of-model. The correct adjudication is not “weather has no effect”; it is “weather effects are unmeasured and unidentified in this study.” If a downstream system adds weather variables, it must retrain and revalidate, because the paper states that retraining is likely required for different contexts and devices [PAGE 12].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Universal-device veto. If a claim says the classifier can be deployed unchanged on any head-impact sensor, veto it against: “The classifier is only evaluated to be used with the specific instrumented mouthguard design used in this study” and “Retraining is likely required for any device that does not have the same mechanical and electrical design” [PAGE 12]. Verifiable prediction: on a different form factor or sensor location, nonimpact kinematics will shift and precision/sensitivity will degrade unless retrained. The paper already notes that “the kinematics of nonimpact recordings will likely differ for different device form factors and sensor locations” [PAGE 12].

2. Threshold-sufficiency veto. If a claim says peak linear acceleration alone is sufficient for field impact detection, veto it against the overlapping distributions and the reported ROC result: a simple threshold classifier “did not perform much better than randomly assigning classes with a probability of impact of 50% (with area under the ROC curve close to 0.5)” [PAGE 9]. Verifiable prediction: a threshold-only rule will produce an ROC AUC near chance on this labeled set, with false positives and false negatives concentrated where impact and nonimpact peak accelerations overlap. The paper reports impact peak linear acceleration from 10.1 g to 65.6 g and nonimpact from 10.0 g to 104.3 g [PAGE 8], so threshold separation is not clean.

3. F-measure-youth-transfer veto. If a claim says the F-measure-optimized classifier is the best youth detector because it had the best collegiate cross-validation, veto it against Table 1: on the youth test the F-measure classifier had sensitivity 93.8%, specificity 68.5%, accuracy 81.3%, and precision 75.0%, while the AUC-optimized classifier had sensitivity 93.8%, specificity 93.8%, accuracy 93.8%, and precision 93.8% [PAGE 10]. Verifiable prediction: the F-measure model’s youth false-positive rate will be substantially higher, consistent with the authors’ statement that “the F-measure classifier likely overfit to the collegiate football training and validation data” [PAGE 11]. A further verifiable prediction is that enlarging the youth dataset will shrink the apparent advantage of the AUC model only if the added youth data cover more positions and more video-confirmed contact types, since the current youth test is only 16 impacts and 16 nonimpacts [PAGE 9].
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
