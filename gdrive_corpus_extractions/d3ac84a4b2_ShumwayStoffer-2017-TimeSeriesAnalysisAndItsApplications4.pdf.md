<THINK_TRACE>
The source forces a disciplined split between two inferential geometries: lagged dependence in time and periodic power in frequency. The book states that “the time domain approach views the investigation of lagged relationships” while “the frequency domain approach views the investigation of cycles” [PAGE 11]. A frontier reading must not treat these as interchangeable plots; they are different projection operators on the same second-order structure. The first derivation chain is therefore: define mean and autocovariance, impose weak stationarity, estimate sample dependence, choose an ARMA or state-space operator, then forecast with uncertainty. The second chain is: map autocovariance to spectral density, estimate periodogram, smooth over frequency bands, and test coherence or signal significance. The gray-zone audit begins where the chains interact.

First, stationarity is not a cosmetic assumption. Weak stationarity requires constant mean and autocovariance depending only on lag [PAGE 29]. If this fails, sample autocorrelation is not estimating a stable operator. The book’s random-walk-with-drift example shows why: a stochastic trend creates nonstationary covariance, so differencing becomes necessary [PAGE 21]. But differencing is not free. Overdifferencing white noise creates an MA(1) dependence structure where none existed [PAGE 137]. Thus the gray zone is not “trend versus noise” but “which operator makes the residual approximately white.”

Second, ARMA identifiability requires root conditions. Causality requires the AR polynomial to have no zeros inside or on the unit circle, so the process can be written as a one-sided linear process [PAGE 97]. Invertibility requires the MA polynomial to satisfy the analogous condition, because otherwise distinct parameter pairs generate the same autocovariance [PAGE 95]. The subtle trap is that an explosive AR root can still yield a stationary but future-dependent representation; that model is statistically awkward because prediction requires unavailable future values [PAGE 91]. Therefore, model selection must simultaneously enforce stationarity, causality, invertibility, and parsimony.

Third, spectral inference has its own singularities. The periodogram is the sample version of the spectral density [PAGE 193], but it is not a consistent estimator because high-lag autocovariances are poorly estimated from few pairs [PAGE 193]. Smoothing over contiguous frequencies creates a bias-variance tradeoff: bandwidth controls spectral resolution and variance. The book’s chi-square approximation for periodogram ordinates and smoothed spectra makes this explicit [PAGE 197]. Aliasing is another hard boundary: discrete sampling cannot represent frequencies above the folding frequency of one-half cycle per point [PAGE 169]. A spectral peak claimed above that boundary is not evidence of a fast cycle; it is evidence of undersampling or aliasing.

Fourth, state-space models generalize the problem. The Kalman filter recursively updates the state estimate when a new observation arrives [PAGE 303]. This is powerful because missing data, structural components, and correlated errors can be handled in one framework. However, the filter has breakdown regimes: unstable transition matrices, nonobservable states, singular covariance matrices, or misspecified observation noise can make the likelihood surface pathological. The steady-state Riccati equation shows that filter stability depends on the transition and covariance structure [PAGE 319].

The deepest gray-zone issue is causal interpretation. Cross-correlation can reveal that SOI leads recruitment, but statistical lead does not prove mechanism. The book’s SOI-recruitment analysis finds a negative cross-correlation at a lag where SOI leads by six months [PAGE 41]. A valid scientific inference must combine that temporal ordering with a physical or biological mechanism. If the proposed mechanism violates conservation, causality, or sampling constraints, the statistical association must be vetoed even if it is numerically strong.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let $x_t$ denote a scalar time series measured in physical units $U$. If $x_t$ is temperature in kelvin, $U=\mathrm{K}$; if $x_t$ is log-transformed thickness, $U$ is dimensionless log-units. The mean function is
\[
\mu_{x_t}=E(x_t),
\]
with units $U$ [PAGE 25]. The autocovariance function is
\[
\gamma_x(s,t)=\operatorname{cov}(x_s,x_t)=E[(x_s-\mu_{x_s})(x_t-\mu_{x_t})],
\]
with units $U^2$ [PAGE 27]. For weak stationarity, $\mu_{x_t}=\mu_x$ and $\gamma_x(h)$ depends only on lag $h$ [PAGE 29]. The stationary autocovariance and autocorrelation are
\[
\gamma_x(h)=\operatorname{cov}(x_{t+h},x_t),\qquad
\rho_x(h)=\frac{\gamma_x(h)}{\gamma_x(0)},
\]
where $\rho_x(h)$ is dimensionless and lies in $[-1,1]$ [PAGE 31].

The sample autocovariance used for estimation is
\[
\widehat{\gamma}(h)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n-h}(x_{t+h}-\bar{x})(x_t-\bar{x}),
\]
with $\widehat{\gamma}(-h)=\widehat{\gamma}(h)$ [PAGE 37]. This estimator divides by $n$, not $n-h$, to preserve nonnegative definiteness [PAGE 37]. For white noise, sample autocorrelations are approximately independent normal with mean zero and variance $1/n$ [PAGE 151]. Breakdown: if the series has a stochastic trend, unit root, or structural break, $\widehat{\gamma}(h)$ is not estimating a stationary lag operator.

White noise is defined by
\[
w_t\sim wn(0,\sigma_w^2),
\]
with mean zero and variance $\sigma_w^2$ in units $U^2$ [PAGE 19]. Gaussian white noise is stronger:
\[
w_t\sim iid\,N(0,\sigma_w^2).
\]

The AR(1) model is
\[
x_t=\phi x_{t-1}+w_t,
\]
with $\phi$ dimensionless [PAGE 89]. Its stationary causal solution is
\[
x_t=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi^j w_{t-j},\qquad |\phi|<1,
\]
and its autocovariance is
\[
\gamma_x(h)=\frac{\sigma_w^2\phi^h}{1-\phi^2},\qquad h\ge 0,
\]
in units $U^2$ [PAGE 89]. Breakdown regime: $|\phi|\ge 1$. At $|\phi|=1$, the process becomes a random walk or explosive boundary case; variance grows with time or fails to stabilize [PAGE 21]. If $|\phi|>1$, a stationary representation exists only as a future-dependent noncausal process [PAGE 91].

The MA(1) model is
\[
x_t=w_t+\theta w_{t-1},
\]
with $\theta$ dimensionless [PAGE 95]. Its lag-one autocorrelation is
\[
\rho_x(1)=\frac{\theta}{1+\theta^2}.
\]
Nonuniqueness arises because $\theta$ and $1/\theta$ can generate the same autocovariance; invertibility selects $|\theta|<1$ [PAGE 95]. Breakdown: $|\theta|>1$ gives a noninvertible representation; $|\theta|=1$ lies on the boundary where the infinite autoregressive representation fails.

The ARMA$(p,q)$ model is
\[
\phi(B)x_t=\theta(B)w_t,
\]
where
\[
\phi(B)=1-\phi_1B-\cdots-\phi_pB^p,\qquad
\theta(B)=1+\theta_1B+\cdots+\theta_qB^q,
\]
and $B$ is the backshift operator [PAGE 95]. Causality holds if and only if
\[
\phi(z)\neq 0\quad\text{for }|z|\le 1,
\]
so that
\[
x_t=\psi(B)w_t=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_jw_{t-j}
\]
is one-sided [PAGE 97]. The $\psi$-weights satisfy
\[
\phi(z)\psi(z)=\theta(z),\qquad |z|\le 1.
\]
Invertibility requires
\[
\theta(z)\neq 0\quad\text{for }|z|\le 1.
\]
Breakdown regimes: common factors between $\phi(z)$ and $\theta(z)$ produce parameter redundancy; roots on or inside the unit circle violate causality or invertibility [PAGE 97].

Differencing uses
\[
\nabla^d=(1-B)^d.
\]
For $d=1$, $\nabla x_t=x_t-x_{t-1}$; for $d=2$, $\nabla^2x_t=x_t-2x_{t-1}+x_{t-2}$ [PAGE 69]. A random walk with drift,
\[
x_t=\delta+x_{t-1}+w_t,
\]
becomes stationary after first differencing:
\[
\nabla x_t=\delta+w_t.
\]
Breakdown: overdifferencing. If $x_t=w_t$ is already white, then $\nabla x_t=w_t-w_{t-1}$ is MA(1), introducing artificial dependence [PAGE 137].

Model selection uses Akaike’s Information Criterion:
\[
\mathrm{AIC}=\log\widehat{\sigma}_k^2+\frac{n+2k}{n},
\]
where $k$ is the number of parameters and $\widehat{\sigma}_k^2$ is the maximum likelihood variance estimate [PAGE 61]. The corrected version is
\[
\mathrm{AICc}=\log\widehat{\sigma}_k^2+\frac{n+k}{n-k-2}.
\]
Units: if natural logarithms are used, information penalties are in nats; if base-2 logarithms are used, they are in bits. Because $\log\widehat{\sigma}_k^2$ depends on the units of $x_t$, only differences among models for the same data are meaningful. Breakdown: AIC can overfit small samples; AICc is preferable when $n/k$ is small [PAGE 61].

The spectral density is the Fourier transform of the autocovariance:
\[
f_x(\omega)=\sum_{h=-\infty}^{\infty}\gamma_x(h)e^{-2\pi i\omega h},
\qquad -\frac12\le\omega\le\frac12,
\]
with $\omega$ in cycles per point and $f_x(\omega)$ in units $U^2$ per cycle per point [PAGE 185]. The inverse relation is
\[
\gamma_x(h)=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}e^{2\pi i\omega h}f_x(\omega)\,d\omega.
\]
The spectral density is nonnegative and even: $f_x(\omega)=f_x(-\omega)$ [PAGE 185]. It is described as “the analogue of the probability density function” [PAGE 185]. Breakdown: if $\sum_h|\gamma_x(h)|=\infty$, the spectral density may fail to exist as an ordinary function; long-memory processes can have divergence at $\omega=0$ [PAGE 257].

The discrete Fourier transform is
\[
d(\omega_j)=n^{-1/2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}x_t e^{-2\pi i\omega_j t},
\]
and the periodogram is
\[
I(\omega_j)=|d(\omega_j)|^2.
\]
The periodogram is “the sample version of $f(\omega_j)$” [PAGE 193]. For white noise with variance $\sigma^2$,
\[
\frac{2I(\omega_j)}{\sigma^2}\xrightarrow{d}\chi_2^2,
\]
approximately independently across distinct Fourier frequencies [PAGE 197]. Breakdown: the raw periodogram is inconsistent; its variance does not vanish as $n\to\infty$. Smoothing over $L$ contiguous frequencies produces an averaged estimator with approximate degrees of freedom proportional to $L$ [PAGE 201]. Aliasing occurs because discrete sampling cannot distinguish frequencies above the folding frequency $1/2$ cycle per point [PAGE 169].

For a linear filter
\[
y_t=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}a_jx_{t-j},
\]
the output spectrum is
\[
f_y(\omega)=|A(\omega)|^2f_x(\omega),
\]
where
\[
A(\omega)=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}a_je^{-2\pi i\omega j}
\]
is the frequency response function [PAGE 187]. Units: $A(\omega)$ is dimensionless if $y_t$ and $x_t$ share units. Breakdown: non-absolutely-summable filter coefficients, leakage from strong spectral peaks, or phase distortion can invalidate interpretation.

For jointly stationary series, squared coherence is
\[
\rho_{y\cdot x}^2(\omega)=
\frac{|f_{yx}(\omega)|^2}{f_{xx}(\omega)f_{yy}(\omega)},
\]
which is dimensionless and lies in $[0,1]$ [PAGE 219]. Breakdown: with insufficient smoothing, coherence estimates can be biased; with only one frequency band, the estimate degenerates.

The linear Gaussian state-space model is
\[
x_t=\Phi x_{t-1}+w_t,
\]
\[
y_t=A_tx_t+v_t,
\]
with state noise covariance $Q$ and observation noise covariance $R$. The Kalman filter updates the filtered state by
\[
x_t^t=x_t^{t-1}+K_t(y_t-A_tx_t^{t-1}),
\]
where the Kalman gain is
\[
K_t=P_t^{t-1}A_t'(A_tP_t^{t-1}A_t'+R)^{-1}.
\]
“The advantage of the Kalman filter is that it specifies how to update” the state once a new observation arrives [PAGE 303]. The steady-state covariance satisfies the Riccati equation
\[
P=\Phi[P-PA'(APA'+R)^{-1}AP]\Phi'+Q,
\]
showing that filter stability depends on $\Phi$, $Q$, $R$, and observability [PAGE 319]. Breakdown: eigenvalues of $\Phi$ outside the unit circle, nonobservable directions, singular $R$, or misspecified missing-data structure can make the likelihood or smoother unstable.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of the source is not a single method but a convergence of three historical streams: time-domain stochastic modeling, frequency-domain harmonic analysis, and state-space recursive estimation. The time-domain stream is associated with autoregressive modeling, moving averages, and the Box-Jenkins ARIMA program. The book explicitly situates ARIMA within the “landmark work by Box and Jenkins (1970)” [PAGE 87]. The frequency-domain stream is older in its physical motivation: Schuster’s periodogram is cited for studying periodicities in sunspot data [PAGE 181], and the star magnitude example uses Whittaker and Robinson’s classic data to expose amplitude modulation and spectral peaks [PAGE 183]. The state-space stream enters through Kalman filtering, originally motivated by tracking problems, and then becomes a general representation for dynamic linear models [PAGE 297].

A multi-week analytical trap is to treat the book’s examples as isolated case studies rather than as repeated failures of naive inference. The global temperature series tempts one to fit a low-order polynomial trend, but the random-walk-with-drift model shows that stochastic trend can mimic deterministic trend [PAGE 21]. The chicken price series tempts one to difference without checking whether detrending is enough; the book compares detrended and differenced sample autocorrelation functions to expose the difference [PAGE 59]. The glacial varve series tempts one to log-transform and difference, but later long-memory and unit-root analyses show that fractional integration may be more appropriate than integer differencing [PAGE 243]. The GNP series tempts one to choose between AR and MA representations, but residual diagnostics and information criteria show that several parsimonious models can fit similarly while implying different forecasts [PAGE 139].

Another lineage trap is cross-correlation without prewhitening. The SOI and recruitment series have strong seasonal cycles; if both series are not transformed to remove autocorrelation, cross-correlation can be dominated by common persistence rather than lead-lag relation. The book introduces prewhitening and lagged scatterplots to avoid this [PAGE 43]. The recruitment example is historically important because it connects oceanographic forcing to biological response: SOI measures pressure changes related to sea surface temperature, and recruitment is estimated new fish [PAGE 15]. The statistical lead of SOI over recruitment becomes scientifically credible only when placed inside an El Niño mechanism with annual and interannual cycles.

A third trap is spectral overinterpretation. The star magnitude spectrum shows two prominent cycles, but the book interprets them as possible amplitude modulation rather than as two independent physical clocks [PAGE 183]. The SOI spectrum shows a narrow annual peak and a broader low-frequency El Niño band, which warns that the El Niño cycle is irregular rather than a pure sinusoid [PAGE 199]. The sunspot and varve examples further show that a spectral peak can be stable enough for description but unstable enough for precise prediction. The retro-causal lesson is that time-series genealogy is a sequence of increasingly refined operators: raw plot, mean and covariance, stationarity, differencing, ARMA, spectral density, smoothing, state-space, and finally mechanistic validation.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory of mind for time-series reasoning must model how an analyst, an institution, or an automated system updates beliefs when a crisis regime appears. The first failure mode is crisis habit regression: after a shock, the agent assumes the recent high-variance regime will persist indefinitely. In time-domain terms, this is equivalent to fitting a high-order AR or integrated model to a short crisis window and extrapolating it as if its covariance structure were stable. The second failure mode is the counter-trap: after recognizing crisis, the agent overcorrects by differencing too aggressively, filtering out genuine persistence, and declaring stationarity too early. The third corrective is the clock: the physical or institutional calendar that limits which cycles are plausible, such as annual seasons, El Niño recurrence bands, or observation schedules.

The book’s threshold models formalize regime dependence without assuming a single linear operator. A self-exciting threshold model lets the current regime depend on past values of the same series, with different ARMA dynamics above and below a threshold [PAGE 273]. The influenza mortality example shows that nonlinear regime shifts can be more plausible than a single linear model. Hidden Markov models add a latent state process: the observation distribution changes according to an unobserved Markov chain [PAGE 345]. Switching dynamic linear models go further by allowing the observation matrix itself to switch between structural configurations, as in the influenza epidemic example where the observation matrix changes depending on whether an epidemic is present [PAGE 355].

The recursive mental model is therefore: do not infer a single persistent law from crisis data. Instead, estimate a family of possible regimes, assign filtered probabilities to those regimes, and propagate uncertainty into forecasts. The Kalman filter is the computational engine for this when the model is linear Gaussian; the hidden Markov and switching extensions are needed when the regime is discrete. The theory-of-mind trap is to treat the filtered probability of a regime as a deterministic label. The book’s smoothing probabilities for earthquakes and influenza show that regime probabilities are themselves time-varying and uncertain [PAGE 339].

The clock also disciplines spectral claims. If a proposed cycle has no physical or observational clock, it may be an artifact of sampling or leakage. The annual cycle in SOI has a clear clock: twelve months. The El Niño band has a slower, irregular clock: roughly three to seven years [PAGE 15]. A model that predicts a sharp El Niño peak at a fixed period without allowing bandwidth or regime variation is vulnerable to the counter-trap of false precision. The robust approach is to combine time-domain regime detection, frequency-domain smoothing, and mechanistic clock constraints.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer veto adjudication asks whether a statistical claim survives physical, biomechanical, and weather-layer constraints. The first veto proof uses temporal ordering. The SOI-recruitment analysis finds that “SOI measured at time $t-6$ months is associated with the Recruitment series at time $t$” and that “SOI leads the Recruitment series by six months” [PAGE 41]. Formally, the cross-covariance is
\[
\gamma_{xy}(h)=\operatorname{cov}(x_{t+h},y_t),
\]
and a negative peak at $h=-6$ indicates that $x_{t-6}$ is associated with $y_t$. If a proposed mechanism claims that recruitment drives SOI without a physical feedback pathway, the statistical direction must be vetoed because it reverses the observed temporal ordering. If the claim is that fish population changes alter sea-level pressure or sea surface temperature in the central Pacific within six months, the physical layer imposes an additional burden of mechanism. Without such a mechanism, the cross-layer veto holds.

The second veto proof uses biomechanical sampling. The speech recording is sampled at 10,000 points per second, so the folding frequency is 5,000 cycles per second, or $0.5$ cycles per point [PAGE 13], [PAGE 169]. The observed pitch period is between 0.0106 and 0.0109 seconds [PAGE 39]. Any claim that the recorded speech contains a biomechanical pitch component above the Nyquist limit is vetoed by the sampling theorem. If an automated classifier claims to infer a glottal oscillation faster than 5,000 Hz from this signal, the claim is not merely uncertain; it is mathematically inadmissible unless aliasing is explicitly modeled.

The third veto proof uses weather and physiological nonlinearity. The cardiovascular mortality example shows that mortality is related to temperature and particulates, with a nonlinear temperature effect. The lowess smooth indicates that mortality is higher at colder temperatures than at hotter temperatures and that the minimum mortality rate occurs near $83^\circ\mathrm{F}$ [PAGE 81]. A model that imposes a monotone linear temperature effect is vetoed by the observed asymmetry. If a weather-layer predictor claims that warming always reduces mortality, the physiological layer rejects it because cold-related mortality can dominate in the observed range.

The fourth veto proof uses seismic discrimination. Earthquake and explosion waveforms differ in P and S phase structure, and “rough amplitude ratios of the first phase P to the second phase S” tend to be smaller for earthquakes than for explosions [PAGE 17]. Later spectral analysis shows that explosion processes can have stronger high-frequency components relative to low-frequency contributions [PAGE 385]. A classifier that ignores P/S amplitude ratios and uses only broadband energy may be statistically convenient but physically underconstrained. The cross-layer veto is not that the classifier is wrong in every case; it is that the classifier fails to use a known physical discriminator. In adjudication, a claim is vetoed if it contradicts a robust constraint from a lower physical layer, unless the model explicitly represents that constraint and its uncertainty.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are three verifiable RLVR veto scenarios. RLVR here means reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards: a model claim is rewarded only if it survives held-out data, physical constraints, or formal diagnostic tests.

Scenario 1: AR(2) recruitment forecast veto.  
Fit the recruitment series with a causal AR(2) model, as suggested by the sample ACF and PACF behavior [PAGE 111]. The model is
\[
R_t=\phi_1R_{t-1}+\phi_2R_{t-2}+w_t.
\]
A verifiable prediction is that 24-month forecasts should remain within the model’s predictive bounds and that residual autocorrelation should not be systematically structured. The Ljung-Box-Pierce statistic is
\[
Q=n(n+2)\sum_{h=1}^{H}\frac{\widehat{\rho}_e^2(h)}{n-h},
\]
which is approximately chi-square with $H-p-q$ degrees of freedom under model adequacy [PAGE 151]. Veto rule: if held-out recruitment residuals produce a $Q$ value above the chosen chi-square critical value, or if standardized forecast errors exceed the nominal predictive bounds at a rate incompatible with the stated probability level, the AR(2) claim is vetoed. Prediction: if the AR(2) model is correctly specified, the residual ACF should not show a persistent seasonal peak near lag 12 after fitting; if such a peak appears, the model is missing a seasonal operator.

Scenario 2: El Niño spectral-peak veto.  
The SOI and recruitment spectra suggest a low-frequency El Niño band centered near a four-year cycle, corresponding to $\omega=1/48$ cycles per month when frequency is expressed in multiples of $\Delta=1/12$ [PAGE 199]. A smoothed spectral estimator over $L$ frequencies has approximate confidence bounds based on chi-square quantiles [PAGE 201]. A verifiable claim is that the lower confidence bound at the El Niño band exceeds a baseline spectral level estimated from neighboring frequencies. Veto rule: if a held-out or split-sample spectral estimate places the El Niño peak inside the baseline band, the claim of a statistically significant El Niño spectral component is vetoed. Prediction: if the El Niño component is real, the smoothed spectrum should show elevated power near the low-frequency band in both sample halves, while the annual harmonic should remain narrower and stronger. If the low-frequency band collapses after smoothing, the original claim was likely periodogram noise.

Scenario 3: State-space missing-data veto.  
The blood parameter example uses a state-space model to estimate missing biomedical markers after bone marrow transplant [PAGE 299]. The model is
\[
x_t=\Phi x_{t-1}+w_t,
\]
\[
y_t=A_tx_t+v_t.
\]
A verifiable prediction is that held-out observed values, artificially masked as missing, should be recovered within the smoother’s uncertainty bounds. Veto rule: if the Kalman smoother or EM estimate produces standardized missing-data errors
\[
z_t=\frac{y_t-\widehat{y}_t}{\sqrt{P_t^n}}
\]
whose empirical coverage is incompatible with the nominal probability interval, the missing-data model is vetoed. Prediction: if the state equation and covariance matrices are correctly specified, the smoothed estimates should reduce isolated missing gaps without creating implausible oscillations. If the smoother imputes abrupt jumps inconsistent with the estimated state covariance, the model is overconfident or misspecified.

These veto scenarios are operational: they specify the statistic, the threshold logic, and the observable consequence. They prevent the system from rewarding a model merely because it fits the observed sample. The model must survive counterfactual perturbation: held-out time blocks, split-sample spectra, or masked observations.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
