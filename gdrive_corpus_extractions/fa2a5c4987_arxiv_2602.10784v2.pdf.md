Let \(m=1,\ldots,M\) index plays, with \(M=3963\) in the reported sample and with 2980 zone and 983 man labels [PAGE 4]. Let \(t=1,\ldots,T_m\) index tracking frames. The data are sampled at \(10\,\mathrm{Hz}\), so the sampling interval is \(\Delta t=0.1\,\mathrm{s}\) [PAGE 4]. Let \(y_t\) be a defender’s vertical coordinate in a field-length unit; the paper does not explicitly print the coordinate unit, so write it as \(u_y\). The paper projects player \((x,y)\)-coordinates onto a single \(y\)-axis [PAGE 7]. The latent state is \(S_t\in\{1,\ldots,5\}\), one state per offensive skill player [PAGE 6–7]. The state-dependent density is Gaussian:
\[
f(y_t\mid S_t=j)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(y_t-\mu_{t,j})^2}{2\sigma^2}\right],\qquad j=1,\ldots,5,
\]
with
\[
\mu_{t,j}=y_{t-l}^{(\mathrm{off},j)}.
\]
The paper states the basic mean as \(\mu_{t,j}=y_t(\mathrm{off}_j)\) [PAGE 7] and then the lagged version as \(\mu_{t,j}=y_{t-l}(\mathrm{off}_j)\) [PAGE 8]. The reaction lag is \(l=4\) frames, which at \(10\,\mathrm{Hz}\) is \(0.4\,\mathrm{s}\); the paper says “l = 5 corresponds to an average reaction time of half a second” [PAGE 8] and reports that “l = 4 provides the best fit according to AIC values” [PAGE 17]. Units: \(y_t\) and \(\mu_{t,j}\) are in \(u_y\); \(\sigma\) is in \(u_y\); probabilities are dimensionless. Assumptions: first-order Markov state process; conditional independence of \(y_t\) given \(S_t\); Gaussian state-dependent densities; scalar \(y\)-projection; known lag; five states. Breakdown regimes: if guarding depends on the \(x\)-dimension, the scalar projection can merge distinct assignments; if two offensive players occupy the same \(y\)-coordinate, the Gaussian components become non-identifiable or nearly singular; if \(\sigma\to 0\), the likelihood becomes degenerate and the forward recursion can underflow; if \(\sigma\) is too large, the state likelihood is nearly flat and decoding is driven almost entirely by the transition matrix; if the reaction lag is heterogeneous across defenders or plays, a single \(l\) misspecifies the mean function; and if tracking noise is non-Gaussian or temporally autocorrelated beyond the Markov state, the likelihood is misspecified.

The transition model is non-homogeneous. For defender role \(r\), defensive team \(d\), play \(p\), and states \(i\neq j\),
\[
\eta_{ij}^{(t,r,d,p)}=\beta_0+\beta_1\left|y_{t-l}^{(\mathrm{off},i)}-y_{t-l}^{(\mathrm{off},j)}\right|+u_r+v_d+w_p,
\]
with \(\eta_{ii}\equiv 0\) for identifiability [PAGE 8–9]. The transition probabilities use the inverse multinomial logistic link:
\[
\gamma_{ij}^{(t,r,d,p)}=\frac{\exp\!\left(\eta_{ij}^{(t,r,d,p)}\right)}{\sum_{k=1}^{5}\exp\!\left(\eta_{ik}^{(t,r,d,p)}\right)},\qquad i,j=1,\ldots,5.
\]
The random effects are modeled as \(u_r\sim N(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{role}}^2)\), \(v_d\sim N(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{defense}}^2)\), and \(w_p\sim N(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{play}}^2)\) [PAGE 9]. Units: \(\beta_1\) has inverse \(u_y\) units; \(u_r\), \(v_d\), and \(w_p\) are in logit units; \(\gamma_{ij}\) is dimensionless. Breakdown regimes: complete or quasi-complete separation in the multinomial logit can drive coefficients to boundary values; \(\sigma_{\mathrm{role}}\to 0\) or \(\sigma_{\mathrm{defense}}\to 0\) collapses partial pooling; \(\sigma_{\mathrm{play}}\to\infty\) can absorb nearly all play-level variation and make fixed effects weakly identified; role, team, and play effects can be non-identifiable when roles are nested in teams or when teams have few plays; and the transition model omits \(x\)-distance, weather, and play-clock, so any such effect is pushed into random effects or error.

The marginal likelihood integrates random effects:
\[
L(\theta)=f_{\theta}(y)=\iiint f_{\beta,\sigma}(y\mid u,v,w)\,f_{\sigma_u}(u)\,f_{\sigma_v}(v)\,f_{\sigma_w}(w)\,du\,dv\,dw,
\]
with \(\theta=(\beta_0,\beta_1,\sigma,\sigma_u,\sigma_v,\sigma_w)\) [PAGE 10]. The paper evaluates this by Laplace approximation around the mode of the integrand [PAGE 10]. For a single defender, the conditional likelihood is the forward-product form
\[
\delta(1)P(y_1)\Gamma(2)P(y_2)\Gamma(3)\cdots \Gamma(T_m)P(y_{T_m}),
\]
where \(P(y_t)=\mathrm{diag}\{f(y_t\mid S_t=1),\ldots,f(y_t\mid S_t=5)\}\) [PAGE 11]. Units: the likelihood is a density in \(u_y^{-T_m}\) for a continuous observation sequence; log-likelihood is in nats if the natural logarithm is used. Breakdown regimes: Laplace approximation error grows when the integrand is far from Gaussian or when random-effect dimension is large relative to play length; short \(T_m\) makes the Gaussian and Markov approximations fragile; boundary estimates of variance components produce singular Hessians; and missing or occluded tracking frames break the equal-spacing forward recursion.

Local decoding computes
\[
\Pr(S_t=j\mid y_1,\ldots,y_{T_m})
\]
and then uses
\[
\hat S_t=\arg\max_{k\in\{1,\ldots,5\}}\Pr(S_t=k\mid y_1,\ldots,y_{T_m})
\]
[PAGE 12–13]. The paper’s summary entropy is
\[
H(m)=-\frac{1}{5}\sum_{j_m=1}^{5}\sum_{k=1}^{5}
\left(\frac{1}{T_m}\sum_{t=1}^{T_m}\mathbf{1}\{\hat S_{t,j_m}=k\}\right)
\log\!\left(\frac{1}{T_m}\sum_{t=1}^{T_m}\mathbf{1}\{\hat S_{t,j_m}=k\}\right),
\]
where \(\hat S_{t,j_m}\) is the most likely guarded offensive player for defender \(j_m\) in play \(m\) [PAGE 13]. Units: if the logarithm is natural, \(H(m)\) is in nats; if base 2, in bits; the paper does not print the base. Assumptions and breakdown: the formula uses hard states, not posterior probabilities; it treats the empirical frequency over time as the distribution; it assumes \(T_m\) is long enough for stable frequencies; and it needs the convention \(0\log 0=0\). With short motion phases, \(H(m)\) is biased downward and can be zero for many defenders. The other HMM features are total switches, number of defenders who switch at least once, and the per-play random effect [PAGE 13, PAGE 35].

The supervised models are an elastic-net logistic regression and XGBoost [PAGE 14]. For binary \(Y_i\in\{0,1\}\), the logistic model uses a linear predictor in the features and the elastic-net penalty; XGBoost uses additive boosted trees. The paper evaluates accuracy, AUC, and logloss, and says logloss is a proper scoring rule [PAGE 14–15]. Cross-fitting uses five folds, with inner five-fold tuning, repeated 50 times [PAGE 20–21]. The reported pattern is that XGBoost outperforms elastic net and that HMM features improve performance [PAGE 20–21]. The GCM operator is
\[
\mathrm{GCM}:=\mathbb E[\mathrm{Cov}(Y,X\mid Z)]
=\mathbb E[(Y-\mathbb E[Y\mid Z])(X-\mathbb E[X\mid Z])],
\]
and it tests \(Y\perp\!\!\!\perp X\mid Z\) by testing whether this expectation is zero [PAGE 15]. The partially linear logistic model is
\[
\operatorname{logit}\pi(X,Z)=X\beta+g(Z),
\]
with \(\pi(X,Z)=\Pr(Y=1\mid X,Z)\) [PAGE 16]. Units: \(Y\) is binary and dimensionless; \(X\) carries the feature’s units; \(\beta\) is in logit units per \(X\)-unit; the GCM statistic has units of \(Y\) times \(X\) before normalization. Breakdown regimes: the test is only as valid as the nuisance regressions \(h(Z)=\mathbb E[Y\mid Z]\) and \(f(Z)=\mathbb E[X\mid Z]\); overfitting XGBoost can invalidate the test; plays are nested in teams and games, so an iid permutation null may be too optimistic; and the test is conditional-independence evidence, not causal evidence. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is explicit. The HMM machinery descends from the standard hidden-Markov time-series tradition represented by Zucchini, MacDonald, and Langrock [PAGE 32], and from sports latent-state applications such as Dutta, Yurko, and Ventura’s mixture-model work on coverage identification [PAGE 2, PAGE 28]. The spatial defensive-assignment idea is also genealogically linked to Franks et al.’s basketball defensive-skill work, which the paper cites for the entropy-style summary [PAGE 7, PAGE 28]. The reaction-lag idea is linked to Wu and Swartz’s soccer off-ball evaluation [PAGE 8, PAGE 31]. The random-effects transition model is linked to Maruotti and Rydén’s semiparametric longitudinal HMM work [PAGE 9, PAGE 29]. The inference layer is linked to Shah and Peters’ GCM test [PAGE 15, PAGE 30] and to Bajons and Kook’s partially linear logistic interpretation [PAGE 16, PAGE 28]. The supervised layer is linked to Zou and Hastie’s elastic net [PAGE 14, PAGE 31] and Chen and Guestrin’s XGBoost [PAGE 14, PAGE 28]. The estimation layer is linked to Kristensen et al.’s TMB/Laplace approximation and the RTMB implementation [PAGE 10–11, PAGE 29]. The paper’s own contribution is the retrocausal bridge: it takes a modern tracking-data object and re-reads pre-snap motion through a latent-state lens, so that “motion” is no longer only a raw displacement but a decoded sequence of guarding assignments.

The multi-week traps are where retrocausal reasoning becomes important. First, the season inconsistency between [PAGE 2] and [PAGE 4] is a provenance trap: if the sample is actually 2023 but a reader assumes 2024, then team-level random effects and the team analysis in [PAGE 23–25] are being interpreted against the wrong league context. Second, the model’s team random effects are estimated from a small number of weeks. A team that changes defensive coordinator, personnel, or scheme between weeks will have a random effect that is a partial-pooled average over nonstationary behavior. The paper’s leave-one-team-out analysis [PAGE 23–24] reduces leakage for team-level evaluation, but it does not solve within-season drift. Third, the HMM is fitted with a single reaction lag \(l=4\) chosen by AIC [PAGE 17, PAGE 33]. That is a global temporal prior; if a team uses late motion, early motion, or motion after the defense has already rotated, the effective lag changes. Fourth, the paper’s feature table [PAGE 35] lists no play-clock, time-to-snap, weather, field-surface, or tracking-quality variable. A multi-week deployment that ignores those variables can attribute a week-specific environmental effect to “HMM motion decoding.” Fifth, the class imbalance and the single-season sample mean that a model with high accuracy can still be poorly calibrated for man coverage; the authors’ use of logloss is a good safeguard [PAGE 14–15], but the reported GCM p-values do not by themselves establish out-of-week stability. Sixth, the running example [PAGE 17–19] is a vivid but local demonstration; it is genealogically useful because it shows why temporal persistence differs from nearest-neighbor clustering, but it is not a population estimate. Seventh, the paper’s own limitations section says the dataset “was quite small, constraining both model complexity and generalizability” and that man-versus-zone is “a coarse abstraction” [PAGE 26]. Those are not throwaway caveats; they are the main reasons a multi-week reader should treat the HMM features as a structured prior, not as a universal coverage detector.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The strategic layer is a recursive belief game. The offense moves a receiver, tight end, or running back because it believes the defense will reveal its assignment; the defense knows the offense is watching and therefore disguises; the offense anticipates disguise and may use motion as a counter-trap. The paper states the first-order version plainly: “If a cornerback follows a motioning receiver across the entire field, this may indicate a man-to-man coverage scheme; if defenders pass on offensive players and shift in the direction of the motion, zone coverage becomes more likely” [PAGE 2]. The HMM is an observer’s third-person belief model: it infers \(\Pr(S_t=j\mid y_{1:T_m})\), the probability that a defender is guarding offensive player \(j\) given the observed trajectories [PAGE 12]. That is not the defender’s belief, nor the coordinator’s belief; it is the analyst’s belief about the defender’s assignment.

“Crisis habit regression” appears in the random-effect table. The paper says smaller values correspond to a higher likelihood of remaining in the current state [PAGE 17]. The table reports Cornerback \(-0.178\), Free Safety \(0.175\), Inside Linebacker \(0.007\), Middle Linebacker \(0.006\), Outside Linebacker \(0.006\), and Strong Safety \(-0.005\) [PAGE 17]. The substantive reading is that cornerbacks are the most persistent: they follow. Safeties and linebackers are more likely to pass off assignments. Under late-clock or high-stress conditions, a plausible behavioral hypothesis is that defenders regress to their role habit: cornerbacks trail, safeties rotate, linebackers hand off. The paper does not directly test clock pressure; it includes “remaining seconds in the current half” as a contextual feature [PAGE 5, PAGE 35], but it does not include time-to-snap or play-clock. So the crisis-habit interpretation is a hypothesis suggested by the random effects, not a demonstrated clock effect.

The counter-trap is the mirror image. If an offense motions to bait a pass-off, a defender may switch even in man coverage; if the HMM sees a state switch, the summary feature “switches” rises, and the GCM direction in the paper is that higher switch/entropy features are associated with lower probability of man coverage [PAGE 22–23]. That is exactly where a counter-trap can fool the model: a man defense that executes a switch release can look zone-like, while a zone defense that pattern-matches a motion can look man-like. The paper’s negative coefficient direction is intuitive, but it is not a proof against counter-traps. The model’s “habit” prior is the transition matrix: cornerbacks have high persistence, so the HMM resists switching even when a defender crosses another offensive player’s \(y\)-coordinate. The paper’s Marco Wilson example says that “although Wilson is momentarily as close to McKinnon as to Hardman, his estimated tendency to stay with Hardman remains high” [PAGE 17]. That is the HMM’s temporal prior acting as a counter-counter-trap: it does not overreact to a momentary spatial crossing.

The clock is both literal and inferential. Literally, the tracking clock is \(10\,\mathrm{Hz}\), and the reaction lag is \(l=4\), i.e. \(0.4\,\mathrm{s}\) [PAGE 4, PAGE 8, PAGE 17]. If motion happens too close to the snap, a defender cannot physically produce a \(0.4\,\mathrm{s}\)-lagged response before the ball is snapped; the HMM then sees mostly pre-motion alignment, and the motion features carry little information. If motion happens earlier, the defender has time to react, and the decoded state sequence becomes more informative. Infernentially, the “clock” is the recursive timing of belief: the offense chooses when to motion based on its model of the defense’s model of the offense. The paper’s features include “remaining seconds in the current half” [PAGE 35], which is a game clock, not a snap clock. A rigorous recursive-theory test would need time-to-snap, motion onset time, and perhaps a defender-specific reaction-time distribution. Without those, the paper’s clock story is suggestive but incomplete.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is a condition under which a statistical result must be rejected or quarantined because a lower layer makes the measurement or the assumed data-generating process invalid. The layers here are: tracking layer (10 Hz coordinates) [PAGE 4]; biomechanical layer (reaction, acceleration, deceleration, change of direction); environmental layer (weather, field surface, visibility); tactical-label layer (Pro Football Focus man/zone label) [PAGE 5]; and statistical layer (HMM, supervised models, GCM) [PAGE 10–16]. The paper’s feature table [PAGE 35] lists no weather, field-surface, tracking-quality, injury, or play-clock covariate. Therefore the statistical model cannot adjudicate those layers by itself. The veto proof is conditional: if a lower-layer variable \(W_p\) affects both the tracking trajectory \(y_p\) and the coverage label \(Y_p\), and \(W_p\) is omitted from \(Z\) in the GCM test, then the conditional-independence null \(Y\perp\!\!\!\perp X\mid Z\) can fail because of confounding, not because the HMM feature carries tactical information. The paper’s GCM test conditions on pre-motion and naive post-motion features and other HMM features [PAGE 15–16, PAGE 21], but not on weather, sensor quality, or play-clock. So a significant GCM result is not a cross-layer proof.

Physical and biomechanical vetoes are stronger than statistical ones. The HMM assumes a defender’s \(y_t\) is generated by a lagged offensive-player mean with Gaussian noise and a first-order Markov state [PAGE 6–8]. If a tracking frame is occluded, swapped, or smoothed across a collision, the observed \(y_t\) can jump in a way that no human can produce. Then the decoded state switch is a sensor artifact. A formal veto predicate can be written as \(V_{\mathrm{phys}}(p)=1\) if any tracking segment violates a pre-registered human kinematic envelope, if tracking identity is ambiguous after a pile-up, or if the frame gap exceeds the motion window. The paper gives no such predicate. A second veto is biomechanical: the single lag \(l=4\) is an average reaction proxy [PAGE 8, PAGE 17]. If a defender’s individual reaction time is longer than the motion window, the HMM will attribute the defender’s non-response to a state transition or to a different assignment. A third veto is environmental: rain, snow, wind, or poor field traction changes acceleration and cut behavior, and those changes are correlated with home team, stadium, and week. Because the model’s naive features include maximum \(x\)- and \(y\)-distance and total distance traveled [PAGE 5], weather can shift the feature distribution without changing coverage. A fourth veto is label-layer: the man/zone label is a coarse PFF annotation [PAGE 5], and the paper admits that real defenses include Cover-1, Cover-2, Cover-3, and hybrid schemes [PAGE 26]. If the label collapses distinct schemes, then a “misclassification” may be a label-granularity failure, not a model failure. A fifth veto is statistical: the GCM test’s validity depends on nuisance estimation [PAGE 15], and plays are nested in teams and games; an iid permutation null can overstate significance. The adjudication rule should therefore be: a play or team-week is vetoed from causal or tactical interpretation if \(V_{\mathrm{phys}}\), \(V_{\mathrm{bio}}\), \(V_{\mathrm{env}}\), \(V_{\mathrm{label}}\), or \(V_{\mathrm{stat}}\) is active; the HMM feature may still be used predictively, but it cannot be interpreted as a clean motion-to-coverage signal. The paper’s own limitations section supports this caution: it says the dataset was small and the coverage classification is coarse [PAGE 26].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

1. Temporal-shuffle placebo. Verifiable reward: refit the HMM on within-play temporally permuted defender trajectories, or on time-reversed trajectories, while keeping the same coverage labels and the same pre-motion and naive post-motion features. Prediction: if the reported HMM gain is real, the omnibus GCM p-value should degrade from the reported approximately \(0.0002\) [PAGE 21] to non-significance, and the logloss improvement over the naive model should collapse toward zero. Veto condition: if the shuffled-feature model still produces a significant omnibus result at the same level, then the HMM features are carrying play, team, or context information that is not motion dynamics; the “latent guarding” interpretation is vetoed. This is verifiable because the shuffle destroys temporal order but preserves marginal spatial distributions.

2. Lag and clock calibration. Verifiable reward: scan \(l=0,\ldots,10\) frames and, separately, annotate motion onset and time-to-snap. Prediction: the AIC minimum should remain near \(l=4\) [PAGE 17, PAGE 33], and the HMM feature gain should peak near the same lag; if time-to-snap is added to \(Z\), the per-play random-effect association should not vanish. Veto condition: if the HMM gain is monotonically increasing with \(l\), or if it disappears once time-to-snap is controlled, then the feature is a clock or motion-duration proxy rather than a reaction-lag guarding signal. This is verifiable from the tracking clock at \(10\,\mathrm{Hz}\) [PAGE 4] and from play-clock annotations.

3. Team-leaveout and posteriorentropy replacement. Verifiable reward: repeat the per-play random-effect GCM test under leave-one-team-out training and under a model that adds team identity and week as conditioning variables; also recompute the entropy feature from full posterior probabilities \(\Pr(S_t=j\mid y_{1:T_m})\) rather than from hard \(\hat S_t\). Prediction: the per-play random-effect association should remain negative and at least marginally significant, near the reported \(p\approx 0.005\) [PAGE 21], and the posterior-entropy version should preserve the negative direction reported for the hard-argmax entropy [PAGE 22–23]. Veto condition: if the per-play effect collapses when team or week is conditioned, then it is a team-prior artifact; if the posterior-entropy version flips sign or loses association, then the paper’s “average entropy” result is an artifact of hard decoding. This is verifiable because both replacements are deterministic given the fitted posteriors and the team/week metadata.
