Third, the seasonality trap: weather-driven non-stationarity violates condition \(c\) on a weekly or monthly basis, rendering previously learned configural discriminations obsolete and forcing reversion to the crude first-approximation calculus [PAGE 375].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression occurs when the organism faces a high-uncertainty or high-cost situation: computing configural interactions \(\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{s})\) is metabolically and neurally expensive relative to the linear summation \(H_{\text{prim}}\) [PAGE 375]. Under crisis, the recursive theory of mind predicts that the organism regresses to the primitive first-approximation calculus, reacting to marginal cue frequencies rather than conjunctions, because the threshold \(sLr\) gates out energetically costly complex pattern evaluation when expected need reduction is too slight [PAGE 375].

The counter-trap is the opposite rigidity: extensive training under stable condition \(c\) produces over-reliance on configural precision. When the environment shifts and condition \(c\) fails, the organism persists in responding to the learned configuration even though it is no longer followed by reinforcement [PAGE 375]. This is a trap because the very mechanism that allowed transcendence of the crude calculus under Woodbury-like conditions becomes a source of systematic error under non-stationarity.

The clock operates on two timescales. Hull asserts that each new reaction increases discrimination training and makes the probability progressively more favorable across the life span [PAGE 375]. This is a slow, lifelong upward drift in adaptive accuracy. The environmental clock, however, can tick faster through weather shifts, cue remapping, or contingency reversals. If the environmental drift rate exceeds the learning rate, the organism’s internal probability estimates never converge, and the residual “gamble” persists indefinitely [PAGE 375]. The recursive tension is therefore between the slow clock of habit-strength accumulation and the fast clock of configural obsolescence; whichever clock dominates determines whether behavior is dominated by precise discrimination or by primitive probabilistic summation [PAGE 375].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: Reaction incurs a metabolic cost, and Hull’s threshold \(sLr\) functions as a physical energy veto: do not act unless estimated need-reduction probability justifies the expenditure [PAGE 375]. If the physical energy budget is constrained, any model that omits this veto is invalidated because the organism would react to sub-profitable stimulus compounds and incur net energy loss.

Biomechanical layer: Search and exploration require active receptor adjustment to sample environmental energies more adequately [PAGE 375]. This places a biomechanical veto on the model: receptor positioning is bounded by joint range, muscle fatigue, sensory adaptation, and actuator bandwidth. If the organism is fatigued or immobilized, it cannot increase afferent exposure even when \(P(NR)\) is high, so the predicted improvement in discrimination fails to materialize.

Weather layer: Environmental instability—wind, precipitation, turbulence, thermal gradients—alters the arrival and reliability of critical stimulus cues [PAGE 375]. Weather acts as a veto on condition \(c\): when cue covariance is non-stationary, configural discrimination \(\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{s})\) cannot be maintained, and the system is forced back to the primitive summation calculus [PAGE 375]. Cross-modal masking (e.g., wind masking olfactory cues) further degrades afferent fidelity, lowering effective \(s_i\) and collapsing \(H_{\text{prim}}\).

Veto adjudication rule: A proposed behavioral model is rejected if any layer signals a veto. Specifically, pure configural-control models are vetoed whenever weather violates condition \(c\), whenever biomechanical fatigue blocks receptor adjustment, or whenever physical energy constraints make \(sLr\) binding. Only a hybrid model—linear summation as default, configural precision only when all three layers pass—survives cross-layer adjudication [PAGE 375].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Scenario 1 (afferent clamp): Counterfactual—if afferent impulse \(s_i\) is physically clamped at baseline (e.g., naris occlusion or retinal stabilization) while reinforcement pairings \(N_i\) continue to increase, then habit strength \(h_i = F(N_i)\) cannot rise because Hull grounds habit strength in afferent impulses of stimulus components [PAGE 375]. Verifiable prediction: response probability \(P(R = 1 \mid \mathbf{s})\) remains at the response floor despite accumulating reinforcement history; the primitive summation stays null and no configural learning emerges.

Scenario 2 (condition-c violation): Counterfactual—if the stimulus compound configuration is randomly remapped on every trial such that critical variable factors are not stabilized, condition \(c\) is violated [PAGE 375]. Verifiable prediction: Woodbury-style precision discrimination fails; behavior reverts to linear summation over marginal cue frequencies. Response probability will correlate with individual cue prevalence rather than with specific conjunctions, and reaction patterns will match \(H_{\text{prim}}(\mathbf{s})\) rather than \(\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{s})\).

Scenario 3 (threshold suppression): Counterfactual—if the reaction threshold \(\theta = sLr\) is pharmacologically lowered toward zero, the energy-conserving veto is removed [PAGE 375]. Verifiable prediction: the organism reacts to sub-threshold stimulus compounds; total reaction count increases; \(P(NR \mid R)\) declines because many reactions occur when need-reduction probability is too slight; net need reduction per unit time and per unit energy expended falls despite elevated activity. This falsifies any model that omits the \(sLr\) veto.
