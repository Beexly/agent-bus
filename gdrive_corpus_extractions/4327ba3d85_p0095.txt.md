I(S_2 ; R_2 \mid \mathcal{H}_{S_1}) = H(R_2 \mid \mathcal{H}_{S_1}) - H(R_2 \mid S_2, \mathcal{H}_{S_1}) \quad [\text{bits}]
where \mathcal{H}_{S_1} denotes the history of metronome–salivation pairings. If I = 0 bits, S₂ carries no information about R₂ beyond the first-order history.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage: The passage sits at an inflection point between Pavlovian first-order conditioned reflexes and Hull's 1943 systematization of secondary reinforcement in *Principles of Behavior* [PAGE 95]. Hull accepts Frolov's Russian experiments as demonstrating the "genuineness of secondary reinforcement, a first-rate scientific achievement," yet immediately objects that even together with other Russian work they leave unanswered the necessary and sufficient conditions [PAGE 95]. This places Hull in a genealogical position where the phenomenon is granted ontological status but its causal structure remains logically open.

Multi-week traps (temporal credit-assignment traps): If secondary reinforcement requires the simultaneous presence of S₁ and R₁—as questions 2 and 3 on [PAGE 95] imply—then over extended training horizons of weeks, the reinforcing event for S₂ is a moving target. In week 1, S₁ reliably evokes R₁; by week 4, spontaneous recovery, extinction, or central inhibition may weaken R₁. A black square paired in week 4 may therefore never encounter a strong R₁, so \mathcal{C}_2 receives degraded or zero reinforcement. The organism faces a non-stationary reinforcement landscape: the value of S₁ as a secondary reinforcer drifts across the multi-week timescale as the primary association consolidates or degrades. Without specifying this dynamics, the conditions necessary and sufficient for secondary reinforcement cannot be generalized beyond the immediate session described on [PAGE 95]. The "trap" is that a secondary association may appear to fail not because secondary reinforcement is impossible, but because the primary anchor upon which it parasitically depends has shifted during the inter-session interval.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: When the organism detects ambiguity in the secondary contingency—for example, S₂ is presented but S₁ is absent or R₁ is not evoked—the response hierarchy may regress to the dominant primary habit. Because R₁ (salivation to the metronome) is more strongly consolidated than the weak secondary response R₂, uncertainty collapses the internal model backward to the lower-order association. This is a recursive model of mind: the organism's estimate of "what stimulus predicts salivation" reverts to the first-order model because the second-order model has higher variance and lower confidence.

Counter-trap: A discrimination test in which S₂ is presented in the absence of S₁ and R₁. If salivation still occurs to S₂, the result may reflect stimulus generalization rather than true secondary reinforcement; this is a counter-trap because a positive result can be explained by perceptual similarity between the black square and some feature of the metronome context rather than associative transfer. The trap is sprung if R₂ extinguishes rapidly when S₁ is withheld, proving dependence on the \mathcal{C}_2 operator rather than on sensory generalization.

Clock: The metronome is explicitly a ticking device [PAGE 95], so S₁ carries temporal structure. Its periodicity functions as a clock signal that can entrain the timing of R₁. If S₂ is presented only during specific phases of the metronome cycle, secondary reinforcement may become phase-locked: R₂ occurs only at certain points in the metronome's temporal cycle. The clock thus modulates \mathcal{C}_2 not by the physical identity of the metronome but by its temporal information content (interval in seconds between ticks).

Competition: Crisis habit regression dominates under high uncertainty; the counter-trap falsifies spurious secondary associations; the clock determines when S₁ can serve as a valid temporal occasion setter for \mathcal{C}_2. These three mechanisms operate simultaneously, and the observed behavior is the outcome of their recursive interaction.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer (peripheral effector): Salivary glands possess a finite maximal secretion rate R_{\max}. If R₁ is already near R_{\max}, then even a perfectly established \mathcal{C}_2 cannot produce an observable R₂ because the response channel is saturated. Veto condition: dR/dt \approx 0 at R \approx R_{\max}. No associative strength can override a saturated effector; the behavioral expression of secondary reinforcement is vetoed at the periphery.

Biomechanical / neural layer (central inhibition): In Hull's framework, inhibitory processes can suppress the expression of a conditioned response. If an inhibitory state I(t) is active (e.g., due to fear, distraction, or competing responses), the motor expression of salivation is vetoed regardless of V₂. Formally: observed R₂ = V₂ \cdot (1 - I(t)), where I(t) \in [0,1]. If I(t) = 1, R₂ = 0 even if the associative strength is maximal.

Ambient / environmental layer (weather-like extraneous stimulation): The source text notes the "distinctly artificial nature of the conditioned-reflex experimental procedure" and warns that this artificiality may interfere with recognizing the bearing on "adaptive dynamics of ordinary life situations" [PAGE 95]. Ambient sensory noise—lighting, background sound, cage conditions—can overshadow the receptor discharge produced by S₂. If ambient stimulation A(t) exceeds the salience of S₂, the conjunction S₂ \land R₁ is not registered by the receptor layer, so \mathcal{C}_2 receives no input. This is an environmental veto: the black square may be physically present but perceptually masked.

Veto proof: A secondary reinforcement result is veto-proof only if it persists across all three layers simultaneously: effector not saturated, inhibition absent, and ambient salience sufficient. The text's admission of artificiality implies that many laboratory demonstrations may fail this veto-proof criterion because they are conducted in low-ambient, controlled settings that do not generalize to ordinary life.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Define RLVR = Reinforcement Layer Veto Rejection: a formal test in which a predicted secondary reinforcement outcome is rejected if a counterfactual manipulation at one layer removes the putative cause while holding associative history constant.

Scenario 1 (Stimulus-layer veto): Present S₂ (black square) paired with direct pharmacological or electrical elicitation of salivation R₁, but omit the metronome S₁ entirely. If secondary reinforcement does not occur (no measurable R₂ to S₂ in a later test), then S₁ presentation is necessary, not merely R₁ evocation. Prediction: V₂ = 0 and I(S₂ ; R₂ \mid \mathcal{H}_{S_1}) = 0 bits because the triplet contingency (S₂, S₁, R₁) is broken at the S₁ node [PAGE 95, question 2].

Scenario 2 (Response-layer veto): Present S₁ (metronome) and S₂ (black square) together, but physiologically block salivation so that R₁ = 0 (e.g., anticholinergic blockade or salivary denervation). If no secondary CR forms to S₂, then R₁ evocation is necessary. Prediction: V₂ = 0 and I(S₂ ; R₂ \mid \mathcal{H}_{S_1}) = 0 bits, because the reinforcing event R₁ never occurred despite S₁ presentation [PAGE 95, question 3].

Scenario 3 (Temporal-layer veto): Present S₁ and S₂ but separate them by \Delta t > \tau_{\text{crit}} (e.g., S₂ onset 30 seconds after S₁ offset), while still allowing R₁ to be evoked by S₁. Prediction: no secondary reinforcement because contiguity is violated; \mathcal{C}_2 collapses to zero even though both S₁ and R₁ occurred in the session.

Each scenario is verifiable by measuring salivation volume to S₂ in a later test trial and comparing against a yoked control that received the full triplet contingency within \Delta t < \tau_{\text{crit}}. Failure to observe R₂ under any one manipulation constitutes a veto of that component's sufficiency.
