Fourth, this vector enters a three-layer deep neural network (230 hidden units per layer, ReLU, batch normalization, dropout p=0.4) shared by two task heads: Model 1 outputs a softmax probability vector over {complete, incomplete, interception}, and Model 2 outputs a scalar expected-yards regression conditional on completion [PAGE 15-16]. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: Under pass-rush pressure, quarterbacks habitually regress to the earliest available short receiver (checkdown) rather than progressing through deeper reads. The model's expected-value operator p̂_C · ŷ typically assigns lower value to these short throws than to deep separations, so the checkdown is coded as suboptimal even when it is the rational choice to avoid a sack, fumble, or forced interception. Because Models 1 and 2 evaluate only the throw event and do not explicitly model sack probability or fumble risk [PAGE 29], the framework penalizes rational crisis conservatism.

Counter-trap: Defenses observe quarterback tendencies and can bait the model's argmax. If the model assigns high expected value to a deep receiver because of separation, but that receiver is in fact double-covered with a narrow throwing window, a rational quarterback correctly withholds. The thesis acknowledges that optimal target rates fall below random selection for most quarterbacks [PAGE 20, 28], interpreting this as either model noise or deviation from expected-yardage maximization. A recursive theory-of-mind reading suggests the latter: quarterbacks play a mixed strategy against adaptive defenses, and a risk-neutral expected-yards objective is a misspecified normative target. The decision metric therefore traps itself by treating deviation from the model's argmax as error rather than as equilibrium behavior.

Clock: Time is a hard constraint, not a soft feature. Contextual inputs include play clock remaining, time since snap, quarter, and time remaining [PAGE 13], but the static throw-time snapshot cannot capture the option value of waiting. Model 3 attempted to value holding the ball versus throwing at each timestep, but failed because receiver readiness windows are unlabeled, the true decision point is ambiguous (~0.5 s pre-release), and sacks always carry strongly negative value that biases the model toward throwing [PAGE 30-31]. The clock thus imposes a deadline at which the option value of waiting collapses to zero; any decision metric that ignores this deadline structure misprices delay and scrambling.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto: The tracking data lacks the vertical (z-axis) position of the ball, making precise ball placement unmodelable [PAGE 14]. Measurement noise corrupts speed and orientation estimates [PAGE 14]. The 10 Hz sampling rate may miss sub-frame events such as tips, deflections, or sudden defender breaks. Any claim that the model isolates quarterback execution from receiver play is vetoed whenever vertical ball trajectory or sub-frame contact events determine the outcome.

Biomechanical layer veto: Quarterback orientation, head/visual field, and throwing-motion anticipation are not included as explicit features [PAGE 31]. Pocket dynamics and pass-rush pressure are under-modeled [PAGE 31]. The release decision point is temporally ambiguous by roughly half a second [PAGE 30]. These omissions mean the model cannot distinguish a quarterback who held the ball because no receiver was open from one who simply failed to process the field. Veto applies in any pressure situation where pocket collapse timing determines the decision.

Weather layer veto: Contextual features include down, distance, yard line, quarter, time remaining, and score differential, but no weather, temperature, wind, or precipitation [PAGE 13]. Wind materially affects deep-ball trajectory and completion probability; without it, expected-value rankings in outdoor stadiums are confounded by atmospheric conditions. A veto is triggered for any game played in material wind or precipitation.

Adjudication rule: A quarterback-level causal inference from CPOE, YOE, or Decision Efficiency is valid only when (a) the play occurs in calm or dome conditions, (b) the pass is not tipped or deflected at the line, (c) receiver separation is sufficient that average-receiver assumption error is small, and (d) the throw is not made under extreme pocket collapse where unmodeled biomechanics dominate. Outside this regime, the metrics describe a joint QB–receiver–system estimand, not an isolated quarterback decision parameter [PAGE 21, 27].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
