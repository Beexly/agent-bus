The source is a computer-vision pipeline paper, not a ball-outcome or market-prediction paper. Its causal chain is: broadcast video → front-pitch-view (FPV) frame classification/detection → replay rejection → shot-boundary extraction → ball tracking → 2D bounce detection → 3D pitch-distance approximation → delivery-length class. The abstract states the target as “segment and extract video shots in which only the ball is being delivered” [PAGE 1 of 22]. But the dataset description says “The dataset contains 3000 unlabeled video clips containing the ball delivery and the stroke play” [PAGE 4 of 22]. That is a first gray-zone mismatch: the semantic object “only the ball is being delivered” is narrower than the delivered artifact “ball delivery and stroke play.” A downstream coach or researcher who assumes the clip ends at bounce will be wrong; a researcher who assumes labels exist will also be wrong because the dataset is explicitly unlabeled [PAGE 4 of 22].

Multi-hop derivation. First, the paper uses MobileNet-v2 image classification and YOLO object detection for FPV recognition, with a dual-stage combination [PAGE 7 of 22–PAGE 10 of 22]. Second, replay identification depends on scorecard presence: “The scorecard at the bottom is only shown when the footage being displayed is live” [PAGE 10 of 22]. Third, shot-boundary detection uses OpenCV BackgroundSubtractor: “If the foreground percentage of the frame is greater than the threshold, the video shot change is detected” [PAGE 10 of 22]. Fourth, RetinaNet tracks the ball; the paper says “The ball usually moves as fast as 90 miles per hour” and that 50 fps extraction was used because otherwise the ball appears blurry [PAGE 11 of 22]. Fifth, bounce position is found by the lowest 2D image position and mapped to pitch distance using a zoom factor; the paper explicitly says broadcast frames are 2D and “This does not contain the depth to determine how far the ball has traveled towards the batter” [PAGE 4 of 22]. Sixth, the delivery is classed by pitching distance from the batsman’s stumps: under 6 m full, 6–8 m good, over 8 m short [PAGE 12 of 22]. Each hop is conditional; failure at any hop propagates.

The largest internal gray-zone is the dual-stage metric. Page 9 says “Our core aim is to increase the precision value which occurs at the cost of a reasonable recall value” [PAGE 9 of 22]. Yet the reported dual-stage confusion matrix gives “= 99.93%” for recall and “= 84.38%” for precision [PAGE 17 of 22]. The conclusion then says “The proposed dual-stage model achieved 99.93% precision” [PAGE 19 of 22]. That is not a minor wording issue: it inverts the operating point. The dual-stage model has the highest recall and the lowest precision among the reported methods; it is a high-recall, false-positive-heavy detector, not a high-precision detector. If a commercial or coaching user trusts the conclusion rather than the table, they will over-collect FPV shots and flood the dataset with non-delivery frames.

A second gray-zone is generalization. The paper reports “100% accuracy when using a small dataset from a single game” [PAGE 8 of 22], but the main test set is 497,040 frames [PAGE 15 of 22] and the training set is only 385 images [PAGE 8 of 22]. That is a transfer-learning demonstration, not proof of cross-broadcaster robustness. The paper itself lists brightness, moving cameras, occlusion, replays, advertisements, and multiple visible pitches as hurdles [PAGE 2 of 22–PAGE 3 of 22]. A third gray-zone is geometry: pitch mapping is fixed by “rotating the image 20 degrees on the y-axis from the front perspective” [PAGE 13 of 22], and the paper concedes that delivery type “is not discrete but continuous” [PAGE 14 of 22]. Thus the 6 m and 8 m thresholds are annotation conventions, not physical discontinuities. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let a broadcast video be \(V=\{F_t\}_{t=1}^{T}\), with frames \(F_t\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}\). The paper’s segmentation operator is a composition
\[
S(V)=\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{D}(V),
\]
where \(\mathcal{D}\) is FPV classification/detection, \(\mathcal{R}\) is replay rejection, and \(\mathcal{B}\) is shot-boundary extraction [PAGE 3 of 22, PAGE 9 of 22–PAGE 10 of 22]. The output is a set of clips \(C_k\), nominally containing ball delivery and stroke play [PAGE 4 of 22].

FPV and replay indicators. Let \(A_t\in\{0,1\}\) be the FPV/delivery-scene indicator from MobileNet/YOLO. Let \(L_t\in\{0,1\}\) be scorecard presence. The paper’s replay rule is effectively \(Q_t\approx 1-L_t\), because the scorecard is treated as a live-broadcast indicator [PAGE 10 of 22]. A retained clip requires \(A_t=1\) and \(Q_t=0\) until a boundary.

Shot-boundary operator. For foreground mask \(M_t=\mathrm{BackgroundSubtractor}(F_t)\), with \(M_t(u,v)\in\{0,1\}\), define the foreground ratio
\[
r_t=\frac{1}{HW}\sum_{u=1}^{H}\sum_{v=1}^{W}M_t(u,v),\qquad r_t\in[0,1].
\]
The boundary alarm is
\[
B_t=\mathbf{1}[r_t>\tau],
\]
where \(\tau\) is dimensionless. This is exactly the paper’s threshold logic: “If the foreground percentage of the frame is greater than the threshold, the video shot change is detected” [PAGE 10 of 22]. Breakdown: if \(\tau\) is too low, camera motion and crowd motion create false cuts; if too high, cuts are missed. If \(HW\to\) small or the frame is static, \(r_t\) loses discriminability.

Detection metrics. For counts \(TP,FP,FN,TN\) (dimensionless),
\[
R=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad P=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad F_1=\frac{2PR}{P+R}.
\]
The paper reports \(R=233{,}358/(233{,}358+15{,}162)=93.89\%\) and \(P=233{,}358/(233{,}358+4{,}845)=97.96\%\) for MobileNet transfer learning [PAGE 15 of 22]. For the dual-stage model, the source reports \(R=248{,}349/(248{,}349+171)=99.93\%\) and \(P=248{,}349/(248{,}349+45{,}942)=84.38\%\) [PAGE 17 of 22]. The conclusion’s phrase “achieved 99.93% precision” [PAGE 19 of 22] is inconsistent with the page-17 table and must be treated as a reporting error.

Zoom and pitch mapping. Let \(H_1,H_2\) be batsman pixel heights in the release and bounce frames, and \(P_1\) the pitch pixel height in the release frame. The paper defines
\[
Z=\frac{H_2}{H_1},\qquad P_2=Z\,P_1,
\]
with \(Z\) dimensionless and \(H_i,P_i\) in pixels [PAGE 14 of 22]. First-order error propagation gives
\[
\frac{\delta Z}{Z}\approx \frac{\delta H_2}{H_2}+\frac{\delta H_1}{H_1},\qquad
\delta P_2\approx Z\,\delta P_1+P_1\,\delta Z.
\]
Breakdown: \(Z\) is singular as \(H_1\to0\); if the batsman is occluded, crouched, or partially outside the box, \(H_i\) measures pose, not zoom. The fixed 20-degree mapping [PAGE 13 of 22] is also a homography assumption; when the true camera azimuth differs, the ground-plane inverse becomes biased. The paper itself notes the delivery class “is not discrete but continuous” [PAGE 14 of 22].

Delivery class. Let \(d\) be estimated pitching distance from the batsman’s stumps in meters. The source criterion is
\[
C(d)=
\begin{cases}
\text{full-pitched}, & d<6\,\mathrm{m},\\
\text{good-length}, & 6\le d\le 8\,\mathrm{m},\\
\text{short-pitched}, & d>8\,\mathrm{m},
\end{cases}
\]
with the caveat that some observers use 6–9 m for good length [PAGE 12 of 22]. Because \(d\) is estimated from a 2D frame, the class boundaries \(d=6\,\mathrm{m}\) and \(d=8\,\mathrm{m}\) are high-uncertainty zones. A formal uncertainty measure is
\[
H(C\mid d)=-\sum_{c}p(c\mid d)\log_2 p(c\mid d)\ \text{bits},
\]
which peaks near the boundaries if \(p(c\mid d)\) is smooth. The source’s own example says a ball “pitches just above the good-length delivery which is still considered to be a good length by most viewers” [PAGE 14 of 22].

Motion-blur constraint. For ball speed \(v\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), exposure time \(\tau_{\exp}\) in seconds, pixel scale \(s\) in \(\mathrm{pixels\,m^{-1}}\), and blur tolerance \(\epsilon\) in pixels,
\[
L_{\mathrm{pix}}\approx s\,v\,\tau_{\exp},\qquad L_{\mathrm{pix}}\le\epsilon.
\]
At 90 mph, \(v\approx40.23\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) [PAGE 11 of 22]. Inter-frame displacement is
\[
\Delta x_{\mathrm{pix}}\approx \frac{s\,v}{f},
\]
where \(f\) is frames per second. The paper extracts at 50 fps to reduce blur [PAGE 11 of 22]. Breakdown: if \(f\) or shutter speed is too low, RetinaNet sees elongated blurs; the shortest-distance association can jump to a different blob. If two candidates are equidistant, the \(\arg\min\) association is non-unique and the track can swap.

Singularity regimes: \(R\) is undefined if \(TP+FN=0\); \(P\) is undefined if \(TP+FP=0\); \(Z\) is singular if \(H_1=0\); the boundary detector is unidentifiable if \(\tau\) lies inside the foreground-ratio distribution’s main mode; the distance classifier is ambiguous near 6 m and 8 m; and the 3D mapping is invalid when the camera azimuth/zoom or ground-plane homography departs from the fixed 20-degree approximation [PAGE 13 of 22–PAGE 14 of 22].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three agent perspectives must be separated. The researcher’s goal is a reusable clip dataset; the coach’s goal is delivery/stroke analysis; the model’s proxy goal is frame-level FPV/replay/boundary classification. When broadcast conditions shift, crisis habit regression is likely: the system falls back to the easiest learned cue—scorecard presence, background subtraction, or a MobileNet frame label—rather than solving the intended 3D delivery problem. The paper’s own challenges—brightness changes, moving cameras, occlusion, replays, advertisements, and multiple pitches [PAGE 2 of 22–PAGE 3 of 22]—are exactly the conditions that trigger that regression.

Counter-trap. The dual-stage design is described as a precision fix: “Our core aim is to increase the precision value which occurs at the cost of a reasonable recall value” [PAGE 9 of 22]. But the measured dual-stage operating point is recall-heavy and precision-light [PAGE 17 of 22]. A user who optimizes for the stated intention will over-trust the output; a user who optimizes for the table will add a precision filter downstream. The counter-trap is to “fix” low precision by adding more parallel stages, which can increase recall further while increasing false positives.

Clock. Real-time constraints are explicit: “Most live sports broadcasts are shot at 30 frames per second. Our model surpassed this by 9 frames per second on a low-end GPU” [PAGE 15 of 22], and single-stage detection reaches “312 frames per second” [PAGE 17 of 22]. The clock favors the dual-stage on recall but not necessarily on precision. A coach wanting a 20x compressed clip stream [PAGE 19 of 22] may tolerate false positives; a researcher building a clean dataset cannot. The theory-of-mind conclusion is that the pipeline is best treated as a high-recall candidate generator with a mandatory human or secondary precision audit.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto. Ball tracking is vetoed when motion blur violates \(L_{\mathrm{pix}}\approx s\,v\,\tau_{\exp}\le\epsilon\). The source warns that if shutter speed or frames per second is not high, “the ball appears as a blurry object, so these images are unreliable for training” [PAGE 11 of 22]. The paper’s 50-fps extraction is a mitigation, not a universal guarantee.

Geometric veto. The bounce-to-distance conversion is vetoed when the camera azimuth departs materially from the fixed 20-degree mapping [PAGE 13 of 22] or when \(H_1,H_2\) are not stable anatomical measurements. Since the source says the 2D frame “does not contain the depth” [PAGE 4 of 22], any claim of exact 3D bounce position without camera calibration is an approximation. The correct veto condition is: if the homography residual or zoom-factor variance exceeds a pre-registered tolerance, the 6 m/8 m class is not adjudicable.

Biomechanical veto. The zoom factor uses batsman height as a scale ruler [PAGE 14 of 22]. If the batsman crouches, switches stance, is partially occluded, or the detector box includes the bat, \(H_i\) changes for reasons unrelated to camera zoom. Bowler/umpire detection is similarly pose-dependent; the paper relies on the umpire’s back view and pitch annotation [PAGE 9 of 22]. A biomechanical veto should fire when the detected box aspect ratio or keypoint geometry is outside the training distribution.

Weather/illumination veto. The paper claims deep learning is robust to “mild changes to the contrast and brightness of videos and the built-in visual noise” [PAGE 2 of 22]. The word “mild” is the scope limit. Heavy shadow, rain, floodlight flicker, or a white ball against a white sightscreen is outside that claim. A weather veto should fire when ball-background contrast or frame exposure changes exceed the training distribution.

Broadcast-layer veto. The replay rule is vetoed when live footage lacks a scorecard or replay footage retains one. The source’s rule is categorical: scorecard present means live [PAGE 10 of 22]. Any broadcaster deviation breaks it. The boundary rule is similarly vetoed under continuous camera follow: the paper notes “There is no CUT or FADE and the ball is directly followed even after the ball touches the bat” [PAGE 4 of 22]. In that regime, background subtraction has no clean cut to detect.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
