<THINK_TRACE>
This source is a small-sample, within-subject psychophysiology study that tries to connect three layers: (1) the subjective layer, measured by post-climb fear ratings; (2) the muscular layer, measured by forearm electromyography; and (3) the computational layer, measured by mixed-effects regression and deep sequence models with random effects. The central inferential chain is: lead climbing creates larger fall consequence, larger fall consequence increases perceived fear, perceived fear co-occurs with muscle fatigue, and muscle fatigue can be indexed by mean frequency decline in the electromyographic spectrum. The paper states that “lead climbing involving larger falls than top rope climbing, which may result in different perceived risk and fear” [PAGE 1]. It also states that “muscle fatigue correlates significantly with increased fear during lead climbing” [PAGE 1]. That is the main claim to be audited.

The first gray-zone issue is sample size and reporting. The abstract says “We conducted an experiment with 19 climbers” [PAGE 1], while the methods say “We conducted a study with 20 recreational climbers” [PAGE 7]. Later, the text says “Fifteen out of nineteen participants reported participating in other extreme sports” [PAGE 7], and the mixed model section says “Participant 20 was excluded from the linear mixed effects model due to faulty EMG signal measurement” [PAGE 16]. This is not a fatal contradiction, but it matters because the effective analytic sample depends on which model is being discussed. The linear mixed model likely uses nineteen participants after exclusion, while the descriptive or deep-learning sample may retain twenty depending on the split. This creates a risk of denominator drift when comparing statistical and machine-learning results.

The second gray-zone issue is the construct called fear. The authors measured anxiety, fear of falling due to fatigue, and fear of heights. They then checked aggregation using Cronbach’s alpha and principal component analysis, reporting “a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.92” and “explaining 87% of the variance” [PAGE 16]. This justifies treating the three items as one latent fear construct, but it does not remove measurement error. The questionnaire scale runs from one to ten [PAGE 15], so the aggregate score is bounded, ordinal, and generated retrospectively after video review [PAGE 8]. The authors acknowledge that “Self-reports after climbing, such as perceived fear, only provide an approximation of the emotional states” [PAGE 23]. Therefore, any equation that treats the fear score as a Gaussian continuous outcome is a modeling convenience, not a literal physical quantity.

The third gray-zone issue is the difference between muscle activity and muscle fatigue. The source distinguishes integrated electromyography as an amplitude-related index of activation, while mean frequency is treated as the main fatigue marker. It says, “The most common measure of muscle fatigue is the decrease of mean frequency (MNF) of the EMG signal” [PAGE 5], and “An increase in amplitude is also correlated with increased fatigue” [PAGE 5]. In the linear mixed model, MNF shows the key interaction with lead climbing, while integrated EMG does not. Table 1 reports “MNF:style1” with estimate “-0.089” and p-value “.019” [PAGE 20], while “IEMG:style1” has estimate “0.283” and p-value “.512” [PAGE 20]. This supports the claim that fatigue, rather than raw activation, is more tightly linked to fear in lead climbing. However, the main effect of MNF itself is essentially null: “MNF” estimate “-0.0008”, p-value “.980” [PAGE 20]. The effect appears only in interaction with lead climbing. This means the statement should not be simplified into “fatigue causes fear” in a global sense. The better formulation is: within lead climbing, lower mean frequency is associated with higher reported fear.

The fourth gray-zone issue is temporal mismatch. The physiological features are windowed, while the fear reports are collected after the climb while watching the video [PAGE 8]. This creates a postdictive label problem: the model learns a mapping from physiological windows to later remembered fear, not necessarily from physiological state to momentary fear. The authors recognize this and suggest real-time reporting in future work [PAGE 23]. Therefore, any causal language must be softened. The data support association and prediction, not direct moment-to-moment causation.

The fifth gray-zone issue is the deep-learning evaluation. The best reported model is the GRU with random effects, with “RMSE (2.49 ± 0.27)”, “MAE (1.42 ± 0.18)” and “MSE (6.25 ± 1.39)” [PAGE 20]. The target variable has “a standard deviation of 4.37” [PAGE 21], so the RMSE-to-standard-deviation ratio is “0.57” [PAGE 21]. This is a useful normalization, but it still means the typical prediction error is more than half of the natural variation in fear scores. The model is better than the linear mixed-effects benchmark in predictive error, but it does not become an oracle. The random-effects result also depends on the split strategy: the authors “randomly added one ascent style for every climber in the training set” and then split the remaining climbs [PAGE 13]. This reduces participant separation, but it may still leak climb-level structure, because multiple windows from the same ascent may appear across partitions if the ascent-level identity is not held out. Therefore, the random-effects gain may partly reflect memorization of participant-specific response style rather than generalization to new climbers.

In summary, the strongest defensible interpretation is: in this indoor climbing sample, lead climbing is associated with higher fear, later height intervals are associated with higher fear, and decreasing mean frequency is more strongly associated with fear during lead climbing than during top rope climbing. The deep-learning extension suggests that personalization via random effects can improve predictive error in repeated-measures psychophysiology, but the small sample, retrospective labels, and split design prevent strong causal or cross-population claims.

Let the aggregate fear score be denoted by \(y\). The three fear items are each bounded on an ordinal scale from 1 to 10 [PAGE 15], so the unweighted sum satisfies
\[
y = a_1 + a_2 + a_3,
\qquad a_k \in \{1,2,\ldots,10\},
\qquad y \in \{3,4,\ldots,30\}.
\]
The unit of \(y\) is therefore scale points, not a physical unit. The source reports high internal consistency, “a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.92”, and that principal component analysis “confirm[ed] that the scores loaded onto a single component, explaining 87% of the variance” [PAGE 16]. This supports a one-factor aggregation but does not make \(y\) an interval-scaled physical measurement.

For electromyography, the source says the raw signal was sampled at \(f_s = 2000\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) and filtered with a 50 Hz notch, then a 20 to 450 Hz bandpass [PAGE 9]. A sliding EMG window of 1000 time points with overlap 500 corresponds to a window duration of
\[
T_{\mathrm{EMG}} = \frac{1000}{2000\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}} = 0.5\ \mathrm{s},
\]
and a hop of
\[
H_{\mathrm{EMG}} = \frac{500}{2000\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}} = 0.25\ \mathrm{s}.
\]
Mean frequency can be written formally as the spectral centroid
\[
\mathrm{MNF}_w =
\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K} f_k P_w(f_k)}
{\sum_{k=1}^{K} P_w(f_k)},
\]
where \(P_w(f_k)\) is the estimated power at frequency bin \(f_k\) in window \(w\), and the unit of \(\mathrm{MNF}_w\) is hertz, \(\mathrm{Hz}\). Integrated electromyography can be represented generically as
\[
\mathrm{iEMG}_w =
\sum_{n \in w} \lvert x_{\mathrm{EMG}}[n] \rvert \Delta t,
\]
where \(x_{\mathrm{EMG}}[n]\) is the filtered EMG sample and \(\Delta t = 1/f_s\). If EMG is normalized, \(\mathrm{iEMG}\) is dimensionless; if EMG is in electrical amplitude units, the unit is amplitude times seconds. The source does not specify a physical amplitude unit, so no unit should be invented.

For electrocardiography, the source says ECG was processed with a bandpass from 1 Hz to 30 Hz at a sampling rate of 500 Hz [PAGE 9]. Heart rate can be defined from successive R-peak intervals \(RR_i\) in seconds as
\[
\mathrm{HR} = \frac{60}{\overline{RR}},
\qquad \text{unit: beats per minute}.
\]
The source also mentions RMSSD for heart-rate variability [PAGE 10], which has units of milliseconds if \(RR\) intervals are measured in milliseconds. However, the predictive model section says the time-series features included “MNF, heart rate (HR) and acceleration of the arm” [PAGE 12], so RMSSD is not necessarily part of the deep-learning input.

The linear mixed-effects model can be written as
\[
y_{ij} =
\beta_0
+ \beta_1 \mathrm{Lead}_{ij}
+ \beta_2 I_{3,ij}
+ \beta_3 I_{4,ij}
+ \beta_4 \mathrm{Experience}_i
+ \beta_5 M_{ij}
+ \beta_6 \mathrm{Lead}_{ij} M_{ij}
+ b_{0i}
+ \varepsilon_{ij},
\]
where \(i\) indexes participant, \(j\) indexes interval, \(M_{ij}\) is either MNF or iEMG, \(I_{3,ij}\) and \(I_{4,ij}\) are interval indicators, \(b_{0i}\) is a participant random intercept, and \(\varepsilon_{ij}\) is residual error. The unit of all fixed-effect estimates is fear-score points per predictor unit. For MNF, the interaction estimate \(-0.089\) [PAGE 20] means that each one-hertz decrease in MNF is associated with approximately \(0.089\) additional fear points during lead climbing, relative to top rope climbing, under the model assumptions.

For the deep-learning random-effects extension, the source gives
\[
y = f(X,\theta) + Zb + \epsilon,
\]
with \(b \sim \mathcal{N}(0,D)\) [PAGE 11]. Here \(f(X,\theta)\) is the fixed-effect neural prediction, \(Z\) is the random-effect design matrix, \(b\) is the vector of random effects, and \(\epsilon\) is noise. The marginal covariance is
\[
V(\psi) = Z D(\psi) Z' + \sigma_e^2 I.
\]
The training objective is not ordinary squared error but negative log-likelihood:
\[
\mathrm{NLL}(f,\theta,D \mid y)
=
\frac{1}{2}(y-f(X,\theta))' V(\psi)^{-1} (y-f(X,\theta))
+
\frac{1}{2}\log |V(\psi)|
+
\frac{n}{2}\log 2\pi.
\]
The first term has units of squared fear-score points weighted by inverse covariance; the logarithm term is dimensionless; the full expression is measured in nats when natural logarithm is used. The source explicitly says that instead of minimizing a loss such as MSE, “we minimize the negative log likelihood” [PAGE 11].

For a random-intercept model,
\[
D(\psi) = \sigma_{b1}^2 I_j,
\]
so \(V(\psi)\) is block diagonal and inversion is simple. For a random-slope model, the source gives the two-by-two covariance block
\[
D(\psi) =
\begin{pmatrix}
\sigma_{b0}^2 & \rho_{0,1}\sigma_{b0}\sigma_{b1} \\
\rho_{0,1}\sigma_{b0}\sigma_{b1} & \sigma_{b1}^2
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
This matrix must be positive semidefinite. It becomes singular if \(\rho_{0,1} = \pm 1\), if either variance component is zero, or if the random-effect design has insufficient rank. The marginal covariance \(V(\psi)\) becomes singular if \(\sigma_e^2 = 0\) and \(D(\psi)\) is singular, or if \(Z\) is rank deficient. In finite samples, the Gaussian negative log-likelihood becomes unstable when \(V(\psi)\) is nearly singular, because \(V(\psi)^{-1}\) and \(\log |V(\psi)|\) both become numerically sensitive. This is especially relevant here because the study has limited participants and repeated climbs [PAGE 7], so the random-effect covariance must be estimated from sparse cluster-level information.

The model-performance summary also requires formal interpretation. The best random-effects GRU has
\[
\mathrm{RMSE} = 2.49,
\qquad
\mathrm{SD}(y) = 4.37,
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{RMSE}}{\mathrm{SD}} = 0.57,
\]
and
\[
\mathrm{IQR}(y) = 6,
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{RMSE}}{\mathrm{IQR}} = 0.42,
\]
as reported on pages 21 and 22. These ratios are dimensionless. They show predictive error relative to the scale of the outcome, but they do not prove individual-level clinical usefulness. The linear random-effects benchmark has \(\mathrm{RMSE}/\mathrm{SD} = 1.0\) [PAGE 22], which means its error is approximately the same magnitude as the outcome standard deviation.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage of this paper is a convergence of three research streams: climbing psychophysiology, mixed-effects longitudinal modeling, and personalized deep learning with random effects. The mixed-effects lineage is explicit in the reference to Laird and Ware, “Random-effects models for longitudinal data” [PAGE 39]. That tradition treats repeated measurements as clustered observations and models between-subject variation through random intercepts or slopes. The paper then adapts that tradition to neural networks by citing Simchoni and Rosset, who integrated random effects into deep neural networks [PAGE 3, PAGE 40]. It also cites Kilian et al., who add random effects to supervised machine-learning regression [PAGE 3, PAGE 40]. The source says, “The inclusion of random effects in deep learning has only recently been explored in the literature” [PAGE 3]. This positions the paper as an applied bridge: it uses a recent statistical-machine-learning method to analyze a psychophysiological problem.

The climbing-psychophysiology lineage is also visible. Hodgson et al. found that lead climbing elicited higher cognitive and somatic stress than top rope climbing in intermediate climbers [PAGE 5]. Draper, Fryer, and colleagues found that intermediate climbers reported leading as more physically and mentally demanding than top roping [PAGE 5]. Pijpers et al. manipulated height and found greater muscle fatigue, higher lactate, higher heart rate, longer climbing times, less smooth motion, more explosive movements, and slower prolonged grasps on higher routes [PAGE 5]. The present study extends this by asking whether muscle fatigue, indexed by mean frequency, is associated with fear differently across ascent style. The result that the MNF-by-style interaction is significant [PAGE 16] is therefore genealogically consistent with earlier findings that lead climbing imposes greater perceived danger and physiological cost.

The affective-theory lineage runs through Russell’s circumplex model, where fear is high arousal and negative valence [PAGE 6]. The source also distinguishes fear from anxiety by saying fear is directed at a real, external, known danger, while anxiety is directed at an unknown threat [PAGE 6]. This distinction matters because climbing risk can be both real and imagined. The authors choose the term fear because two self-report measures used that term and only one used anxiety [PAGE 6]. This is a measurement-genealogy decision, not a metaphysical claim that fear and anxiety are identical.

A multi-week trap in this genealogy is the assumption that repeated exposure to a climbing route produces stable emotional learning. The protocol uses the same route for both lead and top rope, with pseudorandom order, and says this allowed rest between trials to minimize “accumulated fatigue and prior route knowledge” [PAGE 8]. However, route knowledge is not fully eliminated. A climber who first climbs top rope may learn hold sequences, clipping positions, and rest points. A climber who first climbs lead may experience higher fear while simultaneously learning the route. If the route becomes easier across trials, fear may decrease for reasons unrelated to rope protocol. If fatigue accumulates despite rest, fear may increase for reasons unrelated to style. The pseudorandomization mitigates but does not erase this trap.

Another trap is sensor-signal validity. The correlation analysis removed participants 3 and 6 because of noisy ECG signals [PAGE 14], and the mixed model excluded participant 20 due to faulty EMG measurement [PAGE 16]. These exclusions improve signal quality but reduce power. They also create a possible selection effect: the retained participants may have cleaner physiological signals and therefore different fear-fatigue coupling than excluded participants. Because the sample is small, such exclusions can materially alter the random-effects covariance structure.

A deeper retro-causal trap is reverse inference. The model may appear to predict fear from fatigue, but the causal direction could be: fear increases sympathetic arousal, arousal increases muscle tension, sustained tension increases fatigue, and fatigue then increases perceived fall risk, which further increases fear. This feedback loop is plausible given the circumplex model of high arousal and the physiological manifestations listed by the source, such as increased heart rate, blood flow to muscles, adrenaline release, and muscle tension [PAGE 6]. Therefore, the paper’s result should be read as evidence of a psychophysiological coupling, not as a one-way path from muscle fatigue to fear.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind analysis asks: what does the climber believe about the fall, what does the climber believe the belayer or experimenter expects, and what does the model infer about the climber? In top rope climbing, the rope is anchored above the climber, minimizing fall distance and perceived risk [PAGE 2]. In lead climbing, the climber must manage and secure the rope to points of protection, so fall distance is variable and often longer [PAGE 2]. The climber’s belief about fall consequence therefore differs by ascent style. This belief can alter grip force, movement smoothness, breathing, and attention. Those behavioral changes can alter EMG features. Thus, the physiological signal is not merely a readout of muscle fatigue; it is also a readout of the climber’s model of danger.

The crisis-habit regression component is the idea that fear is not just a momentary response but a learned prediction based on prior exposure. More experienced climbers may have fewer unsafe lead falls and more safe experiences at height [PAGE 5]. This could reduce fear for the same physiological state. The source says experience level is important when considering psychophysiological responses [PAGE 5]. In recursive terms, an experienced climber may interpret a given forearm pump as manageable, while a less experienced climber may interpret the same pump as a sign of imminent failure. The same MNF value can therefore map to different fear scores depending on the climber’s internal model. This is exactly why random effects matter: they allow each participant to have a different baseline or slope.

The counter-trap component appears in the protocol detail that top rope participants had to unclip the rope from quickdraws as they ascended “to match the challenge of clipping the rope in the lead ascent style” [PAGE 8]. This is a thoughtful design choice because it prevents top rope from becoming trivially easy in terms of rope management. However, it also introduces a subtle counter-trap: the act of unclipping may create a feeling of exposure or vulnerability even though the rope is anchored above. If unclipping induces a sense of momentary risk, top rope may not be a pure low-fear control condition. The model may then underestimate the true fear difference if top rope fear is elevated by unclipping.

The clock component is the temporal architecture. The deep-learning models process “fixed time windows of 30 sequential measurements” [PAGE 12], and the input windows are generated with “no overlap between consecutive windows” [PAGE 13]. This means the model sees a discrete temporal chain of physiological states and predicts a retrospective fear label. The clock matters because fatigue is cumulative, fear is retrospective, and height is monotonic across intervals. If the model learns that later windows have higher fear, it may exploit interval order as much as muscle physiology. The interval effects in the mixed model support this: later intervals show higher fear, for example Interval 3 and Interval 4 have significant positive estimates [PAGE 16]. The recursive risk is that the model confuses “time on the wall” with “fear caused by fatigue.” To avoid this, one would need to control for height, elapsed time, and movement kinematics, or use real-time fear labels.

A further recursive layer is the experimenter’s belief about the participant. The experimenter may assume that video-assisted recall gives accurate fear timing. The participant may assume the experimenter wants consistent reports across quarters. The model then learns the aggregate of these assumptions. The source acknowledges that self-reports “cannot fully capture the dynamic nature of the psychological states during climbing” [PAGE 23]. Therefore, the theory of mind in this study should be framed as a three-agent loop: climber, experimenter, and model. The climber regulates fear and movement; the experimenter structures the measurement; the model infers latent state from noisy physiological traces and retrospective labels.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto asks whether a physical, biomechanical, or environmental condition could invalidate the model’s apparent inference. The source describes an indoor climbing wall of “19.3 m” height [PAGE 2] and says indoor climbing generally offers a safe, controlled environment if safety procedures are followed [PAGE 5]. This reduces, but does not eliminate, weather-related confounding. There is no outdoor wind, precipitation, sun exposure, or rock-temperature variation in the main protocol. However, indoor climate can still matter. Skin temperature, humidity, sweat, and electrode adhesion can affect EMG amplitude and impedance. The BioPoint sensor includes skin temperature capability [PAGE 9], but the published modeling features do not include skin temperature [PAGE 12]. If the indoor environment were unusually cold or humid, electrode contact and muscle contraction dynamics could change. Without sensor-level calibration data, such a condition would justify a veto until environmental covariates are measured.

A biomechanical veto concerns the measured muscle. The sensor was placed on the flexor digitorum superficialis, approximately three-quarters of the way from elbow to wrist toward the ulnar side, and placement was confirmed by palpation during finger flexion [PAGE 9]. This is anatomically relevant because forearm flexors are central to grip and climbing. However, EMG is sensitive to electrode shift, skin stretch, and movement artifact. The source uses a bandpass filter and notch filter to reduce noise [PAGE 9], and it includes wrist acceleration as a feature [PAGE 12]. Including acceleration can help the model distinguish motion from muscle activity, but it can also create a confound: if fear causes more erratic movement, acceleration may become a proxy for fear rather than a nuisance variable. If acceleration is strongly collinear with fear labels, the model may learn movement style rather than muscle fatigue.

A second biomechanical veto concerns the interpretation of mean frequency. The source states that fatigue reduces motor-unit firing rate and that the most common measure of muscle fatigue is decreased mean frequency [PAGE 4, PAGE 5]. However, mean frequency can also be affected by electrode-muscle geometry, subcutaneous tissue, temperature, and signal quality. If electrode displacement occurs during climbing, MNF may change for non-fatigue reasons. The source reports that participant 20 was excluded due to faulty EMG signal measurement [PAGE 16], which shows that signal validity was not guaranteed for all trials. Therefore, the MNF-fear interaction should be interpreted as conditional on clean EMG placement and stable signal quality.

A third veto concerns fall consequence and fatigue coupling. In lead climbing, falls are longer and more likely if muscles are fatigued [PAGE 22]. This is a physical plausibility argument: if the forearm flexors fatigue, grip security may decrease, which increases the probability of a fall or a slipping hold. If the climber believes a fall is more likely, fear should increase. This mechanism supports the observed interaction. However, the study does not directly measure grip failure, hold slip, or near-fall events. Therefore, the physical mechanism is plausible but not directly verified. A veto would apply if video review showed no relationship between MNF decline and observable grip degradation, or if fear increased equally in conditions where grip security remained constant.

A fourth veto concerns model identifiability under small samples. The random-effects covariance matrix must be estimated from repeated measures per participant. With only about twenty participants and limited climbs per participant, the variance components may be unstable. If the random-effect covariance is near singular, the negative log-likelihood can become overly optimistic about personalization. The source reports that random-effects models consistently outperformed standard models [PAGE 20], but this was evaluated on a test set that included ascent styles from participants also present in training [PAGE 13]. A strict veto would apply if a leave-one-climber-out evaluation showed no improvement or degradation. In that case, the random-effects gain would be participant memorization rather than generalizable personalization.

The final adjudication is therefore conditional: the main conclusion is physically plausible under clean sensor placement, stable indoor conditions, valid MNF measurement, and appropriate handling of participant clustering. It is vetoed if environmental covariates are uncontrolled, if MNF changes are driven by electrode artifacts, if fear labels are too temporally coarse to support physiological inference, or if random-effects gains disappear under strict cross-participant validation.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Leave-one-climber-out personalization veto.
A verifiable counterfactual is to retrain the models with a strict leave-one-climber-out protocol. Instead of randomly placing one ascent style for each climber in training and splitting remaining climbs [PAGE 13], hold out an entire climber for testing. The random-effects model must then predict fear for a participant whose random effect was not observed during training. If the GRU with random effects no longer outperforms the standard GRU, or if its RMSE-to-standard-deviation ratio rises above the linear random-effects benchmark value of 1.0 [PAGE 22], the personalization claim is vetoed. Prediction: because random effects require participant-specific covariance estimates, leave-one-climber-out performance will degrade more than within-participant performance. The original result should therefore be interpreted as within-participant personalization, not as transfer to unseen climbers.

2. Integrated EMG significance reversal veto.
A second verifiable counterfactual is to preregister a larger replication with integrated EMG as the primary predictor. In the source, integrated EMG is not significant: “IEMG” estimate “0.636”, p-value “.097”, and “IEMG:style1” estimate “0.283”, p-value “.512” [PAGE 20]. If a replication with adequate power finds a significant main effect of integrated EMG, but no significant interaction with lead climbing, then the specific claim that fatigue rather than activation drives fear is vetoed. Prediction: because the source’s integrated EMG p-value is near the conventional threshold, a larger sample could make the main effect significant. However, the interaction with climbing style is less likely to become significant unless fear is genuinely coupled to fatigue-specific spectral decline rather than general muscle activation.

3. Real-time fear timing veto.
A third verifiable counterfactual is to collect real-time fear ratings during the climb instead of retrospective video-assisted ratings. The source states that post-climb reports “cannot fully capture the dynamic nature of the psychological states during climbing” [PAGE 23]. If real-time fear ratings peak before MNF decline, or if fear peaks at clipping or height-exposure events independent of MNF, then the retrospective aggregate label is temporally invalid as a fatigue-driven fear marker. Prediction: real-time fear will align more strongly with visible risk cues, such as clipping, high reaches, or anticipated fall distance, while MNF decline will lag behind sustained grip effort. If that occurs, the mixed-model association remains statistically valid but the causal interpretation shifts from “fatigue causes fear” to “fatigue and risk exposure jointly shape remembered fear.”
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
