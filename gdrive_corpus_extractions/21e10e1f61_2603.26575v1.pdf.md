<THINK_TRACE>
This source is not merely a climbing physiology note; it is a testbed for how a psychophysiological label can be modeled from noisy, temporally correlated sensor streams when the label itself is subjective and retrospective. The central causal narrative in the paper is that lead climbing increases perceived risk because the climber must manage the rope and secure protection while ascending, whereas top rope climbing reduces fall exposure. The source says, “In top rope climbing, the rope is anchored above the climber, which minimizes the fall distances and perceived risk” and “in lead climbing, the climber must manage and secure the rope to points of protection along the route” [PAGE 2]. This creates a plausible psychological stressor: longer fall distance and greater responsibility should increase fear. The physiological arm of the argument is that sustained forearm work produces fatigue, and fatigue is detectable through EMG features, especially a decrease in mean frequency and an increase in integrated EMG amplitude. The paper states, “The most common measure of muscle fatigue is the decrease of mean frequency (MNF) of the EMG signal” [PAGE 5]. The empirical claim is that fatigue, more than raw muscle activation, is associated with fear during the more demanding lead condition.

The gray-zone audit begins with the difference between prediction and explanation. The linear mixed-effects model is interpretive: it asks whether lead style, interval, experience, and fatigue-related EMG features covary with the composite fear rating. The deep-learning model is predictive: it asks whether a sequence-to-value network plus random effects can reduce error on held-out climb windows. These are not the same inferential object. A significant interaction in the mixed model does not prove that the GRU learned the same causal mechanism, and a lower RMSE in the random-effects GRU does not prove that the model captured fear rather than participant-specific label regularities. The source itself separates these tasks: Section 4.1 is “statistical analysis” using linear mixed-effects modeling, while Section 4.2 is “model comparison” using MSE, MAE, and RMSE [PAGE 14, PAGE 18].

A second gray zone is the label timing. The climbers watched a video after the climb and reported fear by route quarter. The source acknowledges, “Self-reports after climbing, such as perceived fear, only provide an approximation of the emotional states” and “They are subject to memory bias and cannot fully capture the dynamic nature of the psychological states during climbing” [PAGE 23]. This is critical because the model receives 30 sequential physiological measurements and predicts a composite score associated with a quarter. If the physiological signal is from one segment but the label is a reconstructed evaluation of that segment, the model may learn a post-hoc summarization process rather than real-time fear. The absence of real-time labels is therefore not a minor limitation; it changes the causal interpretation.

A third gray zone is sample handling. The abstract mentions “19 climbers” [PAGE 1], while the methods say “We conducted a study with 20 recreational climbers” [PAGE 7]. Later, participants 3 and 6 were removed from correlation because of noisy ECG, and participant 20 was excluded from the linear mixed-effects model because of faulty EMG [PAGE 14, PAGE 16]. The text also contains an interval estimate inconsistency: the narrative describes Interval 4 estimates that differ from the table. This does not invalidate the overall finding, but it warns against treating every reported coefficient as stable. In small psychophysiology datasets, exclusion, preprocessing, and random initialization can materially shift conclusions.

A fourth gray zone is the data split. The source says, “We split the participant’s ascent trials in a 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% test distribution” and then clarifies, “Rather than separating participants entirely between sets, we randomly added one ascent style for every climber in the training set” [PAGE 13]. This means the test set can contain climbs from climbers who are also represented in training. Random effects can improve performance in this setting by partially memorizing participant-specific baselines. That does not make the result worthless, but it does mean the reported improvement is not yet a strict test of generalization to a new climber. A leave-one-climber-out or leave-one-participant-out protocol would be needed for that claim.

A fifth gray zone is the meaning of “muscle activity” versus “muscle fatigue.” The paper correctly distinguishes them: iEMG indexes activation, while MNF indexes fatigue-related spectral compression. The source says, “The MNF is a well-established measure of muscle fatigue” and “iEMG measures muscle activation based on the neuronal input to the muscle” [PAGE 9]. The empirical pattern supports this distinction: iEMG’s main effect and interaction with style were not statistically significant, while the MNF interaction with lead climbing was significant. The source states that the MNF-style interaction “suggests that muscle fatigue was more strongly associated with self-reported fear during lead climbing than top rope climbing” [PAGE 16]. This is the core empirical result. The deep-learning result reinforces the utility of temporal modeling but does not independently establish that fear is caused by fatigue.

Overall, the safest interpretation is: lead climbing increases fear-related ratings, and forearm fatigue is a measurable correlate of that increase; random effects and GRU temporal modeling improve predictive fit under the study’s split; but the design cannot fully separate fear from effort, task kinematics, memory bias, and participant-specific reporting habits.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s principal statistical operator is the nonlinear mixed-effects regression form:

\[
y = f(X, \theta) + Zb + \epsilon
\]

[PAGE 11]

Here \(y\) is the outcome vector, \(X\) is the fixed-effect design matrix, \(\theta\) are neural or parametric fixed-effect parameters, \(Z\) is the random-effect design matrix, \(b\) is the random-effect vector, and \(\epsilon\) is residual noise. The source states that \(f(X, \theta)\) “can be substituted with a neural network architecture” and that they chose \(g(Z) = Z\) for interpretability [PAGE 11]. The random effects are assumed Gaussian:

\[
b \sim \mathcal{N}(0, D)
\]

where \(D\) is a positive semi-definite covariance matrix [PAGE 11]. The marginal covariance used in the likelihood is:

\[
V(\psi) = ZD(\psi)Z^{\top} + \sigma_e^2 I
\]

[PAGE 11]

The training objective for random-effect models is not ordinary MSE but negative log-likelihood:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{NLL}
=
\frac{1}{2}(y - f(X,\theta))^{\top}V(\psi)^{-1}(y - f(X,\theta))
+
\frac{1}{2}\log |V(\psi)|
+
\frac{n}{2}\log 2\pi
\]

[PAGE 11]

In information-theoretic units, this is an expected surprisal penalty measured in nats if the logarithm is natural. In regression units, \(y\) has the units of the fear score, so the quadratic term contributes score-squared units scaled by inverse covariance units, while the log-determinant term is dimensionless probability-model complexity.

The signal-processing operators define the measurement units. The EMG was sampled at \(2000\ \mathrm{Hz}\): “The EMG signal was sampled at a frequency of 2000 Hz” [PAGE 9]. A 1000-sample EMG window therefore corresponds to:

\[
T_{\mathrm{EMG}} = \frac{1000}{2000}\ \mathrm{s} = 0.5\ \mathrm{s}
\]

with 500-sample overlap, equivalent to a hop of:

\[
H_{\mathrm{EMG}} = \frac{500}{2000}\ \mathrm{s} = 0.25\ \mathrm{s}
\]

The ECG was sampled at \(500\ \mathrm{Hz}\). A 250-sample ECG window corresponds to \(0.5\ \mathrm{s}\), and a 125-sample overlap corresponds to a \(0.25\ \mathrm{s}\) hop. The IMU was sampled at \(50\ \mathrm{Hz}\), with a 0.1 to 25 Hz bandpass [PAGE 9]. Acceleration in the horizontal x-direction was analyzed; in SI terms this is normally \(\mathrm{m/s^2}\), although the exact sensor unit convention is not stated in the source. MNF has units of \(\mathrm{Hz}\). iEMG has amplitude units dependent on preprocessing normalization; if unnormalized, it is in voltage-time units, but the paper treats it primarily as a relative activation index. Heart rate has units of beats per minute, and RMSSD has units of milliseconds.

The fear outcome is a questionnaire composite. The source says response scales ranged from one to ten and that three fear-related scores were aggregated because they measured the same construct [PAGE 15]. If the composite is the sum of three items, its theoretical range is 3 to 30 fear-score points. The source reports a target standard deviation of 4.37 and interquartile range of 6 [PAGE 21]. The random-effects GRU reported RMSE \(2.49 \pm 0.27\), MAE \(1.42 \pm 0.18\), and MSE \(6.25 \pm 1.39\) [PAGE 20]. These units are fear-score points for RMSE and MAE, and fear-score-squared points for MSE.

Breakdown and singularity regimes are explicit in the covariance structure. If \(V(\psi)\) becomes singular, the inverse \(V(\psi)^{-1}\) does not exist and the NLL is undefined. This can occur if the random-effect design matrix \(Z\) lacks rank, if a cluster has too few observations to identify a random slope, or if the random-effect variance collapses to zero in a way that makes the likelihood flat. For a random intercept model, the source says the covariance matrix becomes diagonal and inversion is element-wise [PAGE 12]. For random slopes, the 2-by-2 block must be invertible; if the slope variance and intercept-slope covariance are not identifiable, the model breaks down. In practical terms, a participant with only one usable climb cannot support a participant-specific slope. A participant with all-zero variance in the outcome cannot inform a random slope. A participant whose physiological windows are dominated by sensor dropout cannot support a stable random intercept.

The GRU temporal operator also has breakdown regimes. The source claims GRU networks “effectively learn patterns across time steps without suffering from vanishing gradient problems common in traditional recurrent networks” [PAGE 3]. That is an architectural mitigation, not an absolute guarantee. The model uses fixed windows of 30 sequential measurements [PAGE 12]. Therefore, any dependency longer than the receptive field created by those 30 windows is structurally invisible. If fear depends on cumulative fatigue over a route longer than the modeled window, the model must infer it only indirectly through local slope and amplitude patterns. If the gradient signal is weak, if dropout is excessive, or if participant-specific random effects dominate, the GRU may learn cluster identity rather than temporal fear dynamics.

The preprocessing chain also has failure modes. The EMG notch filter targets 50 Hz electrical interference, followed by a 20 to 450 Hz bandpass of order 4 [PAGE 9]. If the electrical environment is not 50 Hz, the notch is mismatched. If electrode contact shifts, the spectrum changes for mechanical reasons rather than neuromuscular fatigue reasons. If the climbing movement artifact exceeds the bandpass assumptions, MNF may reflect motion artifact rather than fatigue. These are not abstract concerns: they define the physical boundary conditions under which the model’s equations remain meaningful.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The conceptual lineage of this paper runs through three historical streams: EMG fatigue physiology, climbing psychophysiology, and mixed-effects machine learning. The first stream treats EMG spectral compression as a marker of localized muscular fatigue. The source states that muscle fatigue involves “various changes within the muscle that affect metabolism, structure and energy supply” and that reduced blood flow restricts oxygen supply and hinders removal of metabolic waste [PAGE 4]. It also says, “Muscle contractions increase arterial blood pressure, which in turn reduces blood flow to active muscles” [PAGE 4]. This lineage is important because MNF is not a direct fear sensor. It is a physiological signal shaped by force, ischemia, motor-unit recruitment, electrode placement, and time-on-task.

The second stream is climbing psychology. Prior work compared lead and top rope climbing, showing that lead climbing can impose higher cognitive and somatic stress in intermediate climbers. The source cites Hodgson et al. and Draper et al. to establish that ascent style changes psychophysiological demand [PAGE 5]. It also cites Pijpers et al. for the effect of height, noting that higher traverses produced more muscle fatigue, higher blood lactate, higher heart rates, longer climbing times, less smooth motion, more explosive movements, and slower prolonged grasps [PAGE 5]. This historical lineage matters because the present study’s “interval” variable is a descendant of the height-effect literature. The paper’s finding that later intervals and lead style increase fear is consistent with earlier work, but it inherits the same ambiguity: height, fatigue, task difficulty, and perceived risk are entangled.

The third stream is statistical learning. The mixed-effects lineage comes from longitudinal biostatistics, especially Laird and Ware, where clustered observations are modeled with fixed population effects and random subject effects. The source explicitly uses this framework to account for individual differences in “climbing experience, mental resilience, and physical capacity” [PAGE 2]. The deep-learning random-effects lineage comes from Simchoni and Rosset and related work, where random effects are inserted into neural objectives through a covariance-aware loss [PAGE 3, PAGE 11]. This paper contributes a PyTorch layer and loss for random effects [PAGE 3]. The genealogy therefore moves from subject-specific longitudinal modeling to personalized deep learning.

Multi-week traps arise when this lineage is extended beyond the reported protocol. The source describes a within-participant experiment with rest between trials and pseudorandomized order [PAGE 8]. It does not establish a multi-week training effect. If the same climbers were studied over multiple weeks, several traps would appear. First, route familiarity would reduce uncertainty independent of fitness. Second, accumulated forearm fatigue could shift MNF baselines. Third, electrode placement could drift due to skin changes or sensor wear. Fourth, psychological adaptation could reduce fear even if physiological effort remains high. Fifth, self-report recall could become more stable or more biased depending on repeated exposure. None of these can be assumed away. The paper’s evidence supports acute within-session comparisons, not long-term causal adaptation.

A further genealogical trap is the conflation of experience and ability. The source notes that “a recent review highlights the importance of not conflating between experience level and climbing ability when examining differences in climbing ascent style” [PAGE 5]. This matters because the random-effects model may absorb expertise as a participant intercept, while the fixed-effect model may treat expertise as a linear covariate. If experience is nonlinear, if ability is categorical, or if risk perception changes with redpoint grade, then the random effect may be doing more than nuisance adjustment. It may be capturing a latent skill phenotype. The model’s apparent personalization may therefore reflect unobserved competence, not merely fear physiology.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory of mind for this dataset must model the climber as an agent who anticipates falling, evaluates consequence severity, regulates motor output, and later narrates that experience. The lead climber knows that a fall will be longer because protection is clipped during ascent. The source says lead climbers “must manage and secure the rope to points of protection along the route” and therefore face “variable and often longer fall distances” [PAGE 2]. A rational fear model should therefore increase expected loss when a clip is difficult, when the climber is pumped, or when the next protection point is far away. This is not a simple physiological reflex; it is a belief about future states.

Habit regression occurs when a climber under crisis reverts to overlearned movement patterns. Under fear, an intermediate climber may overgrip, climb more statically, breathe shallowly, or rush clipping. These changes alter EMG amplitude and MNF. The model may then interpret the physiological signature of fear-induced motor strategy as generic fatigue. This is a recursive trap: the model sees fatigue, but the fatigue is partly a consequence of fear regulation. The source notes that higher traverses elicited “less smooth motion, more explosive movements, and slower, prolonged grasps” in earlier work [PAGE 5]. Those are not pure metabolic markers; they are behavioral expressions of threat appraisal.

Counter-trap behavior occurs when the climber tries to suppress fear by increasing control. This can reduce subjective fear in the short term but increase physiological cost. The climber may slow down, test holds, or clip cautiously. The post-climb report may then understate fear because the climber successfully regulated it. If the model only sees the physiological cost, it may overestimate fear relative to the climber’s narrated state. Conversely, if the climber suppresses reporting due to self-image, the model may underestimate fear relative to the physiological signal. The paper’s retrospective questionnaire is vulnerable to this because it asks for a summary after the climb, not an online readout.

The clock variable is time-on-wall and fatigue accumulation. The source includes interval as a categorical fixed effect and finds later intervals associated with higher fear [PAGE 16]. This is consistent with both height and time-on-task. A recursive model should treat the clock as an ambiguous state variable: later intervals may mean greater height, greater cumulative fatigue, greater route uncertainty, or simply greater exposure duration. Without separating these, the model may attribute to fear what is actually time-dependent physiological drift. The random-effects GRU may learn that later windows have different label distributions, but that does not prove it understands fear.

The deepest recursive problem is that the label itself is a theory of the climber’s past state. When the climber watches the video and rates fear by quarter, they reconstruct an emotional narrative. The source says, “The video was paused after each quarter of the route as defined by equidistant points on the wall” [PAGE 8]. That design helps localize the label to a segment, but it still relies on memory. If the climber remembers the hardest clip as more frightening than it was in the moment, the physiological features before that clip may be assigned a fear label that reflects retrospective narrative coherence. The model then learns narrative regularization, not pure fear physiology. This is the core recursive trap: the model must infer a hidden affective state from behavior that was itself shaped by the agent’s beliefs about future falling, effort, and self-presentation.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer can veto a pure fear interpretation of MNF because MNF is not specific to emotion. The source states that fatigue involves metabolism, structure, energy supply, reduced oxygen, nutrient limitation, and accumulation of metabolic waste [PAGE 4]. It also says that increased acidity reduces the speed at which electrical signals travel through muscle [PAGE 4]. Those mechanisms affect the EMG spectrum regardless of whether the climber is afraid. Therefore, if a model predicts fear from MNF without controlling for force output, contraction duration, blood flow, electrode shift, or movement artifact, the physical layer can veto the inference as non-identifiable. The model may be tracking metabolic fatigue, not fear.

Formally, let \(F\) be fatigue-related MNF, \(A\) be fear, and \(C\) be mechanical and metabolic covariates such as force, time under tension, occlusion, and electrode contact quality. If \(C \rightarrow F\) and \(C \rightarrow A\) is possible because difficult climbing causes both fatigue and fear, then observing \(F \rightarrow A\) without conditioning on \(C\) is confounded. The causal graph contains a backdoor path through task difficulty. The paper’s mixed model includes style and interval, which partially proxy difficulty, but it does not include direct force or clip-event covariates. Therefore, the physical layer vetoes any claim that MNF is a pure fear biomarker rather than a task-dependent fatigue correlate.

The biomechanical layer can veto a pure ascent-style interpretation because lead and top rope are not identical motor tasks. The source says, “During the lead climbing trials, the climbers clipped the rope into quickdraws along the route” and, for top rope, “participants were required to unclip the rope from the quickdraws as they ascended to match the challenge of clipping the rope in the lead ascent style” [PAGE 8]. This matching is thoughtful, but it does not make the tasks identical. Lead clipping involves reaching for protection, stabilizing the body, managing rope tension, and making a decision about fall consequence. Top rope unclipping may involve different timing, different visual attention, and different safety semantics. If arm acceleration, hand trajectory, or pause duration differs systematically, then the IMU and EMG features may encode task kinematics rather than fear. The biomechanical layer therefore vetoes any model that claims to isolate fear from ascent style without measuring and adjusting for clipping kinematics.

A simple veto proof is as follows. Suppose the observed association is \( \text{lead} \rightarrow \text{higher EMG fatigue} \rightarrow \text{higher fear} \). If lead also causes \( \text{clipping kinematics} \rightarrow \text{higher EMG fatigue} \) and \( \text{clipping kinematics} \rightarrow \text{higher fear} \), then the estimated effect of fatigue on fear is not the total effect of fear physiology. It is a mixture of fatigue, task kinematics, and risk appraisal. Without measuring clip events, the model cannot separate these paths. The source’s own feature set includes wrist acceleration, but acceleration is not used as a covariate in the linear mixed model’s core interaction test. Therefore, the biomechanical layer has sufficient grounds to veto a strong causal interpretation.

The weather layer is mostly a veto against overgeneralization to outdoor climbing. The experiment took place on a “19.3 m high indoor climbing wall” [PAGE 2]. Weather is not a direct causal variable in an indoor controlled environment unless it enters through proxies such as temperature, humidity, crowding, commute stress, or footwear friction. If a future model claims that weather explains fear in this dataset, the weather layer can veto it unless the dataset contains outdoor sessions or measured indoor climate covariates. In the present source, weather is not a measured variable. Therefore, any weather-based prediction would be an external extrapolation, not a finding from the supplied pages.

A proof by conditional independence can be stated informally. Let \(W\) be weather, \(I\) be indoor climbing environment, \(P\) be participant physiology, and \(R\) be reported fear. If all trials occur indoors and \(W\) does not influence \(I\) or \(P\) except through unmeasured proxies, then \(W\) is d-separated from \(R\) given \(I\) and \(P\). In that case, the population coefficient of \(W\) in a correctly specified model should be zero. A finite-sample nonzero estimate may appear due to small sample size or confounding, but it should not be interpreted as evidence of a weather effect. The cross-layer adjudication therefore concludes: physical mechanisms allow fatigue to correlate with fear, biomechanical task differences prevent clean attribution to ascent style alone, and weather cannot be treated as a causal factor in this indoor protocol.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto Scenario 1: Leave-one-climber-out personalization veto. The source’s split is not a strict new-participant test because “we randomly added one ascent style for every climber in the training set” [PAGE 13]. If the random-effects GRU improves mainly because it recognizes the same climbers across training and test, then a strict leave-one-climber-out evaluation should reduce the advantage. Verifiable test: retrain with no participant overlap between training and test, using either population-only fixed effects or random effects estimated only from training participants and applied to a new participant with no climb-specific observations. Prediction: the random-effects GRU advantage over the standard GRU should shrink substantially. In the source, RMSE improves from 3.34 to 2.49, an improvement of 0.85 [PAGE 20]. Under strict leave-one-climber-out evaluation, if personalization is genuine rather than memorization, the advantage should remain positive but smaller; if memorization dominates, the advantage should approach zero or become negative.

Veto Scenario 2: Real-time label veto. The source admits that post-climb reports are “subject to memory bias” and cannot “fully capture the dynamic nature of the psychological states during climbing” [PAGE 23]. If the current model is learning retrospective narrative smoothing rather than online fear, then collecting real-time fear reports during the climb should change the learned mapping. Verifiable test: compare models trained on retrospective quarter labels against models trained on real-time button-press or continuous dial labels time-aligned to the same physiological windows. Prediction: the MNF-by-lead association should become stronger, more temporally localized, and less dependent on interval-level post-hoc aggregation. If the real-time labels do not improve the temporal alignment between MNF decreases and fear reports, then the retrospective label is the dominant information source and the current model’s fear interpretation is weakened.

Veto Scenario 3: Clipping-kinematics confound veto. The source states that lead climbing requires clipping quickdraws, while top rope requires unclipping to match the challenge [PAGE 8]. If the lead-specific MNF interaction is driven by clipping movement rather than fear, then adding clip-event covariates and kinematic controls should attenuate the interaction. Verifiable test: extend the mixed model with clip count, clip duration, wrist acceleration, pause duration, and movement smoothness, then re-estimate the MNF-by-lead interaction. Prediction: the absolute value of the MNF-by-lead coefficient should decrease by a meaningful margin, and the significance should weaken. The source reports an MNF-by-style estimate of -0.089 with p = .019 [PAGE 16]. If after kinematic adjustment the estimate becomes closer to zero and loses significance, the fear-specific interpretation is vetoed. If it remains stable, the fear interpretation survives a stronger biomechanical test.

These three vetoes are counterfactual because each one asks: if the true mechanism is X, then a measurable model behavior must change in a specified direction. They are RLVR-compatible because each can be operationalized as a reward or validation metric: predictive likelihood under strict participant holdout, temporal alignment with real-time labels, and coefficient stability under kinematic controls. A model that passes all three would be stronger evidence for a fear-fatigue relationship. A model that fails them should be treated as a predictive artifact or a task-confounded correlate rather than a validated psychophysiological fear model.
