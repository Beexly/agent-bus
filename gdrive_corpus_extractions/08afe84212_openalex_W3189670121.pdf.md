The paper’s central problem is to invert a monocular sports video into both a dense 3D SMPL motion sequence and a discrete semantic action description. The multi-hop reasoning chain is: (1) detect/segment the athlete’s body and classify the current sub-motion; (2) map the frame into a low-dimensional PCA pose-coefficient space learned from marker-based motion capture; (3) decode SMPL pose/shape and project to 2D joints; (4) feed joints, bones, and pose coefficients into a multi-stream ST-GCN; (5) predict semantic attributes and assemble them into a final action label or score. The key gray-zone assumption is that sports sub-motions are semantically and temporally structured enough that a per-sub-motion PCA prior regularizes monocular depth ambiguity and severe self-occlusion. This is plausible for highly stereotyped movements such as diving or balance beam, but fragile for rare mistakes, novel transitions, severe clipping, or multi-person interactions.

A first derivation layer comes from [PAGE 5]: “we propose the pose coefficient α ... to leverage the rich semantic and temporally structural prior of sub-motions in the motion embedding space.” The embedding is explicitly linear in PCA coordinates: “θ = Mm(α)” and “θ = α⊺Bm + am.” Thus the network does not regress arbitrary SMPL rotations directly; it regresses coefficients in a subspace constrained by mocap statistics. This reduces effective degrees of freedom and encodes semantic structure, but it also creates a representational bottleneck: any pose not well spanned by the chosen basis is systematically pulled toward the sub-motion manifold.

A second layer is temporal segmentation. SportsCap assumes each complete action can be split into sub-motions such as take-off, twist, somersault, and entry for diving [PAGE 9][PAGE 10]. The segmentation classifier is WS-DAN [PAGE 5][PAGE 14]. If segmentation fails, the wrong PCA basis is applied, and the recovered pose may be plausible under the wrong semantic stage. This is a recursive dependency: pose estimation depends on sub-motion classification, and action parsing depends on the resulting pose coefficients. Errors therefore propagate upward: misclassified sub-motion -> wrong basis -> wrong α -> wrong 3D joints -> wrong ST-GCN features -> wrong attributes.

A third layer is loss design. The motion embedding module uses prior loss on pose coefficients, data loss on projected 2D joints, and SMPL pose/shape loss [PAGE 8]. The action parsing module uses attribute cross-entropy and task cross-entropy [PAGE 8]. The multi-task coupling is claimed to improve both capture and understanding. Table 2 shows multi-task semantic embedding with ResNet152 achieving PCK-0.3 88.5 and PCK-0.5 96.0, while single-task semantic ResNet152 achieves 88.1/94.8 [PAGE 11]. The paper states this is “above 1.2% improvement on PCK-0.5 from this multi-task learning” [PAGE 12].

The gray-zone audit must examine failure modes. First, PCA truncation: if K is too small, valid sports poses are excluded; if K is too large, noise and rare motions enter. Figure 9 shows cumulative variance versus number of pose coefficients, but the exact operating point is dataset-dependent [PAGE 11]. Second, sub-motion granularity: diving has four sub-motions, boxing three, and other sports have different decompositions [PAGE 5][PAGE 9]. A too-coarse decomposition mixes distinct kinematics; a too-fine decomposition yields insufficient mocap samples per basis. Third, monocular orthographic projection discards depth, so fast twisting or somersaulting can be ambiguous even with semantic priors. Fourth, occlusion can remove the exact joints needed to disambiguate rotation. The paper explicitly lists failure cases where input does not fall into predefined pose categories, severe occlusion occurs, or images are clipped [PAGE 16].

The overall inference is that SportsCap is strong where sports motion is canonical, densely mocap-supervised, and semantically segmented. It is weak where the input violates the assumed sub-motion grammar, where temporal sampling aliases fast rotations, or where monocular depth ambiguity exceeds the prior’s resolving power.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let a video clip be \(X=\{x_t\}_{t=1}^{T}\), resampled to \(T=90\) frames in the reported experiments [PAGE 10]. Let \(m_t\) be the sub-motion label for frame \(t\). SportsCap learns a per-sub-motion linear pose embedding.

From [PAGE 5]:  
\[
\theta = M_m(\alpha),
\]
\[
\theta = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \alpha_k b^{(m)}_k + a_m = \alpha^\top B_m + a_m.
\]
Here \(\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}\) are SMPL pose parameters, \(N\) is the SMPL joint number, \(K\) is the number of pose coefficients, \(B_m\in\mathbb{R}^{3N\times K}\) is the PCA basis for sub-motion \(m\), and \(a_m\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}\) is the mean pose. Units: SMPL rotation-vector components are typically radians; \(\alpha\) is dimensionless if \(B_m\) carries radian units; \(a_m\) has the same units as \(\theta\).

From [PAGE 6]:  
\[
\alpha(x) = F^m_{\text{conv}}(x; W),
\]
\[
J(x) = \alpha(x)^\top B_m + a_m,
\]
where \(F^m_{\text{conv}}\) is a ResNet-152 encoder followed by fully connected layers [PAGE 6]. The network can regress pose coefficients, shape parameters, and camera parameters [PAGE 6].

The PCA basis is obtained from mocap poses \(\{\theta_i\}\) by computing the covariance and selecting principal directions. Formally, for sub-motion \(m\),
\[
C_m = \frac{1}{M_m}\sum_{i=1}^{M_m}(\theta_i-a_m)(\theta_i-a_m)^\top,
\]
\[
C_m v_k = \lambda_k v_k,
\quad b^{(m)}_k = v_k.
\]
Singularity regime: if \(M_m < \operatorname{rank}(C_m)\) or the sub-motion contains insufficient variation, some \(\lambda_k\approx 0\), making high-\(K\) coefficients unidentifiable and amplifying noise.

The motion embedding losses are given in [PAGE 8]:

Prior loss:
\[
L_{\text{prior}} = \|W(\alpha - \hat{\alpha})\|_2^2,
\]
where \(\alpha\) is the training-set mean coefficient vector, \(\hat{\alpha}\) is predicted, and \(W\) is computed from covariance eigenvalues. The paper states: “We use the smaller weight for the larger eigenvalue to enable more tolerance on principal components” [PAGE 8]. A formal choice consistent with this is \(W=\operatorname{diag}(w_1,\dots,w_K)\) with \(w_k\) nonincreasing in \(\lambda_k\), e.g. \(w_k\propto \lambda_k^{-\gamma}\), \(\gamma>0\), but the exact weighting is not fully specified.

Data loss:
\[
\hat{J} = \hat{s}\,\Pi\!\left(J(M_m(\hat{\alpha}),\hat{\beta})\right)+\hat{t},
\]
\[
L_{\text{data}} = \|V(J-\hat{J})\|_2^2.
\]
Here \(J\) are ground-truth 2D joints, \(V\) is visibility, \(\hat{s}\in\mathbb{R}_+\) is weak-perspective scale, \(\hat{t}\in\mathbb{R}^2\) is translation, and \(\Pi(\cdot)\) is orthographic projection [PAGE 8]. Units: \(J,\hat{J},\hat{t}\) are pixels; \(\hat{s}\) is dimensionless if joint coordinates are normalized.

SMPL loss:
\[
L_{\text{smpl}} = \|\theta-\hat{\theta}\|_2^2 + \|\beta-\hat{\beta}\|_2^2.
\]

Combined motion loss:
\[
L_{\text{mem}} = L_{\text{prior}} + \lambda_{\text{data}}L_{\text{data}} + \lambda_{\text{smpl}}L_{\text{smpl}},
\]
with \(\lambda_{\text{data}}=10\), \(\lambda_{\text{smpl}}=2\) [PAGE 8].

Action parsing uses J-stream, B-stream, and P-stream features [PAGE 7]. The J-stream maps joints to graph nodes and bones to edges; the B-stream reverses this mapping. The P-stream uses 1D convolution over pose coefficients, producing 256-dimensional features [PAGE 7]. The concatenated features enter the Semantic Attributes Mapping Block.

Attribute cross-entropy:
\[
L_{\text{attr}} = \sum_{c=1}^{N_s}\sum_{i=1}^{N_c} y^c_i \log(x^c_i),
\]
where \(c\) indexes attribute type, \(N_s\) is number of attributes, \(N_c\) is number of categories for attribute \(c\), \(x^c_i\) is predicted probability, and \(y^c_i\) is ground truth [PAGE 8]. Breakdown regime: if \(x^c_i=0\) while \(y^c_i=1\), \(\log(0)=-\infty\); numerical clipping or label smoothing is required.

Task loss:
\[
L_{\text{task}} = \sum_{j=1}^{N_f} y_j \log(x_j),
\]
with \(N_f\) total final action labels [PAGE 8].

Action parsing loss:
\[
L_{\text{apm}} = L_{\text{attr}} + \lambda_A L_{\text{task}},
\]
with \(\lambda_A=2\) [PAGE 8].

Evaluation uses PCK-0.3 and PCK-0.5, defined by distance error less than 0.3 or 0.5 torso lengths, respectively [PAGE 11]. Action parsing uses Top-1 accuracy [PAGE 15]. Action assessment uses Spearman’s rank correlation [PAGE 16].

Critical singularity/breakdown regimes:
1. Wrong sub-motion classification: if \(\hat{m}_t\neq m_t\), then \(B_{\hat{m}}\) and \(a_{\hat{m}}\) are wrong, producing biased \(\theta\).
2. Orthographic ambiguity: \(\Pi(\cdot)\) removes depth; two 3D poses with different depth can project identically, so \(L_{\text{data}}\) alone cannot disambiguate them.
3. Invisible joints: if \(V_i=0\), that joint contributes no data-loss gradient, leaving related pose coefficients underconstrained.
4. PCA rank deficiency: if mocap samples per sub-motion are too few, \(C_m\) is singular and high-frequency pose directions are unstable.
5. Temporal aliasing: fixed 90-frame windows may under-sample fast twists or over-sample slow entries, depending on original frame rate.
6. Semantic attribute collapse: without structural mapping, the network may overfocus on one attribute, as observed when somersault accuracy drops in ablations [PAGE 15].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
SportsCap sits on a lineage of parametric human modeling, deep pose regression, skeleton action recognition, and fine-grained sports datasets. The parametric body-model lineage begins with SCAPE [PAGE 3], then SMPL [PAGE 3][PAGE 5], and later SMPL-X [PAGE 3]. SportsCap adopts SMPL pose parameters \(\theta\) as the underlying representation because SMPL provides a compact kinematic body prior and compatibility with mesh rendering [PAGE 5]. The paper also acknowledges MoSh for converting motion capture to SMPL-compatible parameters [PAGE 8].

The deep pose-regression lineage includes HMR, which regresses SMPL pose and shape end-to-end from a single image using an adversarial prior [PAGE 3], and VIBE, which adds temporal adversarial motion discrimination using AMASS [PAGE 3]. SportsCap compares against both: “We ﬁne-tune HMR ... and VIBE ... with our dataset” [PAGE 12]. Table 3 shows HMR and VIBE degrade sharply on challenging sub-motions such as twisting and turning, while SportsCap maintains higher PCK [PAGE 12]. The historical trap here is that general-purpose pose networks implicitly encode everyday pose regularity; sports poses are non-daily, fast, and self-occluding, so their learned priors fail precisely when motion is most extreme.

The skeleton-action lineage includes OpenPose part-affinity fields [PAGE 3], ST-GCN spatial-temporal graph convolution [PAGE 3], and two-stream adaptive graph convolution [PAGE 7]. SportsCap explicitly adopts OpenPose joint/bone conventions and the 2s-AGCN structure for J- and B-streams [PAGE 7]. The retro-causal contribution is to add a P-stream of pose coefficients, arguing that PCA coefficients encode semantic meaning such as somersault count [PAGE 7]. Figure 5 claims that the first two principal components reveal half-somersault rounds: “left: 4 × 2 −1 = 7 rounds; right: 3 × 2 −1 = 5 rounds” [PAGE 7].

The sports-understanding lineage includes Diving48, FineGym, MTL-AQA, and action quality assessment methods [PAGE 4][PAGE 8]. SportsCap positions its SMART dataset as more complete because it includes per-frame action labels, 2D and 3D annotated poses, MoCap pose spaces, and referee assessments [PAGE 4][PAGE 9]. This is historically important because prior sports datasets often provide labels but not dense 3D pose supervision.

Multi-week traps in reproducing or extending this system:
1. Mocap-to-SMPL conversion. The authors convert Vicon skeleton results to SMPL pose parameters to avoid absolute bone-length variation [PAGE 10]. A multi-week trap is marker-to-joint registration error; small marker placement errors can bias PCA bases.
2. Sub-motion definition. Diving has take-off, twist, somersault, entry; boxing has punching, kicking, dodging; other sports have different stages [PAGE 5][PAGE 9]. Defining sub-motions that are semantically meaningful and statistically sufficient requires iterative annotation and cross-validation. The paper says annotations were manually generated with professional cross-validation between more than two individuals [PAGE 9].
3. Dataset scale and balance. SMART has 640 videos/110K frames in one table row, but also reports about 450,000 annotated skeletons and more than 500,000 motion frames of 30 performers [PAGE 9][PAGE 10]. The inconsistency in aggregation granularity is itself a reproduction trap: one must know whether counts refer to clips, frames, skeletons, or mocap frames.
4. Temporal resampling. Videos are resampled to 90 frames [PAGE 10]. If original clips vary in duration, fast twists may be undersampled while slow entries are oversampled.
5. Training schedule. The paper uses stage-wise training: first motion embedding per sub-motion, then action parsing, then fine-tuning the whole network [PAGE 8]. Reproducing convergence behavior requires matching learning rates: \(10^{-3}\), \(10^{-4}\), \(10^{-5}\) at epochs 0, 70, 150 [PAGE 11].
6. Semantic attribute taxonomy. Diving attributes include take-off, twisting number, somersault number, arm-stand, and position [PAGE 6][PAGE 10]. Errors in attribute taxonomy propagate directly into final action-number prediction.

The genealogical lesson is that SportsCap’s performance is not merely a network result; it is a dataset-and-prior co-design. The historical prior that matters most is the assumption that sports motion, despite being non-daily, is still highly regular within sub-motions. If that assumption fails, the lineage from SMPL to sports-specific PCA becomes a liability rather than a benefit.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind reading treats SportsCap as a predictor that models the athlete’s current sub-motion, while simultaneously modeling the judge’s or coach’s semantic interpretation of the action. The model’s internal “belief” about the athlete is not raw pose; it is a latent sub-motion-conditioned pose manifold. This creates three interacting regimes: crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock.

Crisis habit regression occurs when evidence is weak. If the input image is severely occluded, clipped, or outside the predefined pose categories, the model falls back on the learned prior. The paper’s failure cases explicitly include inputs that do not fall into predefined pose categories, severe occlusion, and incomplete clipping [PAGE 16]. In those cases, the posterior over \(\alpha\) is dominated by the prior \(a_m\) and the strongest PCA directions. The model does not necessarily output “unknown”; it outputs the nearest plausible sub-motion. This is a form of regression to the training distribution: under uncertainty, the system behaves as if the athlete is performing a canonical version of the closest known move. This can be useful for denoising, but dangerous for rare mistakes. The paper admits: “when an athlete makes rare mistakes, it is difﬁcult for our method to detect and analyze the situation accurately” [PAGE 16].

Counter-trap occurs when the observed motion intentionally or accidentally violates the assumed sub-motion grammar. Examples include unusual transitions, body parts outside the viewport, team-sport interactions, or movements not covered by the nine sports in the embedding spaces [PAGE 10][PAGE 16]. Because the model’s pose prior is semantic, a counter-trap can force the model to choose between two failures: assign the motion to the wrong sub-motion, or keep the correct sub-motion but produce an out-of-manifold pose. The first failure corrupts the basis \(B_m\); the second failure produces a pose that is too close to the mean and loses the athlete’s actual dynamics. The recursive aspect is that the sub-motion classifier expects canonical stages, while the pose estimator expects the classifier to be correct, and the action parser expects both to be correct. A single counter-trap can cascade across all three levels.

Clock refers to temporal resolution and temporal segmentation. The model receives 90 frames per clip [PAGE 10]. The ST-GCN uses 90 consecutive frames of skeletons [PAGE 7]. If the original video frame rate is low relative to the speed of twisting or flipping, the clock undersamples rotation. If the original video is long and compressed to 90 frames, slow phases may be overrepresented. The semantic attribute “rotation 720 degree” in Fig. 1 [PAGE 2] requires integrating angular change over time. If temporal sampling aliases the rotation, the model may count the wrong number of half-somersaults. The paper’s somersault-count example uses curves of principal components over frame index to infer rounds [PAGE 7], which makes the prediction explicitly clock-dependent.

The recursive mind layer also includes the evaluator. If SportsCap is used for motion scoring, athletes and coaches may adapt their movement presentation to the model’s known priors: cleaner lines, more canonical sub-motion boundaries, clearer silhouettes. This changes the data distribution over time. A model trained on canonical competition footage may later face non-canonical tactical performances. The “mind” being modeled is therefore not only the athlete’s body but also the institutional incentive structure around scoring. The counter-trap is not merely adversarial; it can be rational adaptation to the evaluation system.

Thus, SportsCap’s strongest regime is canonical, well-lit, single-person, indoor or controlled sports with strong sub-motion priors. Its crisis regime is degraded evidence, where it regresses to prior poses. Its counter-trap regime is non-canonical or rare motion, where semantic priors become brittle. Its clock regime is temporally compressed or undersampled fast rotation, where attribute counting becomes uncertain.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer veto adjudication asks whether physical, biomechanical, or environmental constraints can override the model’s learned prediction. SportsCap itself does not include an explicit physics engine, but several equations define natural veto conditions.

First, skeletal length constraints. SMPL pose parameters \(\theta\) define joint locations through a kinematic chain. For a valid body, distances between connected joints must remain near fixed rest lengths, modulated only by shape \(\beta\). Let \(J_i\) and \(J_j\) be 3D joints. A basic veto condition is
\[
\left|\,\|J_i-J_j\| - \ell_{ij}(\beta)\,\right| > \epsilon_{ij},
\]
where \(\ell_{ij}(\beta)\) is the SMPL-implied segment length and \(\epsilon_{ij}\) is a tolerance. If the decoded pose violates this beyond tolerance, the estimate is physically implausible. Because SportsCap uses SMPL and MoSh supervision [PAGE 8], gross skeletal-length violations are less likely than semantic or depth errors, but severe shape regression errors can still break proportions.

Second, joint-limit constraints. Human joints have approximate anatomical ranges. For joint \(q\), a valid pose should satisfy
\[
q \in [q_{\min}, q_{\max}].
\]
If PCA coefficients produce a pose outside these ranges, the estimate should be vetoed or projected back. The paper’s PCA prior reduces but does not guarantee anatomical validity, especially if mocap contains extreme but legal poses.

Third, airborne dynamics. In diving, gymnastics, or high jump, there are phases where the body is airborne. If no contact force acts and aerodynamic forces are neglected, the center of mass obeys
\[
\ddot{r}_{\text{COM}} = g,
\]
with \(g\approx 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2\) downward. A veto condition is
\[
\left\|\ddot{r}_{\text{COM}}(t) - g\right\| > \epsilon_{\text{COM}}.
\]
SportsCap uses weak-perspective projection and does not estimate metric COM acceleration directly [PAGE 8]. However, if downstream 3D reconstruction is used, implausible hovering, sudden direction reversal without contact, or non-parabolic flight should be rejected.

Fourth, contact and support constraints. During take-off, landing, handspring, or balance-beam contact, the body must satisfy contact mechanics. If a foot or hand is labeled visible and in contact with the apparatus, its velocity relative to the apparatus should be near zero at contact:
\[
\|v_{\text{contact}}\| \leq \epsilon_v.
\]
If the model predicts sliding or penetration into the apparatus, that is a veto. The paper’s failure cases include severe occlusion and incomplete clipping [PAGE 16], which often coincide with contact ambiguity.

Fifth, weather and environment. SportsCap does not model wind, rain, or air density. For indoor diving or gymnastics, weather is negligible. For outdoor pole vault or high jump, wind can matter. The aerodynamic force scale is
\[
F_{\text{wind}} \sim \frac{1}{2}\rho C_d A v_{\text{wind}}^2,
\]
where \(\rho\) is air density, \(C_d\) drag coefficient, \(A\) frontal area, and \(v_{\text{wind}}\) wind speed. If the estimated airborne trajectory requires lateral acceleration that cannot be explained by observed body pose alone, and the video is outdoor, the system lacks a weather operator to adjudicate whether wind is responsible. Therefore, the correct cross-layer ruling is not to certify the prediction but to flag it as underdetermined. SportsCap’s input assumptions do not include environmental state, so weather-based veto can only be conservative: reject metric claims about aerodynamic stabilization unless external wind data is provided.

The strongest veto proof is therefore layer separation: SportsCap can estimate plausible poses within its learned semantic manifold, but it cannot alone certify physical realism in metric space. A robust deployment should run a physics validator after SportsCap: check skeletal lengths, joint limits, airborne COM parabola, contact velocities, and environmental metadata. If any check fails, the prediction should be downgraded to “semantically plausible but physically unverified.”
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are three verifiable RLVR-style counterfactual veto scenarios: interventions that, if they fail to produce the predicted degradation, should veto the claimed mechanism.

Scenario 1: Remove semantic sub-motion PCA prior and replace it with a general PCA pose space.
Paper evidence: Figure 9 compares semantic and general pose spaces. The text says: “under the same dimension of pose coefﬁcients, each semantic pose space’s pose spaces show better accuracy than the general pose space” [PAGE 11]. Table 2 also shows that removing \(L_{\text{prior}}\) causes “an approximately 5% drop in accuracy” [PAGE 11-12].
Prediction: On challenging sub-motions, especially SM-2 twisting and SM-4 entry, PCK-0.3 and PCK-0.5 should drop relative to semantic PCA. The drop should be largest where motion is fast and self-occluding.
Veto condition: If general PCA matches or exceeds semantic PCA across SM-1 to SM-4, the claim that sub-motion semantic priors are the main source of robustness is falsified.

Scenario 2: Replace the Semantic Attributes Mapping Block with a black-box final-label head.
Paper evidence: The SAMB ablations show “The one with Black-Box converges after over 30 epochs with 78.0% accuracy. In contrast, the network with SAMB converges after ten epochs with 82.2% accuracy” [PAGE 15]. Table 6 also shows Ours (J+B+P) SAMB achieving 82.2 on Diving No., while Black-Box achieves 78.0 [PAGE 15].
Prediction: Removing intermediate semantic attributes should reduce final action-label accuracy and slow convergence. Attribute-level supervision should also improve interpretability of errors.
Veto condition: If black-box training reaches equal or higher final accuracy with equal or faster convergence, the claimed benefit of explicit semantic attribute decomposition is not supported.

Scenario 3: Train the Motion Embedding Module without the Action Parsing Module, i.e., single-task instead of multi-task.
Paper evidence: Table 2 compares Multi vs Single with semantic ResNet152. Multi-task semantic ResNet152 obtains PCK-0.3 88.5 and PCK-0.5 96.0; single-task semantic ResNet152 obtains 88.1 and 94.8 [PAGE 11]. The text says there is “above 1.2% improvement on PCK-0.5 from this multi-task learning” [PAGE 12].
Prediction: Single-task motion embedding should show lower PCK-0.5, especially on hard sub-motions, because action parsing provides semantic gradient signal that regularizes pose estimation.
Veto condition: If single-task training matches multi-task PCK-0.5 within noise, the mutual-gain hypothesis between motion capture and fine-grained understanding is weakened.

Additional verifiable stress tests, though not required as the three primary vetoes:
1. Occlusion ablation: artificially hide torso or hip joints. Prediction: PCK drops sharply for twisting and entry sub-motions because \(V\) removes gradient for those joints [PAGE 8].
2. Temporal alias test: reduce effective sampling rate for fast somersaults. Prediction: somersault-number attribute accuracy drops because rotation cycles become under-sampled.
3. Cross-sport transfer test: train on one sport’s embedding space and test on another. Prediction: performance collapses unless the sub-motion pose manifolds are similar, because the model “once trained, only tackles speciﬁc sports rather than general movements” [PAGE 16].

The RLVR logic is that the paper’s mechanism claims must survive controlled removal of the proposed components. If the semantic PCA prior, SAMB, or multi-task coupling can be removed without measurable loss, then those components are not the true source of performance.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
