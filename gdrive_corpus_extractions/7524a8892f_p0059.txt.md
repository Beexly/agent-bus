This regression is cognitively economical: it allows the use of standard expected-value and portfolio formulas. But under Knight's framework, it is precisely in crisis conditions that the true law is least likely to be known, so the regression maximizes exposure exactly when uncertainty is highest.

\textbf{Counter-trap.} Knight's counter-trap is the recognition that any contract or model that pretends to price true uncertainty is itself speculative, not insurance. Writing a contract on an unknown distribution does not eliminate uncertainty; it transfers it to a counterparty who also does not know $P$. The apparent liquidity of such contracts is a trap: it creates the illusion that risk has been laid off when it has merely been renamed. The counter-trap is to refuse to act as if non-insurable uncertainty were insurable.

\textbf{Clock.} The operative clock is the interval between resource commitment and uncertainty resolution. Let $\tau$ denote the production or commitment horizon, measured in years. As $\tau$ lengthens, the set of potential states $\theta_{t}$ that can arise before resolution expands, and the probability that at least one state is unforeseen approaches one. Profit, in Knight's account, is the residual earned by the agent who holds the position across this clock and survives the resolution. A short clock admits frequency-based pricing; a long clock under structural novelty forces judgment. The theory of mind is therefore recursive: the entrepreneur must model not only the uncertainty of the world but also the fact that other entrepreneurs are regressing into risk-based habits, creating profit opportunities for those who do not.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
\textbf{Physical layer (capital irreversibility).} Once specific capital—plant, equipment, location—is erected, it cannot be costlessly repurposed. This physical irreversibility vetoes flexible adjustment when the realized state $\theta_{t}$ diverges from the ex ante forecast. Because the capital is specific to a particular product or market, the entrepreneur cannot arbitrage away an uncertain loss by switching uses. This layer is veto-proof: no financial contract restores the sunk, specific capital.

\textbf{Biomechanical layer (cognitive bounds).} The entrepreneur cannot enumerate the full state space $\Omega$ or compute $P$ over novel states. Working memory, attention, and foresight are bounded biological capacities. This bounds the agent's ability to form even a subjective probability distribution over all relevant contingencies. The veto is physiological: no amount of computational assistance removes the fundamental problem that the relevant states may not have been conceivable at the time of commitment.

\textbf{Weather/exogenous layer (aggregate shocks).} Weather-driven yield, energy, or transport shocks are correlated across agents within a region, so they cannot be diversified away through pooling. When the distribution of such shocks is itself non-stationary or unknown, no actuarial premium can be derived. The shock vetoes the production plan directly: crops fail, deliveries are delayed, and output is destroyed, independent of any price signal.

\textbf{Adjudication.} These three layers are cross-checked against each other. Physical irreversibility makes the cost of error real; cognitive bounds make the error unavoidable; aggregate weather shocks make the error systemic and non-poolable. Because the constraints are rooted in thermodynamics, biology, and atmospheric physics rather than in contractual conventions, they are veto-proof: no market mechanism, regulation, or financial innovation can eliminate them when the underlying probability is unknowable. Knight's theory locates profit precisely at the intersection where these vetoes bind and insurance withdraws.
