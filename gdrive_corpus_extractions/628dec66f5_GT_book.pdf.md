The source’s primitive operator is the strategic-form game-frame:
\[
G^{0}=\langle I,(S_i)_{i\in I},O,f\rangle,\qquad S=\prod_{i\in I}S_i,\qquad f:S\to O,
\]
where \(I=\{1,\dots,n\}\) is the player set, \(S_i\) is player i’s strategy set, \(O\) is the outcome set, and \(f\) maps a profile \(s=(s_1,\dots,s_n)\) to an outcome [PAGE 21]. Units: \(S_i\) is a set, \(O\) is a set, \(f\) is a function; no numeric unit is attached until preferences are added. A game adds rankings or utilities \(\pi_i:O\to\mathbb R\) or \(U_i:O\to\mathbb R\) [PAGE 23]. Ordinal utilities are order indices; cardinal von Neumann-Morgenstern utilities represent lottery preferences and are unique up to positive affine transformation [PAGE 183]. Units for money lotteries may be dollars, but the utility index itself is a numerical representation, not a physical quantity.

Strict dominance is:
\[
\forall s_{-i}\in S_{-i},\quad \pi_i(a,s_{-i})>\pi_i(b,s_{-i}),
\]
and weak dominance requires \(\ge\) everywhere plus strict inequality somewhere [PAGE 27]. A strictly dominant strategy is unique; weakly dominant strategies may be multiple and equivalent [PAGE 29]. Nash equilibrium for n players is:
\[
\pi_i(s^{*})\ge \pi_i(s_1^{*},\dots,s_{i-1}^{*},s_i,s_{i+1}^{*},\dots,s_n^{*})
\quad\forall s_i\in S_i,\ \forall i\in I
\]
[PAGE 43]. The inclusion \(NE(G)\subseteq IDSDS(G)\) holds for ordinal strategic-form games [PAGE 43]. Ordinal games need not have a pure Nash equilibrium; Matching Pennies is the source’s example [PAGE 45]. For finite cardinal games, the mixed-strategy extension is:
\[
\Pi_i(\sigma)=\sum_{s\in S}\sigma(s)\pi_i(s),\qquad \sigma\in\prod_{j\in I}\Delta(S_j),
\]
with \(\Delta(S_j)\) the probability simplex over pure strategies [PAGE 209]. The indifference principle requires, for any two pure strategies \(s_i,s_i'\) in the support of \(\sigma_i^{*}\),
\[
\Pi_i(s_i,\sigma_{-i}^{*})=\Pi_i(s_i',\sigma_{-i}^{*})=\pi_i^{*}
\]
[PAGE 213]. This is necessary, not sufficient, for equilibrium.

For Cournot competition the source derives:
\[
\pi_1(q_1,q_2)=(a-c)q_1-bq_1^2-bq_1q_2,\qquad
\frac{\partial\pi_1}{\partial q_1}=a-c-2bq_1-bq_2=0,
\]
with symmetric solution \(q_1=q_2=(a-c)/(3b)\) [PAGE 47]. Units: quantities are output units; \(a,b,c\) are positive constants in the inverse-demand and cost specification; profits are in the same monetary unit as \(a-c\).

Expected utility is:
\[
E[U(L)]=\sum_{k=1}^{m}p_kU(o_k),\qquad 0\le p_k\le1,\quad \sum_{k=1}^{m}p_k=1,
\]
for simple lotteries over basic outcomes [PAGE 179]. Conditional probability and Bayes’ rule are:
\[
P(E|F)=\frac{P(E\cap F)}{P(F)},\qquad P(F)>0,
\]
[PAGE 321]. Bayesian updating at a reached information set \(H\) is:
\[
\mu(x)=\frac{P_{root,\sigma}(x)}{P_{root,\sigma}(H)}
\]
for each \(x\in H\) with \(P_{root,\sigma}(H)>0\) [PAGE 413]. An assessment is \((\sigma,\mu)\), where \(\sigma\) is a behavioral-strategy profile and \(\mu\) is a belief system over nodes in each information set [PAGE 405]. Sequential rationality requires each player’s strategy to maximize expected payoff at every information set given \(\mu\) [PAGE 407]. Sequential equilibrium requires consistency plus sequential rationality [PAGE 449]. Consistency means there is a sequence of completely mixed strategy profiles \(\langle\sigma^n\rangle\) with \(\lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma^n=\sigma\), and associated Bayesian belief systems \(\mu^n\) with \(\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu^n=\mu\) [PAGE 445].

\]
where \(I^{CK}\) is the common-knowledge partition [PAGE 283]. Harsanyi consistency/common prior requires a probability measure \(P\) such that for every player i and state s,

P(I_i(s))>0,\qquad P(\{s'\}|I_i(s))=p_{i,s}(s')\quad\forall s'\in I_i(s)
\]
[PAGE 333]. The agreement theorem says that if a common prior exists and the event that the two agents hold probabilities p and q is common knowledge, then p = q [PAGE 337].
