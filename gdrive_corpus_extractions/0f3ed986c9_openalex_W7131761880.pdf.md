<THINK_TRACE>
The thesis can be reconstructed as a control pipeline whose central invariant is that an advertisement must remain registered to a four-vertex fence-board quadrilateral while foreground humans are preserved above the inserted pixels. The source establishes the priority directly: “Detection and localization of the fence board in sports videos: This is the highest priority, as the fence board is the platform for advertisement placement in this study.” [PAGE 15]. From this premise, the derivation proceeds in layers.

First, the fence board is not merely classified; it must be localized as a quadrilateral because the advertisement is pasted through a perspective transform. The selected detector is Key-point RCNN-PF, described as extending Faster RCNN with a Picture Frame branch that predicts four vertices for each ROI [PAGE 26]. This choice creates an immediate geometric dependency: if any of the four vertices is poorly estimated, the homography used for pasting becomes distorted. The thesis confirms this dependency when it reports that Key-point RCNN-PF can fail under brightness or colour changes: “factors such as scene brightness or alterations in the image's colour may have played a role in this subpar performance.” [PAGE 39]. Therefore, the first gray zone is not only detection accuracy but vertex-level accuracy under illumination and colour variation.

Second, because running Key-point RCNN-PF on every frame would produce frame-to-frame jitter, the initial integration model detects at sparse intervals and uses FlowNet to propagate the keypoints. The thesis explicitly states the schedule: “every 1st, 60th, 120th ,180th, etc.” [PAGE 32]. At thirty frames per second, a sixty-frame interval corresponds to two seconds of propagation without a fresh detector observation. This creates a second gray zone: the optical-flow update is only valid while the motion field remains locally coherent and while the fence board remains visible. The source acknowledges the opposite failure mode: if a bad rectangle is produced, “The consequence of this is the continuous display of a distorted ad for a significant duration” [PAGE 52]. Thus, the system is caught between high-frequency detection, which creates oscillation, and low-frequency detection, which locks in errors.

Third, scene changes break the temporal continuity assumption of optical flow. The thesis names this failure explicitly: “This peculiar occurrence is referred to as the Scene Change Issue within the context of this thesis.” [PAGE 53]. The remedy is PySceneDetect, which forces Key-point RCNN-PF re-detection when a cut is detected. This introduces a third gray zone: scene detection is itself a thresholded estimator, and false negatives leave the advertisement floating over a new scene, while false positives unnecessarily suspend replacement.

Fourth, human occlusion requires foreground restoration. MODNet predicts an alpha matte, and the pipeline thresholds the mask before pasting human pixels back. The thesis reports the operational threshold: “the optimal threshold from the experiment is 128” [PAGE 32]. This is a brittle scalar because semi-transparent or motion-blurred limbs can fall near the decision boundary. The source already shows failures from motion blur and similar colours, where lamps are segmented with players or white clothing is poorly segmented [PAGE 44][PAGE 46].

Fifth, the final integration model introduces two stabilization thresholds: a central-displacement threshold for invoking detection, reported as “threshold of 0.126 was optimal for conducting key point detection” [PAGE 58], and a per-vertex deadband, where displacement below “0.006” is treated as no movement [PAGE 60][PAGE 61]. These thresholds reduce jitter but also create lag. The thesis also imposes hard boundary clamps: “if the value exceeds 1016, it defaulted to 1016.” [PAGE 73]. That clause is itself a breakdown regime because the stated screen height is 1080; a clamp at 1016 is either a typo, a conservative safe-area choice, or an undocumented region-of-interest constraint. The final audit therefore concludes that the pipeline is not a single geometric solver but a set of competing regularizers: detection accuracy, flow smoothness, scene continuity, matting precision, and temporal deadbands.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let a video frame be \(I_n\), where \(n\in\mathbb{Z}\) is the frame index. The thesis assumes a standard frame rate in one evaluation: “a standard video comprises 30 frames per second” [PAGE 48]. Therefore, the sampling interval is \(\Delta t = 1/30\ \mathrm{s}\). The spatial domain is pixel space. The thesis mentions a screen range of \(1920\times1080\) [PAGE 73], so define \(\Omega = \{0,\dots,1919\}\times\{0,\dots,1079\}\). Because no camera calibration or metric scale is provided, SI meters cannot be recovered; the native unit for vertex motion is pixels, and the native temporal unit is frames.

1. Key-point detection operator.
The Key-point RCNN-PF detector is a mapping
\[
K_{\theta}: I_n \mapsto (b_n, V_n),
\]
where \(b_n\in[0,1]\) is an objectness-like probability or confidence score, and \(V_n=\{v_{n,i}\}_{i=1}^{4}\) is the set of four fence-board vertices. Each vertex is \(v_{n,i}=(x_{n,i},y_{n,i})\in\mathbb{R}^2\), measured in pixels. The thesis states that the Picture Frame branch “predicts four vertices for each ROI” [PAGE 26]. The detection schedule is not every frame. The source specifies “every 1st, 60th, 120th ,180th, etc.” [PAGE 32]. With scene detection, the active detection set can be written as
\[
D = \{0\}\cup\{60k : k\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup S,
\]
where \(S\) is the set of scene-change frames identified by PySceneDetect [PAGE 54].

Breakdown regime: if the four vertices are not in general position, for example nearly collinear, or if the detector assigns the wrong quadrilateral, the later perspective transform becomes unstable or meaningless. The thesis reports that brightness or colour changes can degrade detection [PAGE 39], and that frames without a fence board are excluded from evaluation because there are “no key points or designated areas for the advertisement” [PAGE 59].

2. Optical-flow propagation operator.
FlowNet estimates a displacement field between consecutive frames:
\[
F_{\phi}(I_{n-1}, I_n) = P_n,
\]
where \(P_n:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^2\), \(P_n(x,y)=(U_n(x,y),V_n(x,y))\). The source states that the optical flow field is organized with two channels, “one for horizontal movement \(U\) and one for vertical movement \(V\)” [PAGE 29]. The thesis equations are:
\[
u^{n-1}_{i} = U(x^{n-1}_{i}, y^{n-1}_{i})
\]
\[
v^{n-1}_{i} = V(x^{n-1}_{i}, y^{n-1}_{i})
\]
and
\[
x^{n}_{i} = x^{n-1}_{i} + u^{n-1}_{i}
\]
\[
y^{n}_{i} = y^{n-1}_{i} + v^{n-1}_{i}.
\]
These correspond to equations (3-2) through (3-5) in the source [PAGE 40]. Since coordinates are converted to integers for pixel-grid extraction [PAGE 31], an implementation must include rounding:
\[
x^{n}_{i} = \operatorname{round}(x^{n-1}_{i} + U_n(x^{n-1}_{i},y^{n-1}_{i})),
\]
\[
y^{n}_{i} = \operatorname{round}(y^{n-1}_{i} + V_n(x^{n-1}_{i},y^{n-1}_{i})).
\]
The unit of \(U_n,V_n\) is pixels per frame if FlowNet outputs raw pixel displacement. If the implementation normalizes displacements, the numeric values become dimensionless fractions of image width or height; the thesis does not state the normalization, so the units of the reported thresholds are ambiguous.

The classical brightness-constancy assumption underlying optical flow can be stated as
\[
I_n(x+u, y+v) = I_{n-1}(x,y).
\]
A first-order Taylor expansion gives
\[
I_x u + I_y v + I_t = 0,
\]
where \(I_x,I_y\) have units of intensity change per pixel and \(I_t\) has units of intensity change per frame. Breakdown occurs when \(I_x=I_y=0\), when displacement is too large for the local linearization, when illumination changes, or when occlusion introduces new surfaces. The thesis observes large camera motion causing ad drift: “the advertisement's ability to adhere tightly to the fence board is compromised.” [PAGE 67].

3. Central-displacement control threshold.
The final model computes a mean displacement over a central region to decide when to invoke detection. Let \(C\subset\Omega\) be the central region. Define
\[
\bar{d}_n = \frac{1}{|C|}\sum_{(x,y)\in C}\sqrt{U_n(x,y)^2 + V_n(x,y)^2}.
\]
The thesis reports that “a threshold of 0.126 was optimal for conducting key point detection.” [PAGE 58]. Formally, the detector is invoked when
\[
\bar{d}_n \ge \tau_c,
\]
with \(\tau_c = 0.126\) in the reported tuning. If \(\bar{d}_n\) is measured in pixels per frame, then \(\tau_c\) has units of pixels per frame. If it is normalized, it is dimensionless. The source does not clarify this. Breakdown: if \(\tau_c\) is too high, slow drift escapes correction; if too low, detection is invoked too often and jitter returns.

4. Per-vertex deadband.
For each vertex, define the predicted displacement magnitude
\[
\delta_{n,i} = \sqrt{(x^{*}_{n,i}-x^{n-1}_{i})^2 + (y^{*}_{n,i}-y^{n-1}_{i})^2}.
\]
The thesis states that if “the displacement distance between any of the new key points and their corresponding old key point is less than 0.006,” the model treats the new point as identical to the old one [PAGE 61]. Formally,
\[
v^{n}_{i} =
\begin{cases}
v^{*}_{n,i}, & \delta_{n,i} \ge \tau_s,\\
v^{n-1}_{i}, & \delta_{n,i} < \tau_s,
\end{cases}
\]
with \(\tau_s = 0.006\) in the reported implementation. This is a deadband operator. Breakdown: real motion smaller than \(\tau_s\) is ignored; if \(\tau_s\) is too large, the ad lags behind the fence board.

5. Boundary clamping.
The thesis imposes screen-bound constraints: if a coordinate is negative it is set to 0, if \(x\) exceeds 1919 it is set to 1919, and “if the value exceeds 1016, it defaulted to 1016.” [PAGE 73]. Formally,
\[
x' = \min(\max(x,0),1919),
\]
\[
y' = \min(\max(y,0),1016).
\]
The y-clamp is internally inconsistent with a 1080-pixel-high frame unless 1016 is an intentional safe-area margin. If the true image height is 1080, the mathematically consistent clamp is
\[
y' = \min(\max(y,0),1079).
\]
Breakdown: clamping multiple vertices to the same boundary can collapse the quadrilateral and produce a degenerate homography.

6. Perspective pasting operator.
The advertisement is warped using a perspective transform. The thesis states that corner points “empower the calculation of a transformation matrix, computed using the corner points with the cv. getPerspectiveTransform function” [PAGE 26]. Let \(S=\{s_i\}_{i=1}^{4}\) be the source advertisement corners and \(V_n=\{v_{n,i}\}_{i=1}^{4}\) the detected target vertices. Then
\[
H_n = \operatorname{getPerspectiveTransform}(S,V_n).
\]
\(H_n\) is a projective transform, dimensionless up to scale. The warped advertisement is
\[
\tilde{I}^{\mathrm{ad}}_n = \operatorname{warp}(I_{\mathrm{ad}}, H_n).
\]
Breakdown: if the four target points are ordered incorrectly or nearly collinear, the homography is unstable. The thesis reports key-point misalignment where “the left and right edges of the ad were pasted to the top and bottom edges of the fence board” [PAGE 62].

7. Human-mask compositing operator.
MODNet predicts an alpha matte \(\alpha_n\). The thesis says alpha values range from 0 to 1, with 0 background and 1 foreground [PAGE 23]. The binary threshold is reported as 128 on a 0-to-255 scale [PAGE 32]. Thus,
\[
M_n(x,y) = \mathbf{1}\left[\alpha_n(x,y) \ge \frac{128}{255}\right].
\]
Let \(B_n\) be the frame after advertisement pasting. The human-preserving composite is
\[
O_n = (1-M_n)\odot B_n + M_n\odot I_n.
\]
If the alpha matte is used softly instead of thresholded, the composite becomes
\[
O_n = (1-\alpha_n)\odot B_n + \alpha_n\odot I_n.
\]
The thesis instead describes a thresholding process before pasting human pixels back [PAGE 32].

8. MODNet objective.
The source gives the alpha loss as
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha} = \|\alpha_p - \alpha_g\|_1 + \mathcal{L}_c,
\]
where \(\alpha_p\) is predicted alpha, \(\alpha_g\) is ground-truth alpha, and \(\mathcal{L}_c\) is a compositional loss [PAGE 21]. The alpha maps are dimensionless intensities in \([0,1]\), so \(\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\) is dimensionless. The SOC strategy is described as self-supervised sub-objective consistency [PAGE 21], but the source does not provide an explicit formula, so no additional equation should be invented.

9. Scene-change operator.
PySceneDetect identifies cuts by colour and intensity changes [PAGE 53]. Formally, one may write
\[
S = \{n : \Delta(I_{n-1},I_n) > \tau_{\mathrm{scene}}\},
\]
where \(\Delta\) is a frame-difference statistic and \(\tau_{\mathrm{scene}}\) is the scene-detection threshold. The thesis shows example transition frame numbers “0, 678, 766, and so on” [PAGE 54]. At a frame in \(S\), Key-point RCNN-PF is invoked and FlowNet waits for new keypoints [PAGE 54]. Breakdown: missed cuts leave the ad attached to a vanished fence board; false cuts unnecessarily reset tracking.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The technical genealogy of the thesis is a chain of detector and motion-estimation innovations that progressively made sparse-vertex tracking plausible. The R-CNN lineage begins with region-based convolutional detection, then Fast R-CNN, Faster R-CNN, Mask R-CNN, and Keypoint R-CNN [PAGE 20][PAGE 21][PAGE 22]. The thesis selects the Keypoint RCNN-PF variant because it predicts four picture-frame vertices rather than only bounding boxes or human pose keypoints: “the addition of a Picture Frame branch (PF branch) of Key-point RCNN that predicts four vertices for each ROI” [PAGE 26]. This is a retro-causal choice: later geometric pasting requires four vertices, so the earlier detection architecture must be a vertex detector rather than a generic object detector.

The FlowNet lineage is equally important. Classical optical flow is described through differential, region-based, energy-based, and phase-based methods [PAGE 12]. The thesis then states that FlowNet “proposed using CNNs to directly learn the intricate mappings between image pixels and optical flow vectors” [PAGE 23]. FlowNet 2.0 combines FlowNetSimple and FlowNetCorr to handle both small and large displacements [PAGE 20]. This lineage explains why the thesis uses FlowNet to propagate vertices between sparse detections. It also explains the hidden assumption: FlowNet is effective only when inter-frame appearance change remains within the learned motion regime. The thesis later encounters the boundary of that regime when large camera motion or scene cuts occur.

The matting lineage begins with trimap-dependent traditional methods and proceeds to trimap-free real-time portrait matting. The thesis summarizes this evolution: “advancements in portrait matting have evolved from traditional methods with manual annotations to semi-supervised techniques and, more recently, real-time trimap-free solutions.” [PAGE 14]. MODNet is chosen because it avoids trimap construction, which is crucial for video where human boundaries move every frame. However, the retro-causal trap is that MODNet’s training distribution determines which foreground objects are preserved. The thesis trains on humans near fence boards and display boards [PAGE 41], but later discovers that “tennis balls in flight or rackets being manoeuvred might be impeded by the advertisement” [PAGE 66]. The earlier dataset design retroactively limits the final model’s occlusion competence.

The advertising lineage also shapes the evaluation. The thesis reviews virtual advertising effectiveness, automated placement, and replacement frameworks [PAGE 1][PAGE 8]. It notes prior systems such as ADNet and REP-Model, but positions its contribution as an integrated pipeline for fence boards rather than only ad detection or highlight insertion [PAGE 8]. This creates a multi-week trap: the project must label boards, train vertex detection, tune matting, integrate scene detection, and then stabilize temporal output. Each stage introduces its own evaluation metric, but the viewer-facing metric is temporal stability of the pasted ad.

The most consequential retro-causal trap is dataset scale. The Key-point RCNN-PF model is trained on 105 images and tested on 26 images [PAGE 34]. MODNet is trained on 150 images and tested on 15 images [PAGE 34]. These are small datasets for dynamic sports video. The thesis admits the limitation: “due to a lack of training data, the accuracy of fence board detection did not meet the anticipated performance benchmarks” [PAGE 71]. The later ten-minute test retroactively exposes the fragility of the earlier dataset design. Similarly, the thresholds \(\tau_c=0.126\) and \(\tau_s=0.006\) are tuned on a thirty-second clip: “the data from the previously applied 30-second demo video clip was employed for this comparative analysis.” [PAGE 58]. This means the final model’s stability constants are genealogically local to one clip. They may not transfer to different lighting, camera motion, or fence geometry.

Another multi-week trap is annotation under occlusion. The thesis describes labelling occluded fence-board sections by comparing adjacent frames: “a comparative examination of the frames immediately preceding and following the occluded frame is required to accurately mark the occluded portion of the fence boards.” [PAGE 36]. This procedure is reasonable but introduces label smoothing across time. The model may learn an average fence-board shape rather than the exact visible boundary in a single frame. When combined with sparse detection and flow propagation, this can produce a rectangle that is temporally smooth but geometrically stale.

Finally, the final model’s boundary clamp reveals a retro-causal inconsistency. The frame is said to be \(1920\times1080\), but the y-coordinate is clamped to 1016 [PAGE 73]. This may reflect a safe area, a crop, or a typo. In either case, the final implementation contains an undocumented coordinate constraint that can silently truncate the advertisement near the bottom of the frame. The genealogy therefore shows how early choices in detector architecture, dataset scope, and threshold tuning propagate into late-stage geometric and perceptual artifacts.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The system requires a recursive theory of mind because it must predict not only the fence board but also the viewer’s perception of stability, the annotator’s future labelling burden, and the engineer’s future correction behavior. The first mental model is the viewer’s: the advertisement should appear as a stable part of the scene. The thesis says the goal is to ensure “a natural, consistent, and uninterrupted manner” [PAGE 15]. If the ad flickers, the viewer perceives artificiality. The second mental model is the engineer’s: reduce flicker by detecting less often. The thesis implements this by running Key-point RCNN-PF only at sparse intervals and using FlowNet between them [PAGE 32]. The third mental model is the system’s: if detection is sparse, motion must be predictable. The fourth mental model is the scene’s: if the scene cuts, motion prediction must reset.

The crisis habit regression is visible in the transition from per-frame detection to sparse detection. Per-frame detection produces an “incessantly fluctuating rectangular pattern” [PAGE 40]. The habitual correction is to reduce detection frequency. This works locally because fewer detector updates mean fewer discontinuous changes. However, the counter-trap appears immediately: if one sparse detection is wrong, the error persists. The thesis states: “The consequence of this is the continuous display of a distorted ad for a significant duration” [PAGE 52]. Thus, the engineer’s habit of smoothing creates a new failure mode: stale error persistence.

The clock is central. At thirty frames per second, a sixty-frame interval equals two seconds. The thesis explicitly connects detection frequency to duration: an irregular rectangle can persist “for two seconds until the next round of detection” [PAGE 52]. This temporal window is the habit’s half-life. If the clock is too slow, the ad freezes onto a bad quadrilateral. If the clock is too fast, the ad trembles. The final model attempts to make the clock adaptive by using the central-displacement threshold \(\tau_c=0.126\) [PAGE 58]. This is a recursive correction: instead of a fixed clock, the system invokes detection when motion energy crosses a threshold. But this introduces another theory-of-mind problem: the threshold itself must be tuned for the viewer’s tolerance and the scene’s motion statistics. The thesis tunes it on one thirty-second clip, which may not generalize.

The scene-change issue is a deeper recursive trap. FlowNet assumes temporal continuity, but a cut invalidates that assumption. The thesis identifies the failure: the advertisement continues to appear “even when it no longer exists on the screen.” [PAGE 52]. The correction is PySceneDetect [PAGE 53]. This is a counter-trap against the previous counter-trap. Yet scene detection introduces its own recursive uncertainty: if the detector misses a cut, the ad floats; if it falsely detects a cut, the ad disappears. The system must therefore model not only the current frame but the probability that the current frame belongs to a new scene.

The human-occlusion layer adds another recursive agent: the foreground human. The system must predict which pixels belong to players, ball pickers, rackets, and balls. The thesis trains MODNet for humans near boards, but later finds residual failures: “tennis balls in flight or rackets being manoeuvred might be impeded by the advertisement.” [PAGE 66]. This is a failure of recursive foreground modeling. The system modeled the most common occluders but not the full physical class of occluders. The viewer’s expectation is that any foreground object should remain visible; the system’s internal model only includes some foreground objects.

The deadband is a final recursive stabilization. It says that if a predicted vertex moves less than \(\tau_s=0.006\), treat it as unchanged [PAGE 61]. This suppresses micro-jitter and prevents human-overlap pulling. But it also creates a new hidden state: the true vertex may have moved slightly, yet the system reports no movement. Over many frames, this can accumulate as a slow drift. The recursive theory of mind therefore must include the system’s own deadband: the viewer may not see immediate jitter, but may later see a gradual misalignment.

In summary, the thesis is a sequence of recursive corrections: detect every frame, then detect sparsely, then detect by scene, then detect by displacement threshold, then deadband small vertex motion. Each correction is rational relative to the previous crisis, but each creates a new gray zone. The final model’s competence depends on balancing these clocks: detector clock, flow clock, scene clock, matting clock, and deadband clock. The source’s own conclusion acknowledges residual error: “a 100% recognition accuracy is unachievable” [PAGE 66]. That statement is not merely technical; it is an admission that the recursive model cannot fully eliminate perceptual uncertainty.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto framework is required because advertisement replacement is not a single detection problem. It is a layered admissibility problem. Let \(V\) be a veto predicate. The advertisement should be rendered only if
\[
V = V_{\mathrm{board}} \lor V_{\mathrm{flow}} \lor V_{\mathrm{foreground}} \lor V_{\mathrm{illumination}} = 0.
\]
If any layer vetoes, the system should defer placement. The thesis itself states the intended behavior: “ad placement is deferred until the fence board or the optimal location, becomes visible.” [PAGE 2].

1. Physical and geometric veto: fence-board visibility.
The first veto is whether the fence board is visible and geometrically valid. The thesis excludes “frames without any fence board” because they have “no key points or designated areas for the advertisement.” [PAGE 59]. Formally, let \(m_n\) be the number of visible fence-board vertices. If \(m_n < 4\), the quadrilateral is underdetermined. If the four vertices are nearly collinear, the homography is ill-conditioned. Therefore,
\[
V_{\mathrm{board}} = 1
\]
when \(m_n < 4\), when vertex confidence is below a required threshold, or when the quadrilateral area is too small or degenerate. This veto is physical: without a valid planar target, perspective pasting has no defined geometry.

2. Optical-flow validity veto.
The second veto concerns motion estimation. FlowNet is valid under local smoothness and appearance continuity. The thesis shows failure under large camera motion: “the advertisement's ability to adhere tightly to the fence board is compromised.” [PAGE 67]. It also notes that moving frames can cause the ad to jump “from one location to another.” [PAGE 59]. Define a flow-confidence or flow-magnitude condition. If the central displacement exceeds the range for which propagation is reliable, or if frame difference indicates a cut, then
\[
V_{\mathrm{flow}} = 1.
\]
This veto prevents integrating motion across discontinuities. The scene-change issue is precisely the case where \(V_{\mathrm{flow}}\) should activate: the ad should not persist when the fence board no longer exists [PAGE 52].

3. Biomechanical foreground veto.
The third veto is biomechanical: humans, rackets, balls, and limbs occupy the foreground. MODNet is used to preserve human pixels, but the thesis documents failures under motion blur and similar colours: lamps can be segmented with players, and white clothing can be poorly segmented [PAGE 44][PAGE 46]. Later, it states that “tennis balls in flight or rackets being manoeuvred might be impeded by the advertisement.” [PAGE 66]. Define \(V_{\mathrm{foreground}}=1\) when a foreground object intersects the advertisement quadrilateral but is not reliably included in the alpha mask. In that case, compositing would overwrite a real object. The veto is necessary because viewer immersion is broken if a ball or racket disappears behind an ad.

4. Environmental illumination veto.
The fourth veto is environmental illumination. The thesis trains on frames “from daylight scenes to under-the-floodlight moments.” [PAGE 35]. It also reports that detection failures may be caused by “scene brightness or alterations in the image's colour.” [PAGE 39]. Brightness changes violate the brightness-constancy assumption behind optical flow and can shift detector features. Define \(V_{\mathrm{illumination}}=1\) when global illumination change, glare, or colour cast exceeds the calibrated range of the detector and flow estimator. This veto is especially important for outdoor sports where weather, floodlights, and camera exposure can change rapidly.

5. Proof of veto necessity.
Suppose \(V_{\mathrm{board}}=1\). Then the target quadrilateral is absent or invalid. The homography \(H_n\) cannot be reliably computed, so any pasted advertisement is geometrically meaningless. Suppose \(V_{\mathrm{flow}}=1\). Then the predicted vertex update
\[
x^{n}_{i}=x^{n-1}_{i}+U_n,
\quad
y^{n}_{i}=y^{n-1}_{i}+V_n
\]
has unbounded or scene-invalid error, so the ad drifts or persists after a cut. Suppose \(V_{\mathrm{foreground}}=1\). Then the mask \(M_n\) fails to protect a real foreground object, causing disappearance or occlusion artifacts. Suppose \(V_{\mathrm{illumination}}=1\). Then both \(K_{\theta}\) and \(F_{\phi}\) operate outside their reliable appearance regime. Therefore, the only safe policy is to veto rendering until the relevant layer is restored.

6. Layer adjudication order.
The adjudication order should be conservative: first check scene cut, then board visibility, then flow reliability, then foreground mask reliability, then illumination. This order follows the causal structure of the video. A scene cut invalidates flow before board geometry can be propagated. A missing board invalidates pasting before foreground compositing matters. An unreliable foreground mask invalidates human preservation before final aesthetic evaluation. The thesis’s final model attempts to approximate this order through PySceneDetect, displacement thresholds, deadbands, and MODNet compositing, but the source also shows that no single layer is sufficient. The cross-layer veto proof therefore concludes that robust advertisement replacement requires explicit deferral logic, not merely better per-frame inference.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are three verifiable reinforcement-learning-with-verifiable-rewards style veto scenarios. In this context, a veto is a condition under which the ad-placement action should be rejected or deferred because the verifiable state predicate is false. The predictions are stated in terms of observable frame-level outcomes.

Scenario 1: Scene-change veto.
Counterfactual: a hard scene cut occurs at frame \(n_s\), but PySceneDetect fails to mark \(n_s\), and the next scheduled Key-point RCNN-PF detection is at the next multiple of 60. Source basis: the thesis defines this as the “Scene Change Issue” and states that the advertisement continues to appear “even when it no longer exists on the screen.” [PAGE 52][PAGE 53].
Prediction: for frames \(n_s+1\) through the next detection frame, the advertisement remains rendered over pixels that no longer contain the original fence board. The verifiable signature is a non-zero ad mask over a background whose colour histogram or edge structure has changed abruptly. If scene detection is correctly triggered, the ad should be suppressed until Key-point RCNN-PF confirms a new quadrilateral.
Veto predicate:
\[
V_{\mathrm{scene}}(n) = 1
\]
if a frame-difference statistic exceeds the scene threshold but no fresh board detection is available.
RLVR reward implication: reward should penalize ad pixels after an unverified cut. A verifiable check is to compare the ad mask at \(n_s+1\) with the post-cut background segmentation. If overlap with non-board background exceeds a small tolerance, the action is vetoed.

Scenario 2: Vertex deadband and ordering veto.
Counterfactual: FlowNet predicts new vertices, but either the displacement is below \(\tau_s=0.006\) or the vertex order is not canonical. Source basis: the thesis reports shape distortion from inaccurate keypoints [PAGE 60], infinite extension from vertices leaving the frame [PAGE 61], and key-point misalignment where ad edges are pasted to the wrong fence-board edges [PAGE 62]. The deadband rule states that if displacement is “less than 0.006,” the new keypoint is treated as the old one [PAGE 61].
Prediction: if the deadband is applied without geometric validation, a slowly moving fence board can be frozen while the camera moves, causing the ad to lag. If vertex ordering is not enforced, the quadrilateral can be self-intersecting or rotated, causing the advertisement to appear on the wrong edges. The verifiable signature is a quadrilateral whose vertex order violates a canonical ordering, or whose area changes abruptly without a corresponding scene event.
Veto predicate:
\[
V_{\mathrm{vertex}}(n)=1
\]
if the four predicted vertices are not in canonical order, if any pair is too close, if the quadrilateral is self-intersecting, or if any vertex lies outside the valid screen bounds after clamping.
RLVR reward implication: reward should include a geometric consistency term. A verifiable check is to compute the polygon area and edge-length ratios before and after FlowNet update. If the polygon becomes degenerate or inverted, the update is vetoed and the previous stable vertices are retained.

Scenario 3: Foreground occlusion veto.
Counterfactual: a tennis ball, racket, or motion-blurred limb crosses the fence-board region, but MODNet was trained primarily on human figures near boards. Source basis: the thesis states that “tennis balls in flight or rackets being manoeuvred might be impeded by the advertisement.” [PAGE 66]. It also documents motion-blur segmentation failures [PAGE 44].
Prediction: when the unmodeled foreground object intersects the ad quadrilateral, the compositing step will overwrite it if the object is not included in \(M_n\). The verifiable signature is the disappearance of a tracked small object, for example a ball, for one or more frames while its trajectory predicts it should be visible. For rackets, the signature is missing edge pixels that were present in the previous frame and are not explained by camera motion.
Veto predicate:
\[
V_{\mathrm{foreground}}(n)=1
\]
if a foreground object intersects the ad region and the alpha mask does not cover it with sufficient confidence.
RLVR reward implication: reward should penalize occlusion of verifiable foreground trajectories. A verifiable check is to track the ball or racket independently and require that its pixels remain unaltered when it crosses the fence-board region. If the tracked object vanishes, the placement action is vetoed or deferred until the object leaves the region.

These three veto scenarios show that the thesis’s final integration model is not merely a composition of three networks. It is a temporal arbitration system. The verifiable failure modes are not abstract; they correspond directly to source-reported artifacts: post-cut ad persistence, shape distortion, infinite extension, key-point misalignment, and foreground disappearance. A robust deployment should therefore expose explicit veto signals: scene-cut detected without new board geometry; vertex order or polygon degeneracy; foreground mask uncertainty for non-human objects. When any veto is active, the rational action is deferral, exactly as the thesis’s abstract states: “ad placement is deferred until the fence board or the optimal location, becomes visible.” [PAGE 2].
