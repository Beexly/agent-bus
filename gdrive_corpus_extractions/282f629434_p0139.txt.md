Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock operates on three recursively nested levels. Level 0 is the player executing under pressure: a trailing team in the final minutes regresses to the habitual on-side kickoff or the routine place-kick try because those are the drills most rehearsed under stress [PAGE 130]. Level 1 is the referee applying the protocol: the non-rotation rule [PAGE 130] is itself a counter-trap against a Level-0 offense that attempts to angle the ball forward after the whistle to gain inches; by fixing the gauge at declaration, the referee removes the angular degree of freedom that a habit-driven offense might exploit. Level 2 is the coach modeling the referee: knowing the referee will not rotate the ball, the coach changes snap placement or holder positioning to compensate, anticipating that the forward point will be measured without post-declaration optimization. Level 3 is the referee modeling the coach's model: anticipating that the offense expects strict gauge fixation, the referee may pre-commit to an even more rigid declaration cadence to reduce τ_d variance. The game clock acts as an adversarial agent in this recursion: as clock time dwindles, the trailing team's crisis habit intensifies, increasing the frequency of rushed plays and rushed whistles, which raises τ_d and pushes the system toward the singularity regime |v|·τ_d ≥ Δx [PAGE 130]. The referee must therefore resist the recursive pressure to quick-whistle, because shortening τ_d under duress degrades the very measurement the non-rotation rule protects. The counter-trap is stable only if the referee's protocol is time-invariant; if the clock induces non-stationary officiating, the offense can regress to a meta-habit of snapping late to provoke measurement error.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto (hard, automatic): Rule XXI Section 1 states that if a kick-off or fair-catch kick does not travel at least 10 yards (9.144 m) toward the opponents' goal line and is not touched by an opponent, "the ball is not in play" [PAGE 130]. This predicate requires no discretion; it is a mechanical distance/contact test. Rule XXI Section 2 adds that a loose ball in possession of neither side may not be kicked, with automatic loss of ball to opponents at the spot of the foul [PAGE 130]. Biomechanical layer: the place-kick try [PAGE 130] assumes a stationary ball held on the ground; the holder's placement error σ_place (≈ inches) and the prohibition on rotation [PAGE 130] bound the forward-point uncertainty. A veto at this layer is valid if the ball was rotated after declaration or if holder placement violated the spotted position, because the measurement functional x_f is defined only for B(t_d) under gauge O = I. Weather layer: wind or rain can move a dead ball after declaration but before measurement. If the coefficient of friction between leather and turf drops below the threshold needed to resist field slope or gust velocity (i.e., if m·g·sinθ > μ·m·g·cosθ or if wind force exceeds static friction), the ball may roll after t_d, invalidating x_f unless the ball is re-declared dead at its new position. The 1915 text provides no weather re-spot rule, so a weather veto is admissible only when the displacement exceeds Δx = 0.0254 m before the whistle. Adjudication hierarchy: hard binary vetoes (not in play, loose-ball kick penalty) override all softer considerations; soft vetoes (weather displacement, marginal touch) require evidence exceeding measurement resolution Δx and must be referenced to the declaration time t_d [PAGE 130].
