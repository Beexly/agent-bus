<THINK_TRACE>
The source paper’s central inferential object is a counterfactual expected-value comparison: observed defender positioning at the moment of catch is evaluated against a conditional distribution of hypothetical “ghost” defenders. The logic is multi-hop. First, the model estimates a conditional density for the receiver’s ending field position, equivalently yards after catch conditional on catch-moment covariates [PAGE 6, PAGE 9]. Second, that density is converted into expected play value through a utility map, typically expected points, producing an observed catch-moment value [PAGE 10, PAGE 11]. Third, the nearest defender’s observed covariates are replaced by hypothetical ghost covariates to generate a counterfactual catch-moment value [PAGE 12]. Fourth, the observed-minus-ghost difference is averaged over a ghost-location distribution and over sampled defender trajectories, yielding a defender-specific expected value contribution [PAGE 13, PAGE 14].

The gray-zone audit begins with the estimand itself. The paper explicitly conditions on completed passes and catch-moment information: “our analysis is limited to 10,363 completed passes” [PAGE 9]. Therefore the estimand is not “total defensive coverage value.” It is value conditional on the event that a forward pass was completed outside the end zone. This creates selection: plays where the defender successfully prevents a catch are excluded. The authors acknowledge this indirectly when they write that for a catch to occur, “the defender has already ‘failed’ in preventing the receiver from catching the football” [PAGE 23]. The framework therefore evaluates post-catch positioning, not pre-catch coverage efficacy. Any interpretation that treats the metric as a complete coverage grade violates the paper’s own scope.

A second gray zone is the nearest-defender abstraction. The model orders defenders by Euclidean distance to the receiver at catch and evaluates the closest defender [PAGE 8]. The authors note that this “may be viewed as undesirable” because another defender responsible for coverage may be farther away [PAGE 22]. This means the metric is not a coverage-assignment model. It is a proximity-based spatial attribution. If the nearest defender is not the responsible defender, the metric can misattribute value. The paper suggests a Hidden Markov Model extension for coverage assignment but does not implement it [PAGE 22].

A third gray zone is the ghost distribution. The paper estimates a 2D conditional density for ghost location with nearest-defender features removed, then samples defender trajectory features conditional on location [PAGE 13, PAGE 14]. The trajectory model is simplified. Instead of a full 3D conditional density, the authors use weighted resampling of observed defender trajectories based on distance to the receiver. The source states: “observations with similar distances to the receiver as the hypothetical location ℓ will have a higher probability of being sampled” [PAGE 14]. This preserves some empirical correlation between speed, direction, and orientation, but it does not model the full joint distribution of trajectory and location. The authors explicitly say they “leave the exploration of properly modeling the trajectory information for future work” [PAGE 14].

A fourth gray zone is temporal granularity. The tracking data are recorded at 10 Hz [PAGE 3, PAGE 8], but the model collapses each play to the catch moment for receiver/defender features, with quarterback features measured at pass release [PAGE 8]. Thus, the method is not continuous-time ghosting. It is a catch-moment snapshot evaluated against a counterfactual distribution. The paper’s own discussion says moving beyond a single moment would require “full continuous-time” modeling [PAGE 23]. Therefore, any claim that the metric captures entire-play defender trajectory must be rejected unless explicitly qualified as catch-moment attribution.

A fifth gray zone is model uncertainty. The RFCDE estimator uses tree terminal-node weights to perform weighted kernel density estimation [PAGE 9, PAGE 10]. This is nonparametric, but it is still sensitive to bandwidth, tree count, feature set, and training-week stability. The authors use leave-one-week-out cross validation and report that YAC RFCDE performance stabilizes after about eight weeks, while the 2D location RFCDE requires more weeks, with similar values after twelve weeks [PAGE 11, PAGE 13]. These facts create multi-week traps: early-season rankings may be unstable, and cross-validation improvements do not guarantee causal validity.

The deepest audit point is that the paper produces a normative counterfactual value, not an observed physical trajectory. The ghost defender is a statistical construct derived from league-wide conditional densities. The observed defender is compared against what an average defender, conditional on receiver and quarterback context, would have been expected to do. This is useful for evaluation, but it is not a direct measurement of effort, skill, or coaching intent. The authors caution: “we cannot attribute the entire change in EPV to a single player” [PAGE 22]. The framework therefore requires interpretive restraint: it measures deviation from a conditional baseline, not isolated causal impact.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The paper’s primary operator is random forests for conditional density estimation, abbreviated RFCDE. The source states that the framework “leverages multi-dimensional tracking data features through flexible random forests for conditional density estimation” [PAGE 1]. For an observation \(X^*\), tree \(t\) has terminal-node region \(R(X^*,\theta_t)\). The RFCDE weight for training observation \(i\) is

\[
w_i(X^*) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\frac{\mathbf{1}\left(X_i \in R(X^*,\theta_t)\right)}
{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\left(X_j \in R(X^*,\theta_t)\right)}.
\]

Units: \(w_i\) is dimensionless. Assumptions: each terminal node contains at least one training observation, so the denominator is nonzero. Breakdown regime: if a leaf is empty, or if numerical underflow produces all-zero weights, the density estimate is undefined. If \(T\) is too small, weights become noisy; the paper uses \(T=500\) trees [PAGE 10].

The conditional density estimate is

\[
\widehat{f}(Y\mid X^*) =
\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i(X^*)}
\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i(X^*) K_h(Y_i - Y).
\]

If \(Y\) is yards after catch, measured in yards, then \(\widehat{f}\) has units of probability density per yard. The bandwidth \(h\) has units of yards. If \(Y\) is converted to SI units, length is meters, density is per meter, and \(h\) is in meters. The kernel bandwidth controls bias-variance tradeoff. Singularity/breakdown: as \(h\to 0\), the estimate approaches a weighted empirical delta mass and loses smooth density interpretation; as \(h\to\infty\), the estimate becomes overly flat and conditional information is washed out.

The expected play value at catch is defined as

\[
\mathrm{EPV}_{\mathrm{catch}} = \mathbb{E}[V \mid X_{\mathrm{catch}}],
\]

where \(V\) is a play-value random variable, such as expected points [PAGE 10]. If the receiver’s ending field position is \(Y\), and the utility map is \(g(Y)\), then

\[
\mathrm{EPV}_{\mathrm{catch}} =
\int g(Y) f(Y\mid X_{\mathrm{catch}})\,dY.
\]

Units: \(g(Y)\) is in expected points; \(f(Y\mid X_{\mathrm{catch}})\) is per yard; \(dY\) is yards; therefore \(\mathrm{EPV}_{\mathrm{catch}}\) is in points. In implementation, the density is evaluated over a discrete grid in one-yard increments, normalized to sum to one, and the integral becomes a sum:

\[
\widehat{\mathrm{EPV}}_{\mathrm{catch}}
=
\sum_{y \in G} g(y)\widehat{p}(y\mid X_{\mathrm{catch}})\,\Delta y,
\]

with \(\Delta y = 1\) yard. The grid includes negative yardage, described as “additional two yards” of padding beyond maximum possible gain, and lower bound based on observed yardage distribution [PAGE 12]. Breakdown: truncation of the grid induces bias if true conditional mass lies outside the grid. Normalization over the grid turns a continuous density into a discrete-like probability mass function; this is an approximation.

The ghost counterfactual replaces observed nearest-defender covariates with ghost covariates:

\[
\mathrm{EPV}^{\mathrm{ghost}}_{\mathrm{catch}} =
\int g(Y) f(Y\mid \widetilde{X}_{\mathrm{catch}})\,dY.
\]

The play-level difference is

\[
\delta_{\mathrm{catch}} =
\mathrm{EPV}_{\mathrm{catch}} -
\mathrm{EPV}^{\mathrm{ghost}}_{\mathrm{catch}}.
\]

Units: expected points. Positive \(\delta_{\mathrm{catch}}\) means observed defender positioning is better for offense than the ghost baseline; negative means better for defense. The paper’s example obtains \(\widehat{\mathrm{EPV}}_{\mathrm{catch}}=-1.35\) and \(\mathbb{E}[\delta_{\mathrm{catch}}]\approx -1.22\) [PAGE 15, PAGE 17]. These are point-scale values, not physical distances.

Because ghosts are distributions, the expected defender value integrates over ghost identities:

\[
\mathbb{E}[\delta_{\mathrm{catch}}] =
\int_{\gamma \in G}
\left(
\mathrm{EPV}_{\mathrm{catch}} -
\mathrm{EPV}^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{catch}}
\right)
h(\gamma \mid X^{-\mathrm{def1}}_{\mathrm{catch}})
\,d\gamma.
\]

Here \(h\) is the conditional density of ghost defender state given covariates excluding nearest-defender features [PAGE 13]. Units: if \(\gamma\) is a 2D location, \(h\) has units per square yard; if \(\gamma\) includes trajectory, units become more complex. The paper decomposes the ghost state into location and trajectory:

\[
\mathbb{E}[\delta_{\mathrm{catch}}] =
\int_{\ell \in L}
\left[
\int_{v \in V}
\left(
\mathrm{EPV}_{\mathrm{catch}} -
\mathrm{EPV}^{\ell,v}_{\mathrm{catch}}
\right)
p(v\mid \ell)\,dv
\right]
h(\ell \mid X^{-\mathrm{def1}}_{\mathrm{catch}})
\,d\ell.
\]

Assumptions: conditional factorization \(p(\gamma\mid X)=p(v\mid \ell)h(\ell\mid X)\). This is a simplification because trajectory and location are not conditionally independent in full physical reality. Breakdown: if \(p(v\mid\ell)\) is misspecified, the ghost EPV distribution is biased.

For trajectory sampling, the source defines weights

\[
\omega_{i,\ell} =
\frac{1}
{\left|d_i - d_\ell\right|},
\]

where \(d_i\) is observed nearest-defender distance to receiver and \(d_\ell\) is the distance associated with hypothetical location \(\ell\) [PAGE 14]. Units: if distances are yards, the denominator has yards, so raw weights have inverse yards; after normalization, sampling probabilities are dimensionless. Singularity: if \(d_i=d_\ell\), the weight diverges. A practical estimator must regularize, e.g.

\[
\omega_{i,\ell}^{(\epsilon)} =
\frac{1}
{\left|d_i - d_\ell\right| + \epsilon},
\]

with \(\epsilon>0\) in yards. The paper does not specify the numerical guardrail in the quoted equation, but the breakdown regime is explicit: exact distance equality causes undefined weights.

The Monte Carlo approximation is

\[
\widehat{\mathbb{E}[\delta_{\mathrm{catch}}]} =
\int_{\ell \in L}
\left[
\frac{1}{B}
\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\left(
\mathrm{EPV}_{\mathrm{catch}} -
\mathrm{EPV}^{\ell,b}_{\mathrm{catch}}
\right)
\right]
h(\ell \mid X^{-\mathrm{def1}}_{\mathrm{catch}})
\,d\ell.
\]

The paper uses \(B=100\) resampled trajectory vectors per ghost location [PAGE 15]. Units remain expected points. Breakdown: if \(B\) is too small, Monte Carlo variance contaminates player comparisons. If the grid \(L\) is coarse, spatial integration error appears. If \(h\) is poorly estimated, the ghost baseline is biased.

Feature units are given in Table 1: \(x,y\) are field coordinates; speed \(s\) is yards per second; acceleration \(a\) is yards per second squared; distance \(dis\) is yards; orientation \(o\) and direction \(dir\) are degrees between 0 and 360 [PAGE 9]. In SI units, coordinates and distances would be meters, speed meters per second, acceleration meters per second squared, and angular measurements would be radians if used analytically. Probability densities must then be expressed per meter or per square meter. The paper’s Table 2 defines features such as horizontal yards from target endzone, vertical yards from center of field, speed, distance to receiver, and angular differences [PAGE 30]. These are directly derived tracking features, not latent psychological variables.

Information-theoretic quantities appear in validation. The paper evaluates 2D location RFCDE using cross entropy [PAGE 13, PAGE 29]. If cross entropy is computed with natural logarithms, units are nats; if base-2 logarithms are used, units are bits. The source does not specify the base in the quoted text. Breakdown: if estimated probabilities are zero for observed outcomes, cross entropy diverges unless smoothing is applied.

A physical feasibility operator, not explicitly included in the paper but necessary for vetoing counterfactual claims, is

\[
\left\|p_{\mathrm{ghost}} - p_{\mathrm{def}}\right\|_2
\leq
s_{\max}\tau + \frac{1}{2}a_{\max}\tau^2,
\]

where \(p\) is position in yards, \(\tau\) is available time in seconds, \(s_{\max}\) is maximum plausible speed in yards per second, and \(a_{\max}\) is maximum plausible acceleration in yards per second squared. Assumption: straight-line, bounded acceleration motion. Breakdown: at \(\tau=0\), only the observed position is feasible. If the RFCDE ghost distribution assigns substantial mass to locations violating this inequality, the counterfactual is physically non-attributable.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The conceptual genealogy runs from discrete game-state valuation to continuous tracking-based valuation to ghosting. The paper traces expected value estimation to baseball’s runs expectancy matrix and soccer’s expected goals [PAGE 2]. In American football, expected points estimates the value of a game state using context such as down, distance, field position, and time remaining [PAGE 2]. Expected points added is the difference between pre-play and post-play expected points [PAGE 2]. The source states that expected points added “can range from roughly -14 to +14” and provides an interpretable point scale [PAGE 2]. This historical lineage matters because the ghosting framework inherits the same interpretive advantage: values are expressed in expected points, not abstract residuals.

The next lineage step is continuous-time valuation. The paper cites basketball expected possession value, soccer possession value, and football expected yards models [PAGE 3]. For football tracking, the authors reference a long short-term memory model for expected yards gained during a play [PAGE 3]. This establishes the shift from discrete pre-snap valuation to within-play tracking-based valuation. The NFL tracking system is described as using RFID tags at 10 frames per second, providing positional information for players and the ball [PAGE 3]. The Big Data Bowl competition is identified as the public data ecosystem enabling such research [PAGE 3].

The ghosting lineage begins with basketball player evaluation against average players, then moves to deep imitation learning in soccer, where optimal defender locations minimize scoring chance given positional information [PAGE 4]. The paper notes that later work extended coordinated multi-agent imitation learning to basketball and American football [PAGE 5]. It also notes that much prior ghosting produced point estimates of optimal location or trajectory, whereas this paper’s contribution is to use conditional density estimation to return a distribution: “the expected within-play value for ghosts can be computed using a full probability distribution of player positioning and movement information” [PAGE 5].

The methodological lineage is RFCDE. The paper cites random forests for conditional density estimation and states that tree splits are determined to minimize a loss specific to conditional density estimation, rather than traditional regression or classification loss [PAGE 10]. The weights come from terminal nodes, and the conditional density is obtained through weighted kernel density estimation [PAGE 9, PAGE 10]. This lineage matters because the framework is not a deterministic “ghost trajectory generator.” It is a conditional density baseline, which permits uncertainty quantification.

Multi-week traps emerge from the paper’s own sensitivity analyses. The authors use leave-one-week-out cross validation to assess feature sets [PAGE 11]. They report that YAC RFCDE performance “stabilizes after about eight weeks of data” [PAGE 11]. For the 2D ghost location RFCDE, they observe that it “appears to require more weeks to stabilize” with similar values after twelve weeks [PAGE 13]. These facts imply that early-season player or team rankings may reflect training-data instability. A retro-causal trap is to infer stable player skill from a few early weeks when the density estimators have not stabilized. Another trap is to treat the ghost baseline as timeless league average when it is estimated from a single 2018 season [PAGE 8].

Another genealogical trap is feature selection. The paper finds that adding the nearest defender beyond receiver and quarterback information did not improve YAC RFCDE out-of-sample performance [PAGE 11]. For the 2D ghost model, adding nearest teammate or second-closest defender features did not improve cross-validation performance [PAGE 13]. This creates a trap: absence of improvement in this dataset does not prove those features are irrelevant in football. It may reflect data limitations, omitted linemen, or the catch-moment snapshot. The source states: “offensive and defensive linemen information are omitted from the provided tracking data” [PAGE 8]. Therefore, any claim about full-team defensive structure must be retroactively discounted.

The final genealogical trap is metric reification. The framework produces interpretable point-scale values, which makes it tempting to treat them as pure player skill. But the paper itself says, “we cannot attribute the entire change in EPV to a single player without recognizing that the scheme of the team’s coaching staff may have played a large role” [PAGE 22]. The lineage therefore leads to a disciplined conclusion: the metric is a conditional spatial-deviation measure, not a full causal estimate of individual defensive ability.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind audit must model the beliefs of quarterbacks, receivers, defenders, coaches, and analysts. The paper’s estimand is conditional on a completed catch. This means the defender’s observed state is already embedded in an outcome where the offense succeeded in completing the pass. A defender may have been positioned in a way that reduced catch probability but, conditional on a catch occurring, increased or decreased yards after catch. The authors recognize this: “There may be a tradeoff between the positioning of a defender with regards to limiting the catch probability versus the value gained after the catch” [PAGE 23]. A recursive model must therefore separate two defender objectives: prevent completion, and limit post-catch gain. The paper’s metric addresses only the second, conditional on the first having failed.

Crisis habit regression occurs when players under high-pressure situations revert to learned habits rather than optimize the specific game state. In the paper’s framework, habit regression would appear as stable deviations from ghost baseline across similar contexts even when strategic optimization would predict different behavior. For example, a defender may habitually trail the receiver rather than play orthogonal leverage, even if the ghost distribution suggests a different location would lower expected offensive value. The source observes asymmetry in optimal locations, noting that locations “more orthogonal to the receiver’s direction of movement are more optimal relative to trailing or directly going towards the receiver” [PAGE 17]. This is a hypothesis-generating observation, not a causal behavioral claim. A recursive ToM model would ask: did the defender believe the receiver would break in a certain direction? Did the quarterback’s throw confirm or contradict that belief? The paper’s catch-moment snapshot cannot fully answer this.

Counter-trap arises when the evaluation metric itself changes behavior. If defenders, coaches, or front offices optimize for the ghost metric, they may improve measured catch-moment positioning without improving true coverage. For example, a defender might learn to be near the receiver at catch to reduce conditional YAC while conceding a low-percentage catch that the metric ignores because it only includes completed passes. The source explicitly says the metrics are only for completed passes, whereas outcome-based pass defense EPA includes all pass attempts [PAGE 20]. This creates a Goodhart risk: optimizing the conditional metric may not optimize full-pass value.

Clock effects operate at multiple temporal layers. At the play level, the source tracks from snap to catch, with event labels such as ball snap, pass release, pass arrived, catch, and tackle [PAGE 8, PAGE 9]. The model uses catch-moment features for receiver and defense, and pass-release features for quarterback [PAGE 8]. This means the clock is compressed into discrete event landmarks. It does not model the entire trajectory from snap to catch as a continuous control problem. At the season level, the clock matters because the ghost distribution is estimated from weeks of data. The paper’s week-by-week sensitivity analyses show that early-week estimates are less stable [PAGE 11, PAGE 13]. A recursive ToM audit must therefore distinguish three clocks: within-play temporal causality, season-long estimation stability, and strategic adaptation over multiple games.

A formal belief hierarchy can be sketched without inventing toy variables. Let \(S_t\) be game state, including down, distance, field position, and time. Let \(D_t\) be defender belief about receiver intent, and \(O_t\) be offense belief about defender positioning. The defender’s positioning at catch, \(p_{\mathrm{def}}\), is a function of \(D_t\), coaching scheme, and physical constraints. The offense’s route and throw decision are a function of \(O_t\) and expected defender behavior. The ghost baseline estimates the distribution of \(p_{\mathrm{def}}\) conditional on observable context, but it does not observe \(D_t\) or \(O_t\) directly. Therefore, the metric captures revealed positioning, not belief.

The crisis habit regression test is: stratify \(\mathbb{E}[\delta_{\mathrm{catch}}]\) by high-pressure context, such as third down, red-zone proximity, or late-game clock, using features like distance to first down and field position [PAGE 30]. If a defender’s deviation from ghost baseline collapses toward league-average behavior under pressure, that suggests habit regression or reduced strategic flexibility. The counter-trap test is: compare the ghost metric to full-pass outcome models that include incomplete passes, interceptions, and sacks [PAGE 20]. If a defender improves conditional YAC metric but worsens full-pass defensive value, the counter-trap is active. The clock test is: estimate the metric only after the training windows stabilize, because early-season estimates may be contaminated by insufficient data [PAGE 11, PAGE 13].

The deepest recursive trap is that the ghost baseline is not neutral. It is a league-average conditional distribution estimated from the same season. If many defenders are evaluated relative to this baseline, and if coaching decisions respond to the metric, the baseline may become endogenous. Future data may reflect strategic adaptations that invalidate the original conditional distribution. The paper’s framework is therefore best viewed as a diagnostic snapshot, not a stationary law of defensive value.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical and biomechanical veto requires rejecting any interpretation of the ghost metric that violates kinematic feasibility. The source provides tracking variables for speed, acceleration, distance, orientation, and direction, with speed in yards per second and acceleration in yards per second squared [PAGE 9]. Let \(p_{\mathrm{def}}\) be the observed defender position at catch, and \(p_{\mathrm{ghost}}\) be a hypothetical ghost position. If the catch occurs after time \(\tau\) from a prior event, a necessary physical condition for the ghost position to be causally plausible is

\[
\left\|p_{\mathrm{ghost}} - p_{\mathrm{def}}\right\|_2
\leq
s_{\max}\tau + \frac{1}{2}a_{\max}\tau^2.
\]

This inequality assumes straight-line motion, bounded acceleration, and no abrupt direction change. It is conservative in some contexts and restrictive in others. If the ghost density assigns substantial posterior mass to positions that violate this bound given the defender’s observed state, the counterfactual is physically vetoed. The paper does not enforce this constraint; it evaluates hypothetical defender locations across a grid [PAGE 7, PAGE 14]. Therefore, some ghost locations may be statistically likely but physically unreachable from the observed pre-catch state.

A second physical veto concerns velocity direction. Position alone is insufficient. A defender’s momentum and orientation constrain where they can arrive. The source includes orientation and angle of motion in degrees [PAGE 9]. If a ghost location requires a velocity vector orthogonal to the defender’s current momentum within a short \(\tau\), the required lateral acceleration may exceed biomechanical limits. The formal turning constraint can be expressed as

\[
\left\|\Delta \theta\right\| \leq \omega_{\max}\tau,
\]

where \(\Delta\theta\) is required change in movement direction and \(\omega_{\max}\) is maximum feasible angular rate. The paper does not model \(\omega_{\max}\), but the absence of this constraint means the ghost framework can propose locations that are statistically plausible yet mechanically implausible.

A third physical veto concerns omitted players. The source states: “offensive and defensive linemen information are omitted from the provided tracking data” [PAGE 8]. Therefore, any claim about defensive coordination, pass-rush pressure, or pocket collapse is outside the observed data. If a ghost location implies a coverage shell or safety help that depends on linemen or untracked players, the claim is vetoed at the data layer. The metric can evaluate the nearest defender’s catch-moment location, but it cannot evaluate the full 11-player defensive geometry.

A fourth veto concerns scheme and teammate adjustment. The paper’s framework “imposes a simplified representation of American football, as we imagine that we can effectively move the nearest defender around without altering the positions of their teammates” [PAGE 23]. This is a major physical and tactical veto. In real defense, moving one defender changes spacing, leverage, and help responsibilities. The counterfactual of moving only the nearest defender is therefore not a physically complete defensive alternative. It is a partial-equilibrium spatial perturbation.

Weather is a cross-layer veto because the source’s feature table does not include environmental variables such as wind, precipitation, field surface, or temperature [PAGE 30]. Football trajectories, footing, catch probability, and acceleration are affected by weather. If a game’s conditions systematically alter ball flight or player movement, the ghost distribution estimated from league-wide data may not be transportable. The veto condition is: if environmental covariates are correlated with both defender positioning and YAC, omission biases the estimated ghost baseline. The paper does not test this. Therefore, weather-adjusted causal claims are unsupported.

Biomechanical veto also applies to interpretation of “top percentile performance.” The paper says Jaire Alexander’s positioning and trajectory were “a top percentile performance for a defender at this moment in time” [PAGE 17]. This statement is conditional on the ghost distribution and feature set. It does not prove that the defender’s body could have maintained that positioning over the full route, nor that the positioning was caused by skill rather than scheme, receiver route, or quarterback decision. Physical veto: a metric can rank a position as optimal without proving that the player had the biomechanical capacity to occupy it consistently.

A formal proof structure for physical veto is as follows. Suppose the ghost model assigns density \(h(\ell)\) to location \(\ell\). Let \(F(\ell)\) be the physical feasibility indicator, with \(F(\ell)=1\) if \(\ell\) satisfies kinematic constraints from the defender’s observed state and \(F(\ell)=0\) otherwise. A physically admissible ghost expectation is

\[
\mathbb{E}[\delta_{\mathrm{catch}}]_{\mathrm{phys}}
=
\frac{
\int_{\ell} \delta(\ell) h(\ell) F(\ell)\,d\ell
}{
\int_{\ell} h(\ell) F(\ell)\,d\ell
}.
\]

If \(\int h(\ell)(1-F(\ell))d\ell\) is large, the unadjusted ghost baseline is physically contaminated. The paper’s reported \(\mathbb{E}[\delta_{\mathrm{catch}}]\) does not include \(F(\ell)\). Therefore, any cross-layer physical claim must first reweight or filter ghost locations by feasibility. Without that step, the metric may reward or punish defenders for failing to occupy positions they could not physically have reached.

The final veto is temporal. The source’s model uses catch-moment features, except quarterback features at pass release [PAGE 8]. A defender’s earlier positioning may have caused the catch-moment configuration. If the metric rewards or penalizes only the catch-moment location, it can reverse the causal arrow. Physical causality runs from earlier trajectory to later position, but the model evaluates only the later position. Therefore, causal claims about defender skill from catch-moment ghosts require a temporal veto unless supplemented by continuous-time trajectory modeling.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following three counterfactual veto scenarios are verifiable because they rely on observables already present in the paper’s framework or in the cited data ecosystem. They are not betting constructs; they are causal and kinematic checks.

Veto 1: Physically unreachable ghost mass.  
Scenario: The 2D ghost location RFCDE assigns substantial probability to defender locations that are not reachable from the defender’s observed pre-catch state given speed, acceleration, and time. Verification: For each play, compute \(\tau\) from pass release or an earlier event to catch. Using observed speed and acceleration bounds, compute feasible region

\[
\mathcal{F} =
\left\{
\ell :
\left\|\ell - p_{\mathrm{def}}\right\|_2
\leq
s_{\max}\tau + \frac{1}{2}a_{\max}\tau^2
\right\}.
\]

Then calculate the ghost mass outside \(\mathcal{F}\):

\[
M_{\mathrm{infeasible}} =
\int_{\ell \notin \mathcal{F}}
h(\ell \mid X^{-\mathrm{def1}}_{\mathrm{catch}})
\,d\ell.
\]

Veto rule: if \(M_{\mathrm{infeasible}}\) exceeds a pre-registered threshold, the unadjusted ghost counterfactual is rejected for that play. Prediction: plays with high \(M_{\mathrm{infeasible}}\) will show unstable or exaggerated \(\mathbb{E}[\delta_{\mathrm{catch}}]\) values. After filtering to feasible ghost locations, season rankings will compress toward zero, especially for defenders whose observed positioning is far from league-average locations. This veto is verifiable because the paper provides the necessary kinematic fields: speed, acceleration, and coordinates [PAGE 9].

Veto 2: Completion-selection bias veto.  
Scenario: The framework evaluates only completed passes [PAGE 9], but true defensive value includes preventing completions. A defender may receive a poor conditional YAC metric because they allowed a completed pass, even if their coverage reduced completion probability relative to the ghost baseline. Verification: Compare the ghost metric to an all-pass outcome model, such as pass defensive expected points added, which the paper notes includes all pass attempts [PAGE 20]. Let \(C\) be catch indicator, \(Y\) be YAC conditional on catch, and \(V\) be full-pass value. The paper estimates value conditional on \(C=1\), while full-pass value requires

\[
\mathbb{E}[V] =
\mathbb{E}[V \mid C=1]\Pr(C=1) +
\mathbb{E}[V \mid C=0]\Pr(C=0).
\]

Veto rule: if a defender’s conditional ghost metric improves but their all-pass defensive value worsens, the conditional metric is vetoed as a standalone evaluation. Prediction: defenders with tight coverage that induces incompletions but occasionally allows higher conditional YAC will be undervalued by the catch-only metric. Conversely, defenders who allow easy catches but limit YAC may be overvalued. This veto is verifiable by comparing the paper’s metric to outcome-based metrics that include incomplete passes [PAGE 20].

Veto 3: Coverage-assignment misspecification veto.  
Scenario: The nearest defender is not the responsible defender. The paper acknowledges that “by chance a defender that is covering a different receiver may be physically closer than the defender that is actually responsible for covering the receiver” [PAGE 22]. If coverage assignment is misspecified, the metric may attribute offensive value to the wrong defender. Verification: Use coverage labels, route assignments, or a probabilistic assignment model such as the Hidden Markov Model approach mentioned by the authors [PAGE 22]. Let \(A\) be the assignment variable indicating the defender responsible for the receiver. The paper’s metric implicitly uses \(A=\mathrm{nearest}\), but the correct assignment may be \(A\neq\mathrm{nearest}\). Define the assignment-corrected expectation:

\[
\mathbb{E}[\delta_{\mathrm{catch}} \mid A]
=
\int
\left(
\mathrm{EPV}_{\mathrm{catch}} -
\mathrm{EPV}^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{catch}}
\right)
h(\gamma \mid X^{-A}_{\mathrm{catch}})
\,d\gamma.
\]

Veto rule: if the responsible defender differs from the nearest defender in a nontrivial fraction of plays, the nearest-defender metric must be vetoed as a pure individual metric until assignment is modeled. Prediction: after assignment correction, nearest-defender rankings will correlate less with true coverage responsibility, and team-level metrics may remain more stable than individual metrics because team aggregates partially wash out assignment errors. This veto is verifiable because the paper itself identifies the limitation and suggests a modeling path [PAGE 22].

These three vetoes establish a conservative interpretation rule: the NFL Ghosts framework is valid as a catch-moment conditional density baseline, but it must be vetoed when ghost counterfactuals are physically infeasible, when completion selection reverses the sign of full-pass value, or when nearest-defender proximity fails to identify coverage responsibility. The source supports each veto through its own scope statements, data limitations, and validation comparisons.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
