Therefore the uniform solution is vetoed by the joint physical-biomechanical-environmental layer unless certainty holds over the entire horizon, a regime that Knight's own footnotes reject as descriptively absurd [PAGE 132, fn. 2].

Scenario 1: A model assumes a representative agent with ρ(t) ≡ 0 and no uncertainty. Prediction: c(t) = C/T is flat across all t. Veto: Knight's observation of "waves of greater or less period and amplitude" and "blow-outs" [PAGE 132] falsifies flatness. Counterfactual prediction: consumption exhibits periodic spikes whose amplitude is positively correlated with the agent's "spiritedness" and negatively correlated with externally imposed smoothing constraints.

Scenario 2: A model abstracts uncertainty from the future, as the main text does. Prediction: mortality and incapacitation risk do not affect the timing of consumption. Veto: footnote 2 establishes that loss of earning power is more likely than loss of enjoyment capacity and that provision must be made "as far ahead as he was at all likely to live" [PAGE 132, fn. 2]. Counterfactual prediction: when S_y(t) is experimentally lowered (e.g., by raising perceived job-insecurity or health risk), agents shift consumption earlier even if ρ is unchanged.

Scenario 3: A normative model labels present preference ("a bird in the hand") as irrational because time preference alone cannot justify preferring otherwise similar enjoyments [PAGE 132, fn. 1]. Prediction: agents should never systematically prefer present to future consumption when alternatives are equal. Veto: Knight states that "to many, a bird in the hand is worth two or more in the bush" [PAGE 132]. Counterfactual prediction: in a forced-choice task with certain future rewards, a majority of subjects will still select the immediate option at a rate significantly above chance, and this rate will rise when the future is framed as uncertain rather than certain.
