**Gray-Zone Audit**: The text implies a moral judgment — self-sacrifice is superior. In military tactics, Sun Tzu's *The Art of War* emphasizes deception and role specialization, but the spontaneous coordination seen here resembles emergent flocking behavior in starlings — a decentralized system where local interactions produce global coherence.

Scientifically, this mirrors the development of game theory from von Neumann-Morgenstern to Schelling’s focal points. The 1920 context reflects early sports sociology, viewing teams as organic wholes (Durkheimian collectivism). A multi-week trap arises in training: coaches often drill isolated skills (passing, blocking) without integrating high-pressure decision scenarios. Players develop strong individual policies but weak joint policies, leading to coordination failures in live games. This is a "retrocausal" effect: the future outcome (lost game) influences past training choices, but the causal link is obscured by intervening variables like luck. To counteract, simulations must include noisy communication channels and variable teammate reliability, fostering robustness.

Historically, the rise of analytics in baseball (Moneyball) shifted focus from individual metrics to win probability added (WPA). In football, similar metrics (expected points added) could reframe such moments: the value of eliminating a defender may exceed recovering the ball if it guarantees first down. Yet, cultural narratives glorify the touchdown scorer, creating a misalignment between instrumental and expressive values.

Thus, the mind operates as a hierarchical predictor: low-level reflexes handle gross movement, mid-level routines manage routine decisions, and high-level planning intervenes only in novel crises. Failure occurs when layers conflict — e.g., reflex urges to grab ball while plan says to block.
