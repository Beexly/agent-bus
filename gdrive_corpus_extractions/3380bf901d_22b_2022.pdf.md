The source’s vortex example defeats this habit: the field has local potential but nonzero circulation around a loop [PAGE 182]. The counter-trap is to inspect the domain. The source gives the exact theorem: “If F is irrotational on a simply connected E then F = ∇f in E” [PAGE 200]. If E is not simply connected, the theorem does not apply.

The fourth recursive model is the student who treats a parametrized surface as if any fine triangulation preserves area. The source’s Schwarz lantern shows that triangulation can diverge even when triangles become small [PAGE 152]. The counter-trap is to check smoothness of the parametrization and convergence of the metric, not merely mesh size.

The manuscript also models the examiner’s mind. The examiner expects proofs, not examples. The source says, “Checking a statement by showing a few examples is not a proof” [PAGE 16]. It also gives Polya principles: understand the problem, devise a plan, carry out the plan, examine the solution [PAGE 140]. These are recursive checks: the solver must model both the problem and the eventual verification.

A rational crisis policy can be described as follows. At the start of a problem, identify the object: scalar, vector field, curve, surface, form, or graph. Next, identify the operator: dot product, cross product, gradient, curl, divergence, exterior derivative, or boundary. Next, identify hypotheses: differentiability, orientation, compactness, simple connectivity, integrability. Finally, compute a controlled example or counterexample. If time is short, the policy should prioritize hypothesis checks over lengthy computation, because a correct formula applied outside its domain is worse than a partially computed answer.

The counter-trap is not paranoia. It is a structured habit: every theorem has a domain; every integral has hypotheses; every boundary has orientation; every singular point changes the topology. The source repeatedly demonstrates this through false theorems, pathological curves, and discrete models. The learner who internalizes these counter-traps becomes robust under clock pressure because the checks are fast: inspect the formula, inspect the domain, inspect the boundary, inspect units.

Physical veto layer.
Claim: a closed mechanical loop can produce net positive work from a conservative force field without external energy. Let F be a force field. If F = ∇f, then the work along a curve C from A to B is
\[
W = \int_C F\cdot dr = f(B)-f(A).
\]
If C is closed, A = B, so W = 0. The source states the closed-loop property for gradient fields [PAGE 180]. Therefore any claimed positive net work around a closed loop in a conservative field is vetoed. Gravity near Earth’s surface is represented by a potential, for example f(x, y, z) = z in the source’s perpetual-motion discussion [PAGE 185]. The gravitational force is then conservative, and a closed path cannot generate net work.

The source discusses perpetual motion machines and says, “The first law of thermodynamics forbids the existence of such a machine” [PAGE 181]. It also says, “It is an experimental fact that all isolated physical process we know preserve energy” [PAGE 184]. The formal veto is:
\[
E_{\text{overall}}(t_2)-E_{\text{overall}}(t_1)
=
W_{\text{external}} - W_{\text{dissipated}}.
\]
For an isolated system, W_external = 0 and W_dissipated ≥ 0 by the second law, so the overall energy cannot increase. If a machine claims W_net > 0 over a cycle with no external source, the claim contradicts energy conservation.

Magnetic machines require a second veto. Static magnetic fields acting on magnetic dipoles can be derived from potentials in regions without free currents and changing electric fields. If the configuration is static and isolated, no net energy can be extracted over a closed cycle. If the field is produced by electromagnets, the energy comes from the power supply. The source’s magnet machine is analyzed as an illusion of non-conservative push [PAGE 185]. The veto is: either the field is conservative and the loop integral vanishes, or the field is externally powered and the energy source must be included.

Biomechanical veto layer.
Claim: a human or animal can move cyclically inside a conservative field and emerge with net mechanical energy without metabolic intake. Let mechanical energy be E_mech = K + U. For a conservative force F = −∇U,
\[
\frac{dE_{\text{mech}}}{dt}=0
\]
absent nonconservative forces. Muscles can add energy, but only by consuming metabolic free energy. The source’s chain-rule interpretation says that power is force times velocity and that the rate of change of potential energy along a path is the dot product of force and velocity [PAGE 95]. If muscles perform positive work, internal chemical energy decreases. Over a closed cycle with no intake, the net mechanical work cannot exceed zero after dissipation. Therefore a biomechanical perpetual-motion claim is vetoed by conservation of energy and thermodynamic accounting.

A more formal inequality is
\[
W_{\text{muscle}} \le -\Delta E_{\text{metabolic}} - Q_{\text{heat}},
\]
where Q_heat ≥ 0. If ΔE_metabolic = 0, then W_muscle ≤ 0. Thus no net positive mechanical work can be produced by a closed biological cycle without energy input.

Weather veto layer.
Claim: a device can extract unlimited work from wind or atmospheric motion without external forcing. The source states that wind energy is driven by external sources, especially solar energy, which heats different parts of the Earth’s surface [PAGE 184]. The formal veto is an energy-budget inequality:
\[
P_{\text{extracted}} \le P_{\text{solar forcing}} - P_{\text{radiative loss}} - P_{\text{dissipation}}.
\]
A local turbine can reduce local kinetic energy or alter pressure gradients, but it cannot create energy beyond the atmospheric reservoir. If the device claims to increase the energy of the atmosphere while extracting work, it violates the first law. If it claims to reduce entropy while isolated, it violates the second law. The weather layer therefore vetoes any self-sustaining atmospheric motor.

Geometric veto layer.
Claim: a non-conservative loop integral can be produced by a field that is curl-free everywhere on a punctured plane and still admit a global potential. The source’s vortex field is locally a gradient but has loop integral 2π around the origin [PAGE 182]. The theorem says that irrotational implies gradient only on simply connected domains [PAGE 200]. Therefore the claim is vetoed unless the domain is simply connected or the singularity is included with additional structure.

Adjudication rule:
1. If the field is a gradient on the full domain, every closed-loop work integral is zero.
2. If the loop integral is nonzero, either the domain is not simply connected, the field is singular, or an external source is doing work.
3. If the system is isolated and cyclic, net positive work is vetoed by energy conservation.
4. If the system is biological, net positive work is vetoed without metabolic input.
5. If the system is meteorological, net positive work is vetoed without solar or external forcing.
This adjudication uses the source’s own operators: gradient fields, closed-loop integrals, curl, divergence, and simply connected domains.

Veto scenario 1: false arc-length claim.
Counterfactual claim: “The circumference of the unit circle is 8.”

\[
L = \int_0^{2\pi} \|r'(t)\|\,dt.
\]
For r(t) = [cos t, sin t], r′(t) = [−sin t, cos t], so
\[
\|r'(t)\| = \sqrt{\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t}=1.
\]
Therefore
\[
L = \int_0^{2\pi} 1\,dt = 2\pi.
\]
Prediction: the verifier rejects the claim and returns 2π. If the policy proposes 8, the reward is negative because the answer violates the proven arc-length theorem [PAGE 44].
