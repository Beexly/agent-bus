The source is Astfalck, Bertolacci, and Cripps, “Evaluating probabilistic forecasts for maritime engineering operations,” Data-Centric Engineering 4:e15 (2023), 18 pages. The GSE/GSI operator here is not a single accuracy number; it is a prediction-space score plus a horizon-indexed adjudication rule. The multi-hop derivation is as follows. First, the paper defines a prediction space as the joint object \((F,y^*)\), where \(F\) is a forecast CDF and \(y^*\) is the realized observation, conditioned on information \(A\); the ideal forecast is the true predictive distribution \(G=p(y^*|A)\) [PAGE 4]. Second, a scoring rule \(S(F,y^*)\) is proper when its expectation under \(G\), \(S(F,G)=E_G[S(F,y^*)]\), is minimized at \(F=G\) [PAGE 4]. Third, because operational models produce many forecasts indexed by time \(t\), the paper aggregates to model skill \(\bar S_F=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^n S(F_t,y_t^*)\) [PAGE 5]. Fourth, because maritime decisions have different lead times, the paper replaces the scalar aggregation with a horizon-indexed skill \(\bar S_F(h)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^n S(F_{t,h},y_{t,h}^*)\), where \(h\) is measured in hours into the future and \(h=0\) is the nowcast [PAGE 5]. Fifth, the paper selects a three-rule panel: squared error (SE) for the predictive mean, Dawid–Sebastiani score (DSS) for mean plus covariance/tails, and energy score (ES) for the whole predictive body [PAGE 6]. Sixth, the case study applies this to surface winds at Australia’s North–West Shelf, comparing a deterministic numerical weather prediction (NWP) model, a hybrid model-output-statistics (MOS) model, and a data-only vector autoregressive (VAR) model over \(h\in[0,48]\) hours [PAGE 8, PAGE 12]. The reported result is that the VAR model scores best for the first three prediction horizons, while the MOS model scores best thereafter [PAGE 14, PAGE 15].

The gray-zone audit is where the source needs care. The paper’s industrial critique is exact: “The standard practice in industry is to evaluate these forecasts by distance-based metrics (such as squared or absolute error)” and “Under a distance-based metric, forecasts with identical point measures (e.g., mean) but different uncertainties (e.g., variance) receive the same score” [PAGE 2]. That is the core motivation. But the panel is not self-executing. First, the DSS requires an invertible covariance; the paper explicitly states that “the DSS cannot be computed for \(h=0\) for the VAR model as the variance of \(F_{t,0}\) is zero and so the inverse is not defined” [PAGE 14]. Second, the empirical ES and CRPS require genuine random samples; the paper warns that ensemble members “must be genuine random samples and not an ad-hoc collection of discrete forecasting models” [PAGE 6]. Third, the Diebold–Mariano variance estimate assumes independent forecast cases, and the paper says “If there is known autocorrelation in the forecast cases, this should be dealt with in the estimation of \(\hat\sigma_n\)” [PAGE 6]. Fourth, the paper’s probability-of-best Monte Carlo equation is printed as \(1\{S(F_t,y_t^*)\gt S(G_t,y_t^*)\}\), which estimates the probability that \(F\) has a larger score than \(G\); since lower is better, this is not literally “probability F is best” unless the indicator is reversed or a minimum-over-models definition is used [PAGE 6, PAGE 15]. Fifth, the validation is one year after one training year [PAGE 9], so seasonal monsoon structure and 24-hour autocorrelation can dominate a single aggregate. These are not fatal objections; they are veto conditions that must be checked before the score is used for model selection.

The operational inference is therefore: use the panel, use horizon-specific scores, and veto any probabilistic claim whose probabilistic object is invalid. The source gives the formal scaffolding but does not itself provide a vessel-response threshold linking wind-score improvements to heading, gap resonance, or crew/operation safety; that cross-layer mapping must be supplied separately.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The paper’s formal operators are defined over a prediction space \((F,y^*)\), where \(F\) is a forecast distribution and \(y^*\) is the observation. The true predictive distribution is \(G=p(y^*|A)\), and the expected score is

\[
S(F,G)=E_G[S(F,y^*)]=\int S(F,y^*)\,dG(y^*).
\]

The paper states propriety as \(S(G,G)\leq S(F,G)\), with equality only for strict propriety [PAGE 4]. In words quoted from the source: “the expected score is minimized when the true predictive distribution is forecast” [PAGE 4]. For point forecasts, a scoring function \(s(f^*,y^*)\) is consistent if

\[
E_G[s(T,Y)]\leq E_G[s(x,Y)]
\]

for any real \(x\), where \(T\) is a point summary such as the mean or median [PAGE 5]. Model skill is

\[
\bar S_F=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}S(F_t,y_t^*),
\]

and horizon-indexed skill is

\[
\bar S_F(h)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}S(F_{t,h},y_{t,h}^*),
\]

with \(h\) the prediction horizon in hours; \(h=0\) is the nowcast [PAGE 5]. The Diebold–Mariano statistic is

\[
z_n=\frac{\bar S_F-\bar S_G}{\hat\sigma_n},
\qquad
\hat\sigma_n=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left[S(F_t,y_t^*)-S(G_t,y_t^*)\right]^2,
\]

assuming independent forecast cases [PAGE 6]. The Monte Carlo exceedance estimate is

\[
p(S(F,y^*)\gt S(G,y^*))\approx
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}1\{S(F_t,y_t^*)\gt S(G_t,y_t^*)\}.
\]

The three selected scoring rules are:

\[
\mathrm{SE}(\mu_t,y_t^*)=(\mu_t-y_t^*)^\top(\mu_t-y_t^*),
\]

with \(\mu_t=E[F_t]\). If wind components are in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), SE has units \(\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}\). The source notes that “a good score can be achieved even if the forecast has zero or infinite variance” [PAGE 7].

\[
\mathrm{DSS}((\mu_t,\Sigma_t),y_t^*)
=
\log|\Sigma_t|
+
(\mu_t-y_t^*)^\top\Sigma_t^{-1}(\mu_t-y_t^*).
\]

The first term penalizes high variance or complex covariance structure; the second is the squared Mahalanobis distance [PAGE 7]. With \(\Sigma_t\) in \((\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}})^2\), the log-determinant is only dimensionally clean after normalization by a reference covariance; the paper does not explicitly normalize it. The DSS is undefined when \(\Sigma_t\) is singular or non-positive-definite. The paper’s own example is the VAR model at \(h=0\), where the predictive variance is zero [PAGE 14].

For univariate forecasts,

\[
\mathrm{CRPS}(F_t,y_t^*)
=
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(F_t(z)-1\{y_t^*\lt z\}\right)^2dz
=
E_{F_t}|Y_t-y_t^*|
-
\frac{1}{2}E_{F_t}|Y_t-Y_t'|,
\]

with units of \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) [PAGE 7]. The empirical sample version is

\[
\widehat{\mathrm{CRPS}}
=
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}|Y_{t,j}-y_t^*|
-
\frac{1}{2m^2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}|Y_{t,j}-Y_{t,k}|,
\]

where \(Y_{t,j},Y_{t,k}\sim F_t\) [PAGE 8]. The multivariate energy score is

\[
\mathrm{ES}(F_t,y_t^*)
=
E_{F_t}\|Y_t-y_t^*\|_2
-
\frac{1}{2}E_{F_t}\|Y_t-Y_t'\|_2,
\]

with empirical form

\[
\widehat{\mathrm{ES}}
=
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|Y_{t,j}-y_t^*\|_2
-
\frac{1}{2m^2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\|Y_{t,j}-Y_{t,k}\|_2.
\]

The ES and CRPS have units of \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) for wind vectors; they penalize error linearly and are therefore less tail-sensitive than the DSS [PAGE 8].

The case-study model operators are also explicit. The MOS model is

\[
y_{t+h}=\beta_{0,h}+\beta_{1,h}f_{t,h}+\beta_{2,h}f_{t-6,h+6}+\varepsilon_{t,h},
\qquad
\varepsilon_{t,h}\sim N(0,\Sigma_h),
\]

with a \(2\times1\) intercept, \(2\times2\) scaling matrices, and horizon-specific covariance [PAGE 18]. The VAR model is

\[
y_t=\mu+\sum_{i=1}^{p}(\Gamma_i\odot\Phi_i)(y_{t-i}-\mu)+\varepsilon_t,
\qquad
\varepsilon_t\sim N(0,\Sigma),
\]

with \(p=24\) chosen because the partial autocorrelation plots suggest roughly 24 hours of temporal correlation [PAGE 18]. The stochastic selection matrices \(\Gamma_i\) have Bernoulli elements and are intended to promote parsimony [PAGE 18].

Breakdown and singularity regimes are concrete. DSS requires \(\Sigma_t\succ0\); singular or degenerate covariance vetoes the DSS. Empirical ES/CRPS require random draws from \(F_t\); non-random ensemble members destroy propriety and can collapse ES to absolute error. ES/CRPS require finite first moments; heavy-tailed forecasts can make sample estimates unstable. Diebold–Mariano inference requires independent score differentials or a corrected dependence structure; autocorrelated forecast cases invalidate the printed variance estimate. Horizon aggregation can hide the fact that one model is better at \(h\leq3\) and another at \(h\gt3\). Measurement error is included in the forecast error because the models fit measurements directly [PAGE 12]; if instrument error is large, the score is not purely a forecast-model score.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is a retrocausal chain from mid-twentieth-century scoring theory to a 2023 operational panel. The paper situates proper scoring rules with Winkler (1969) and Gneiting and Katzfuss (2014) [PAGE 3], and it builds directly on Gneiting, Balabdaoui, and Raftery (2007) and Gneiting and Raftery (2007) [PAGE 3]. Prediction spaces come from Murphy and Winkler (1987) and Gneiting and Ranjan (2013) [PAGE 4]. The CRPS lineage is Matheson and Winkler (1976) and Székely and Rizzo (2005) [PAGE 7]. The DSS lineage is Dawid and Sebastiani (1999) [PAGE 7]. The Diebold–Mariano test is Diebold and Mariano (1995) [PAGE 5]. The MOS lineage is Glahn and Lowry (1972) [PAGE 3]. The VAR selection representation is Korobilis (2013), with parsimony concerns from Jochmann, Koop, and Strachan (2010) [PAGE 18]. This is “retrocausal” because a 2023 evaluation panel retrospectively assigns quality to forecasts generated from a 2017–2019 archive: the later statistical frame determines which historical model was “best,” and the choice of panel determines the narrative. The paper’s training window is July 17, 2017 to July 17, 2018, and its validation window is July 17, 2018 to July 19, 2019 [PAGE 9]. The prevailing seasonal wind pattern is the Indo–Australian monsoon, “predominantly from the South–West in summer months and East in winter months” [PAGE 9]. The 24-hour autocorrelation is not incidental: the paper says the ACF and pACF plots “suggest an approximately 24-hr auto-regressive (AR) order in both the easting and the northing components” [PAGE 11].

The multi-week traps follow from that lineage. A single-week or single-regime operational trial can reward a model that has learned the current monsoon phase rather than the forecast physics. A multi-week trial with autocorrelated weather will also pseudo-replicate: \(n\) hourly scores are not \(n\) independent draws, so a Diebold–Mariano test that assumes independence can overstate significance. The paper warns that known autocorrelation “should be dealt with in the estimation of \(\hat\sigma_n\)” [PAGE 6]. Another trap is validation-window reuse: if the one-year validation period is inspected repeatedly while choosing among SE, DSS, and ES, the panel becomes a selection device rather than an out-of-sample test. A third trap is conflating deterministic NWP correction with probabilistic calibration: MOS corrects “persistent scaling or rotation errors” [PAGE 12], but correction does not by itself prove calibrated uncertainty. A fourth trap is overfitting the VAR: the paper chooses \(p=24\) from the pACF and uses stochastic selection to prevent overfitting [PAGE 18], but a 24-hour lag structure in a two-year record can still absorb seasonal regime information. The correct retrocausal reading is therefore not “the 2023 panel proves one model is universally better”; it is “the panel proves horizon-dependent superiority under a particular archive, with dependence and seasonality explicitly in view.”

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis habit regression is the operator’s fallback under time pressure to a deterministic, distance-based mental model: take the NWP line or the forecast mean, compare it to observed wind, and act. The paper identifies exactly why that habit is dangerous: distance metrics give “the same score” to forecasts with identical means but different variances [PAGE 2]. In a crisis, the operator may also collapse the prediction horizon to the immediate nowcast. That is rational for thruster activation, because the paper says “immediate activation of thrusters to stabilize vessel motions requires knowledge of immediate winds” [PAGE 8]. But it is a regression when applied to offloading decisions, because “go/no–go decisions for offloading of vessel product require knowledge of the winds for up to 48 hr ahead of time” [PAGE 8]. The crisis habit is therefore not just a preference for point forecasts; it is a horizon collapse.

The counter-trap is the statistical modeler’s response: replace the single RMSE habit with a three-score panel and horizon-indexed skill. The panel is designed so that SE assesses the mean, DSS assesses the tails, and ES assesses the body [PAGE 6, PAGE 14]. This counter-trap works because it forces the operator to ask which failure mode matters: “critical applications where outliers must be rigorously quantified should focus on DSS, whereas more routine operations that do not need to be risk adverse may be better assessed by SE” [PAGE 14]. But the counter-trap can itself become a game. If the modeler knows the operator will select on DSS, the modeler may overfit tails; if the operator selects on ES, the modeler may optimize the body and underweight extreme events. The paper’s safeguard is the probability-of-best calculation: “Definitive results as to the performance of competing models require (1) a lower skill (i.e., better performance on average) and (2) a high probability of best performance” [PAGE 15]. That is a recursive check on the counter-trap: average superiority is not enough if the individual-score variance dominates.

The clock is the third layer. The operator’s belief about model reliability is not constant over \(h\). At \(h=0\), the relevant object is the nowcast and the immediate wind vector; at \(h=24\), it is the same time tomorrow; at \(h=48\), it is the offloading decision window [PAGE 5, PAGE 8]. The modeler’s belief about the operator’s clock should change model selection: the VAR model is best for the first three prediction horizons, while the MOS model is best thereafter [PAGE 14, PAGE 15]. A theory-of-mind failure occurs when the modeler reports one aggregate skill and the operator hears one universal ranking. The correct recursive statement is: “For your decision at this horizon, use this score panel and this probability-of-best; do not transfer the \(h=0\) ranking to \(h=48\).” The paper does not model feedback from thruster use or heading changes back into the wind data, so that recursive loop remains a gray zone rather than a measured result.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical veto begins with the forecast object. A probabilistic wind forecast is admissible only if its covariance is positive-definite where the DSS is used, its CDF or sample representation is valid, and its vector components are in a consistent coordinate system. The paper translates polar wind coordinates into rectangular eastings and northings to ease statistical modeling and states that the scores “score equivalently over the polar and rectangular coordinates (provided with equivalent statistical inference)” [PAGE 9]. That equivalence is a physical-coordinate condition: if the rotation is wrong or the scaling matrices are unconstrained, the MOS model can produce a statistically lower score while still misrepresenting the wind vector. The paper notes that “Physics-based forecasts often have persistent scaling or rotation errors” [PAGE 12]; the MOS model is explicitly built to correct those errors. A cross-layer veto should therefore check covariance conditioning, determinant sign, and rotation consistency before accepting a score improvement.

The biomechanical/operational veto is indirect but important. The paper says surface winds, with surface currents, are “a major forcing component on weathervaning structures and hence are one of the main determinants of vessel heading” [PAGE 8]. It further states that “small changes in wind speed and direction can have large effects on vessel heading” and that this “can lead to problematic effects such as gap resonance … and severe vessel motions such as resonant roll and heave” [PAGE 9]. Therefore a scoring-rule improvement is not automatically an operational improvement. If a model reduces SE by a small amount but increases the probability of a heading error that crosses an operational threshold, the physical/biomechanical layer vetoes adoption. The paper provides one magnitude reference: at the 48-hour horizon the VAR model is “on average approximately \(1\,\mathrm{ms^{-1}}\) worse than the numerical model, which in the order of magnitudes of expected wind behavior at the NWS is not large” [PAGE 14]. That is a useful scale check, but it is not a heading-response threshold. The paper scores wind, not vessel motion, so the biomechanical veto must be supplied by a separate response model or operational limit.

The weather veto is seasonal and dependence-based. The validation period spans a full year, but the prevailing monsoon reverses from south-westerly in summer to easterly in winter [PAGE 9]. A model that wins on annual average but fails in one monsoon phase is not automatically admissible for a campaign that operates in that phase. The 24-hour autocorrelation also means that hourly score differences are not independent [PAGE 11]. A valid cross-layer adjudication should require: (i) \(\Sigma_{t,h}\succ0\) for every DSS use; (ii) lower \(\bar S_F(h)\) across the relevant decision window, not just in an all-horizon average; (iii) high probability of best performance at that horizon; (iv) no physical-coordinate or covariance violation; and (v) no contradiction with the wind-to-heading or wind-to-motion operational limit. If any of these fails, the model is vetoed for that operation even if its aggregate score looks favorable.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: degenerate covariance at the nowcast. Intervention: compute the DSS for the VAR model at \(h=0\) using its posterior predictive covariance \(\Sigma_{t,0}\). Verifiable check: the minimum eigenvalue of \(\Sigma_{t,0}\) is zero or below numerical tolerance. The paper already reports the failure: “the DSS cannot be computed for \(h=0\) for the VAR model as the variance of \(F_{t,0}\) is zero and so the inverse is not defined” [PAGE 14]. Prediction: the DSS is undefined; if one regularizes with \(\Sigma_{t,0}+\epsilon I\), the score depends strongly on \(\epsilon\), and the ranking at \(h=0\) is unstable. Veto: do not use DSS at \(h=0\); use SE or ES, or exclude the nowcast from DSS-based selection.

Scenario 2: deterministic NWP masquerading as an ensemble. Intervention: set all \(m\) forecast samples equal to the deterministic NWP value \(f_t\), so \(Y_{t,j}=f_t\) for every \(j\). Verifiable check: sample variance is zero and the number of distinct forecast values is one. Prediction: the empirical scores collapse to point scores:

\[
\widehat{\mathrm{CRPS}}=|f_t-y_t^*|,
\qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{ES}}=\|f_t-y_t^*\|_2,
\]

because the second terms in Equations (10) and (12) vanish. The score then rewards only absolute error and cannot reward calibration or sharpness. This directly matches the paper’s warning that ensemble members “must be genuine random samples and not an ad-hoc collection of discrete forecasting models” [PAGE 6]. Veto: reject any claim of probabilistic skill based on a degenerate or non-random ensemble.

Scenario 3: autocorrelated score differentials with iid Diebold–Mariano inference. Intervention: compute \(d_t=S(F_t,y_t^*)-S(G_t,y_t^*)\) over hourly forecast cases, estimate \(\hat\sigma_n\) by the printed Equation (5), and compare it with a block bootstrap that preserves the roughly 24-hour autocorrelation. Verifiable check: the ACF of \(d_t\) shows nonzero autocorrelation at hourly lags, consistent with the paper’s statement that the wind data “suggest an approximately 24-hr auto-regressive (AR) order” [PAGE 11]. Prediction: the iid \(\hat\sigma_n\) is too small, the absolute \(z_n\) is inflated, and the p-value is anti-conservative; model preference can flip when dependence is respected. The paper itself says, “If there is known autocorrelation in the forecast cases, this should be dealt with in the estimation of \(\hat\sigma_n\)” [PAGE 6]. Veto: require block bootstrap, HAC covariance, or horizon-stratified testing before accepting a Diebold–Mariano preference.
