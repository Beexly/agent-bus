<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
When facing a crisis, agents do not simply recalibrate their probability estimates `f`; they regress through a predictable hierarchy of cognitive defenses tied to the two judgments. The ultimate danger is mistaking a breakdown in the second judgment `g` (meta-confidence) for a mere adjustment of the first `f` (the estimate itself).

**Stage 1: Denial & Anchoring.** The agent clings to the original `f` even as evidence contradicts it. This is an over-investment in the first judgment, refusing to update. It represents a failure of the feedback loop from outcome to `f`.

**Stage 2: Overconfidence Re-calibration.** The agent updates `f` drastically (e.g., "the market will correct") but maintains an absurdly high `g` in this new, extreme estimate. This is the hubris trap. They have moved from one point estimate to another without engaging the second judgment properly.

**Stage 3: Second-Order Collapse.** Here, the crisis hits `g` directly. The agent realizes their entire classification scheme is suspect. This can manifest as paralysis (unable to form any `g`) or as a frantic search for a new, more granular classification system (lowering the "bars" as Knight suggests). This is the most dangerous stage for a counter-trap. A predator (another agent) can exploit this by presenting a new, compelling narrative that offers a high `g` for a novel `f`, luring the distressed agent into a false sense of restored control.

**Stage 4: Introspective Loop & Clock Awareness.** The mature agent recognizes that the instability is in `g`, not `f`. They begin to measure the *rate of change* of `g` across different classes. This introduces a temporal dimension—a clock—to the Knightian model. By monitoring how quickly their meta-confidence erodes in one class versus another, they can anticipate which domains of experience are becoming truly uncertain (profitable/risky) and which are simply noisy. The counter-trap is to misinterpret a temporary dip in `g` as a permanent shift, leading to premature capitulation. The key is distinguishing between a failure of *knowledge* (adjust `f`) and a failure of *judgment about knowledge* (rebuild `g`).

**Operator 1: The Primary Estimator (First Judgment)**
`f: C → Δ(Θ)`
where `Δ(Θ)` is the simplex of probability distributions over outcomes `Θ`. For a given class `c ∈ C`, `f(c)` produces a subjective probability vector. This corresponds to "the formation of an estimate" and "a probability judgment" from [PAGE 240].

**Units:** Dimensionless probabilities `[0,1]`. Assumptions: The classification `C` is non-empty and fixed for the estimation. The mapping `f` is continuous within a class.

**Operator 2: The Meta-Evaluator (Second Judgment)**
`g: f(C) × C → [0,1]`
This maps a primary estimate `f(c)` and its originating class `c` to a scalar confidence level. It is "the estimation of its value." This captures the "degree of confidence that a certain outcome will be realized" mentioned in [PAGE 240]. Note that `g` can depend on the class context, allowing for domain-specific calibration of confidence.

**Formal Model of Action Selection**
An agent chooses an action `a` from a set `A` to maximize expected utility weighted by meta-confidence:
`a* = argmax_{a∈A} ∑_{θ∈Θ} U(a,θ) · g(f(c), c) · P_f(θ|c)`
where `P_f(θ|c)` is the probability from `f(c)` for outcome `θ`, and `U` is the von Neumann-Morgenstern utility function. This operationalizes Knight's claim that action follows upon "the amount... [of] opinion as it does upon the favor[ability]" [PAGE 240].

**Singularity/Breakdown Regimes**
1.  **Unclassifiable Novelty (`c = ∅`):** When an event occurs that cannot be placed in any existing class `c ∈ C`, the operator `f` is undefined. The entire decision calculus breaks down. This is Knightian uncertainty par excellence.
2.  **Zero Meta-Confidence (`g = 0`):** If `g(f(c), c) = 0` for all actions, the objective function is zero regardless of `f`. The agent is paralyzed, as no probability estimate, however precise, holds any perceived value.
