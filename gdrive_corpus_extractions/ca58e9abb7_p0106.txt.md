Crisis habit regression vs counter-trap vs clock.

Tolman’s description implies a nested theory of mind. At level 0, the organism emits a habitual act contingent on the presence of the expected color. At level 1, when the color “fails to be present,” the organism registers a contingency violation; the habitual act regresses—extinguishes or is suppressed—and behavior “give[s] way to some other behavior on the immediately subsequent occasions” [PAGE 1]. This is crisis habit regression: the dominant response hierarchy collapses under discriminandum withdrawal, and a lower-probability alternative behavior is recruited. At level 2, the experimenter models the organism’s model: by varying λ across the interchangeability bounds (a) and (b), the experimenter probes the organism’s implicit expectation and forces the organism to discriminate “this specific color” from its near neighbors.

Counter-trap. The experimenter can set a trap by presenting a wavelength λ′ that lies just outside the true equivalence class [λ]_Δ but within the organism’s loose tolerance ε_d, baiting the organism into treating a non-substitutable stimulus as substitutable. If the organism responds without disruption, the counter-trap reveals that ε_d was overestimated or that the organism is using a lower-dimensional cue (e.g., brightness rather than hue). If disruption occurs, the boundary of Δ is located and the organism’s expectation is falsified. The trap is recursive because the experimenter is manipulating the organism’s belief about the experimenter’s contingency.

Clock. Tolman imposes a strict temporal contiguity constraint: disruption must appear “on the immediately subsequent occasions” [PAGE 1], implying a short decay constant τ_decay for the expectation trace. If the probe is delayed beyond τ_decay, the observed behavior change may reflect forgetting, circadian drift, or spontaneous recovery rather than discriminandum withdrawal. Thus the crisis regression is valid only at τ ≈ 0; the counter-trap must be delivered within the clock window; and any protocol that spaces substitutions by minutes, hours, or days confounds the discriminandum effect with temporal-decay effects, invalidating the inference that color per se governed the behavior.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical/biomechanical/weather veto proof.

A wavelength-class [λ]_Δ is admissible only if it survives three independent veto layers.

Physical layer. The discriminandum is defined relative to a fixed spectral power distribution E(λ). A veto is triggered if (i) radiometric calibration drifts by more than 1 nm, (ii) two nominally identical monochromatic settings differ in bandwidth or stray-light contamination, or (iii) illuminant chromaticity shifts enough that CIEDE2000 between cone-excitation vectors exceeds ~1.0 (approximate JND). In that case the same λ produces different cone excitations, so wavelength cannot be the controlled variable; the veto replaces λ with a cone-contrast invariant such as Δ(L*,a*,b*) or a luminance-normalized opponent ratio.

Biomechanical layer. Human and animal color vision is eccentricity-dependent: foveal vision is approximately trichromatic, while peripheral vision is effectively dichromatic because S-cone density drops outside the fovea. Pupil size, accommodation, lens yellowing, saccadic suppression, and head fixation versus free viewing all change the effective retinal illuminance and spectral weighting. If the same λ is discriminable at foveal fixation but confusable in periphery (or vice versa), the biomechanical layer vetoes any wavelength-only class that does not specify viewing geometry, retinal locus, and pupil state.

Weather layer. Daylight correlated color temperature spans roughly 2,500 K (tungsten/sunrise) to 10,000+ K (clear skylight), and overcast versus clear conditions shift short-wavelength power via Rayleigh scattering. Temperature dependence of photoreceptor kinetics (Q₁₀ effects) and of interference filters or LED sources further alters the effective spectrum. Humidity and thermal drift can change filter transmission. A discriminandum class established under one sky condition vetoes itself if, under another weather state, the same physical λ yields cone excitations separated by more than the JND threshold.

Veto proof formalization: ∃ p ∈ {physical, biomechanical, weather} such that D_o(λ, λ′; E ∘ p) ≥ ε_d ⇒ [λ]_Δ is vetoed as a wavelength-class and must be redefined as an invariant I(λ) = f(cone excitations, geometry, illuminant) that remains stable across p. Without passing this three-layer veto, Tolman’s “specific color” is under-specified: the behavior may be controlled by radiance geometry, adaptation state, or illuminant spectrum rather than by wavelength per se.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR veto scenarios and predictions.

Scenario 1 (within-observer wavelength JND). Protocol: normal trichromatic human, fixed D65 illuminant, monochromatic λ = 580 nm trained as the expected discriminandum; immediate probe with λ′ = 585 nm. Prediction: if 5 nm lies below the individual’s foveal wavelength JND, then D_o < ε_d and no disruption occurs; λ and λ′ are mutually substitutable and belong to the same [λ]_Δ. Veto criterion: if disruption occurs on the immediately subsequent occasion [PAGE 1], the class is vetoed; the implied diagnosis is that ε_d was set too narrow, monochromator bandwidth is excessive, or the observer has atypical photopigment density. Verification: measure the wavelength discrimination psychometric function in a separate forced-choice session and confirm that the 5-nm pair falls below threshold.

Scenario 2 (metameric illuminant veto). Protocol: two physically distinct surface reflectances matched under illuminant E₁ (tungsten, ≈2850 K) and presented under E₂ (daylight, ≈6500 K) at equal luminance. Prediction: the surfaces are metamers under E₁ but produce different cone excitations under E₂ (CIEDE2000 > 1.0), so an organism trained under E₁ shows disruption under E₂ despite identical nominal color appearance. Veto criterion: behavior disruption under E₂ vetoes any wavelength-only discriminandum; Δ must be defined in cone-excitation space, not λ. Verification: measure reflectance spectra spectrophotometrically, compute cone excitations and CIEDE2000 under both illuminants, and confirm >1.0 difference under E₂.

Scenario 3 (cross-species polyhedron veto). Protocol: human vs. dog (dichromat) trained on the same red–green wavelength pair that humans treat as non-substitutable. Prediction: the human color-pyramid class does not port to the dog; the dog’s dichromatic confusion axis collapses some human-distinguishable red–green pairs into one class while splitting pairs along a yellow–blue axis that humans lump. Veto criterion: if the symmetric difference [λ]_h △ [λ]_d ≠ ∅, the human discriminandum class is vetoed for the canine organism. Verification: run go/no-go generalization gradients for both species across λ, fit D_h and D_d, and show the boundary locations differ beyond inter-subject variability.
