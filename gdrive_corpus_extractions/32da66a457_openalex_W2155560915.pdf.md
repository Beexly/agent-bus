<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical acoustic veto. The source’s detector is a band-limited energy detector. Let the received whistle pressure at the microphone have band energy
\[

\]

\[

\]
and air absorption adds a frequency-dependent term \(\alpha(f)r\) in dB. If the resulting band signal-to-noise ratio is below the empirical operating point, the event is physically unobservable by this detector. This is a physical veto: absence of detected energy is not proof that no whistle occurred; it is proof that the received band energy did not cross the tuned threshold.

Biomechanical veto. A referee whistle is a fluid-jet cavity resonator. Its acoustic output depends on air pressure, flow, mouth geometry, whistle geometry, and cavity resonance. If the fundamental or dominant harmonic lies outside \(3750\)–\(4100\,\text{Hz}\), or if the blown duration is shorter than the decision clock, the detector is biomechanically vetoed even if a human hears a whistle. The paper’s stated duration range is “normally 0.5 to 3.5 seconds” [PAGE 4], but “normally” is not “always.” A weak, single-beat, low-intensity whistle is exactly the kind of event the annotation quality note removes [PAGE 5]. Therefore the detector cannot adjudicate referee intent or effort; it can only detect a sufficiently strong, sufficiently long, sufficiently in-band acoustic event.

Weather veto. Wind, rain, temperature, and crowd noise alter the channel. Temperature changes the speed of sound approximately as
\[
c\approx20.05\sqrt{T_K}\ \text{m/s},
\]
and a cavity resonance scales with \(c\), so a temperature change shifts the whistle frequency. Wind produces low-frequency turbulence and can add broadband noise; rain adds broadband high-frequency noise; crowd cheers add nonstationary masking. The paper acknowledges that the American football video “has high background noise from the audience cheers and applauses, excited commentary and various environmental sounds” [PAGE 4]. A weather or crowd condition that raises the noise floor in \(3750\)–\(4100\,\text{Hz}\) is a cross-layer veto. The detector’s output should therefore be interpreted as “in-band energy exceeded the empirical threshold,” not as “a referee whistle certainly occurred.”
