1. Reaction time:
\[
T_{\mathrm{react},i}=t_{\mathrm{press},i}-t_{\mathrm{flash},i}\quad [\mathrm{s}]
\]
Assumption: both timestamps are on one monotone clock. Breakdown/singularity: undefined for a miss or no press; right-censored at trial end; negative values indicate clock desynchronization; precision is bounded below by the frame period and input latency. The paper reports random flash intervals of “5–15s” [PAGE4] and trial counts “typically 5 or 10” [PAGE3].

2. Frame period and display update:
\[
\Delta t_f=\frac{1}{f},\qquad f\ge 75\ \mathrm{Hz}\ \Rightarrow\ \Delta t_f\le 0.0133\ \mathrm{s}
\]
This follows from “render at 75fps or higher frame rate” [PAGE3]. Breakdown: \(f\to 0\) gives \(\Delta t_f\to\infty\); \(f=0\) is undefined. The source does not report measured latency, so this is a lower bound on quantization, not a measured end-to-end delay.

3. Accumulator score rate:
\[
R=\frac{N_{\mathrm{hit}}}{T_{\mathrm{limit}}},\qquad T_{\mathrm{limit}}\in\{30,60\}\ \mathrm{s}
\]
where \(N_{\mathrm{hit}}\) is the number of correctly struck targets and \(R\) has units \(\mathrm{s}^{-1}\). The paper says the user must “strike as many targets as possible during a set time limit of 30/60 seconds” [PAGE3]. Breakdown: \(T_{\mathrm{limit}}=0\) is undefined; the score counts hits, not errors unless separately logged; if a bounce or repeated press is counted, \(N_{\mathrm{hit}}\) is not a clean count of target acquisitions.

4. Inter-press interval:
\[
\Delta t_j=t_{j+1}-t_j\quad [\mathrm{s}]
\]

Under the assumption of uniform independent target choice with \(K=12\), \(H_{\mathrm{item}}=\log_2 12\approx3.585\) bits per item, and a length-\(L\) sequence carries \(L\log_2 12\) bits. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive model has at least three nested beliefs: the user believes the artefact is a game or a test; the designer believes the user needs guidance; the clinician believes game performance transfers to rehabilitation. Under frustration, users regress to the physical board’s affordances: “I found it more difficult to hit the buttons” [PAGE6], “The physical board was more comfortable and easier to use” [PAGE7], and “Physical- No added weight on the head, the buttons were easier to press and more responsive” [PAGE7]. The paper supports the usability side but not the counter-trap side: v2 improved “ease of play, button control and extent of focus” [PAGE8], but v2 was tested only on the “accumulator game” [PAGE7], not the sequence mode.

The clock layer is the timing regime. Accumulator play uses 30/60 s limits [PAGE3]; reaction flashes occur at random 5–15 s intervals [PAGE4]; sequence complexity increases until error [PAGE3]. Time pressure can motivate or distort: one user wanted “more time in playing the game” [PAGE8], while another found the buttons too low and had to “bend down” [PAGE8]. A recursive design should therefore separate speed, accuracy, and memory load; otherwise the user’s strategy changes from “remember and repeat” to “guess before the clock,” and the clinician’s inference from score to acuity is invalid.

If it shows improvement only in game score but not in transfer tasks, the construct claim is vetoed.

2. Feature-attribution veto. Claim under test: v2’s usability gain is caused by tooltips, radial displays, 3D audio, and haptics [PAGE7]. Verifiable test: a within-subject ablation with full v2, v2 minus tooltips, v2 minus haptics, v2 minus audio, and v1, randomized and counterbalanced in one lab; outcomes are the same Likert attributes and logged button-control errors. Prediction: the source supports only a bundled v1-versus-v2 difference, with “significantly higher scores for VHB (v2) ... for the ease of play, button control and extent of focus” [PAGE8] and no significant difference for “engagement and ease of light tracking” [PAGE8]. If removing a single feature does not reduce the corresponding outcome, the causal attribution to that feature is vetoed; the honest conclusion is “bundled redesign helped,” not “haptics caused the gain.”

3. Ecological-validity veto. Claim under test: VHB is ecologically valid because BATAK and VHB scores correlate. Verifiable test: preregistered replication with a larger, diverse sample; same 12-target accumulator task [PAGE6]; report \(r\), confidence interval, and covariates for prior VR experience, handedness, height, and controller skill. Prediction: the source’s “Pearson r= 0.45” [PAGE5] at \(n=20\), with 11 frequent video/VR players [PAGE6], will replicate only weakly and with a wide interval. If the interval includes zero, or if controlling for controller skill eliminates the correlation, the ecological-validity claim is vetoed. A pass would require a stable positive correlation across gamers and non-gamers and across physical and virtual orderings, not just a single moderate coefficient.
