The source’s derivation is a conditional chain from broadcast pixels to metric field coordinates, and each hop creates a gray zone that must be audited separately. First, shot filtering is not cosmetic: the paper computes homography change \(L_H(H_t,H_{t+1})=\|H_t-H_{t+1}\|_2\) after per-shot min-max normalization and keeps main-camera shots only when \(L_H\le\tau\), with \(\tau=0.35\) determined experimentally [PAGE 3, PAGE 9]. A close-up or replay that passes this filter produces a homography estimated from a small or absent field region, and every downstream meter inherits that error. Second, sports-field registration returns a \(3\times3\) homography with eight degrees of freedom; the paper explicitly states, “Formally, the matrix H deﬁnes a two-dimensional projective transformation and is deﬁned by a 3 × 3 matrix with eight degrees of freedom” [PAGE 3]. The camera calibration is a nearest-neighbor retrieval in a synthetic edge-map dataset, followed by Lucas–Kanade refinement [PAGE 3]. This means the method’s physical validity is conditional on the broadcast camera’s location, focal length, pan, tilt, and visible field markings resembling the synthetic prior. The paper’s own Table 2 shows the cost of violating that prior: on WC14 the reproduced method reaches mean IoU 93.6 and median 96.5, but on TV12 the mean is 66.5 with standard deviation 37.4, while TV14, TC14, and TV14-S are 85.2, 88.7, and 90.2 respectively [PAGE 6]. TV12 is therefore a breakdown regime, not merely a harder dataset. Third, player detection supplies a bounding box; the image position is the bottom-center point, and the field position is \(\hat p=H^{-1}\tilde p\) [PAGE 4]. The bottom-center assumption is biomechanically a foot-on-plane assumption. Occlusion, camera motion blur, small player scale, and false positives from referees, goalkeepers, substitutes, or staff violate that assumption. Fourth, self-verification discards a frame if any projected player position lies outside the field dimensions plus tolerance \(\rho\) in meters; the paper states, “If one of the projected player positions is far outside the dimensions of the ﬁeld including a tolerance distance ρ in meter, then normally there is an error in the homography estimation” [PAGE 4]. But this is only a gross-error detector. The paper admits that in Figure 2 I,J “the output is obviously erroneous, but not discarded in the sv process” [PAGE 8]. Fifth, player mismatch compares detected and expected player counts by \(\alpha_t:=1-\zeta< |D_t^{\mathrm{real}}|/|D_t^{\mathrm{gt}}| <1+\zeta\) with \(\zeta=0.3\) [PAGE 7]. This is a count-consistency veto, not a proof that the homography is correct: a wrong homography can still project the right number of players into plausible field positions.

The gray-zone audit is where the paper is most honest and most vulnerable. The final metric is a distance in meters, but the paper warns that even marginal homography errors—about 95% IoU—produce roughly 1 m absolute mean error when back-projecting known keypoints [PAGE 8]. Therefore detector AP is not a monotone proxy for positional accuracy. CenterTrack’s average precision is 90.1 on ISSIA-CNR and 90.2 on Soccer Player, but that does not imply sub-meter positions [PAGE 5]. The evaluation also has to solve a linear assignment problem with the Hungarian method, and the per-frame distance set \(D_t=\{d_1,\dots,d_n\}\) can contain outliers from false-positive detections [PAGE 7]. The authors therefore report the best 80% of position estimates rather than the raw mean, because “an outlier, e.g., a false-positive player detection (like a substitute player or goalkeeper) can be matched to a ground truth position with high distance” [PAGE 7]. That choice is defensible, but it creates a reporting gray zone: the metric improves partly by excluding difficult matches, not only by improving the estimator. The same is true of self-verification. Table 5 shows that on TV12, \(\rho=1.0\) m keeps ratio 0.81 and gives \(d_{\mathrm{mean}}=2.12\) m, while \(\rho=3.0\) m keeps ratio 0.90 and gives 2.15 m; the stricter filter discards about nine additional percentage points of frames for essentially no gain [PAGE 10]. The paper itself concludes, “The stricter (smaller) the value is chosen, the more frames are discarded without much improvement of the results” [PAGE 10]. So any claim of accuracy must be paired with coverage ratio.

Team assignment adds another layer. DBScan clusters HSV features from the upper half of each bounding box, scaled to \(20\times20\) and center-cropped to \(16\times16\), with \(\epsilon\) chosen by a cost function \(c(\epsilon)=|X_{\mathrm{Other}}|+||X_A|-|X_B||\) and \(n_{\mathrm{cls}}=0.2\) [PAGE 4, PAGE 5]. The reported macro accuracy is 0.91 and micro accuracy 0.93 [PAGE 6]. Finally, temporal consistency is absent: the paper says player tracking is not covered, stable predictions across frames are not guaranteed, and temporal consistency of registration is not evaluated quantitatively [PAGE 8]. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source supplies a formal operator chain with explicit units and failure regimes. Assumptions: the field is a planar metric surface; \(H\) maps image homogeneous coordinates to field coordinates; the bottom-center of a player bounding box approximates the foot point; video and positional data are synchronized; two dominant jersey-color clusters represent the field teams.

1. Shot-type gate. The homography-change operator is
\[
L_H(H_t,H_{t+1})=\|H_t-H_{t+1}\|_2,
\]
where each entry of \(H\) is min-max normalized per shot; a shot is main-camera if \(L_H\le\tau\), with \(\tau=0.35\) [PAGE 3, PAGE 9]. Units: \(H\) entries and \(L_H\) are dimensionless after normalization; \(\tau\) is dimensionless. The source says, “We calculate the average LH of the homography changes for each shot” and “we classify each shot as the main camera shot if the condition LH ≤τ is fulﬁlled” [PAGE 3]. Breakdown regime: close-ups, replays, or cuts that still yield low \(L_H\) pass the gate and corrupt registration; conversely, legitimate main-camera motion can exceed \(\tau\). The appendix reports TransNetV2 F1 \(=0.89\) with two-frame tolerance and shot-type F1 \(=0.88\), precision \(0.90\), recall \(0.87\) [PAGE 9], so the gate is good but not infallible.

2. Homography and inverse projection. The projective transform is
\[
\tilde{x}_f \sim H\tilde{x}_i,\qquad H\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3},\qquad \mathrm{DoF}(H)=8,
\]
and the player position is
\[
\hat p=H^{-1}\tilde p,\qquad \tilde p\in\mathbb{R}^2,\quad \hat p\in\mathbb{R}^2.
\]
The source states, “The predicted position ˆp ∈R2 of the player on the ﬁeld is then calculated with the inverse homography matrix and the detected image positions of the players: ˆp = H−1 ˜p” [PAGE 4]. Units: \(\tilde p\) is in pixels (or homogeneous image coordinates); \(\hat p\) is in meters if the field coordinate system is metric. Breakdown regimes: \(\det(H)\approx0\) or a large condition number \(\kappa(H)\) makes \(H^{-1}\) numerically unstable; extrapolation outside the visible pitch amplifies registration error; camera parameters outside the synthetic training distribution cause systematic bias. The paper’s synthetic retrieval is described as “the nearest neighbor search in a synthetic dataset of edge map camera pairs” [PAGE 3]. The camera prior is not universal: on TV12 the reproduced method has mean IoU \(66.5\) with standard deviation \(37.4\), indicating a heavy-tailed failure regime [PAGE 6].

3. Self-verification veto. The frame-level validity indicator can be written as
\[
V_t^{\mathrm{sv}}=\mathbf{1}\{\forall k:\hat p_{t,k}\in P\oplus B_\rho(0)\},\qquad \rho=3\,\mathrm{m},
\]
where \(P\) is the field polygon and \(B_\rho(0)\) is a metric tolerance ball. The source states, “If one of the projected player positions is far outside the dimensions of the ﬁeld including a tolerance distance ρ in meter, then normally there is an error in the homography estimation” [PAGE 4]. Units: \(\rho\) in meters; \(V_t^{\mathrm{sv}}\) is a dimensionless indicator. Breakdown regime: \(\rho\) too small discards valid frames; \(\rho\) too large admits bad homographies. Table 5 quantifies this: on TV12, \(\rho=1.0\) keeps ratio \(0.81\) with \(d_{\mathrm{mean}}=2.12\) m, \(\rho=3.0\) keeps \(0.90\) with \(2.15\) m, and no self-verification keeps \(1.00\) with \(3.69\) m [PAGE 10]. The source concludes, “The stricter (smaller) the value is chosen, the more frames are discarded without much improvement of the results” [PAGE 10].

4. Player-mismatch veto. The count-consistency condition is
\[
\alpha_t:=\mathbf{1}\left\{(1-\zeta)\le \frac{|D_t^{\mathrm{real}}|}{|D_t^{\mathrm{gt}}|}\le(1+\zeta)\right\},\qquad \zeta=0.3,
\]
where the source uses strict inequalities in the numerator/denominator ratio and \(\zeta=0.3\) in the example [PAGE 7]. Units: \(|D_t^{\mathrm{real}}|\), \(|D_t^{\mathrm{gt}}|\), and their ratio are dimensionless counts. Breakdown regime: occlusion, false positives, boundary truncation, and referee/goalkeeper inclusion change the count without changing the homography correctness. The paper’s own wording is “player mismatch (pm)” and it uses this as a diagnostic for “the high impact of the sports ﬁeld registration” [PAGE 8].

5. Position error and aggregate metrics. The per-player distance is
\[
d_i=\|\hat p_i-p_i\|_2,\qquad D_t=\{d_1,\dots,d_n\},
\]
with meters as the unit; the source says, “We measure the distance (in meters) between the estimated positions and the actual positions” [PAGE 7]. The reported fractions are
\[
a_l=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{\bar d_t\le l\},\qquad l\in\{2.0,3.0\}\,\mathrm{m},
\]
where \(a_l\) is a dimensionless fraction in \([0,1]\). The paper’s outlier-resistant aggregation is described as “we propose to report the average distances of the best 80-percent position estimates” [PAGE 7]; an interpretive formalization is
\[
\bar d_t^{(q)}=\frac{1}{\lceil qn_t\rceil}\sum_{i=1}^{\lceil qn_t\rceil}d_{(i),t},\qquad q=0.8,
\]
with \(d_{(i),t}\) the ordered distances. Units remain meters; \(q\) is dimensionless. Breakdown regime: a mean without quantile filtering is dominated by false positives; a quantile that is too strict hides real errors. Table 6 shows the effect on TV12: raw mean gives \(d_{\mathrm{mean}}=8.90\), median gives \(3.70\), and the proposed aggregation gives \(3.69\) without filtering [PAGE 10]. Ground-truth noise is also nonzero: the paper assumes “An error of one meter is to be assumed in the data provided to us” [PAGE 5], and a domain expert treats \(\le2\) m as correct and below 3 m as still meaningful [PAGE 7].

6. Calibration metric. The field-registration score is
\[
\mathrm{IoU}_{\mathrm{part}}=\frac{|E(\hat H)\cap E(H^\star)|_{\mathrm{visible}}}{|E(\hat H)\cup E(H^\star)|_{\mathrm{visible}}},
\]
where \(E(\cdot)\) denotes edge images and the subscript restricts to the visible pitch; it is dimensionless. The paper says it is “computed between the two edge images using the predicted homography and the ground-truth homography on the visible part of the image” [PAGE 6]. Breakdown regime: high IoU on WC14 does not transfer automatically; the reproduced method scores \(93.6\) mean / \(96.5\) median on WC14 but \(66.5\) mean on TV12 [PAGE 6]. Even \(\approx95\%\) IoU can correspond to about 1 m mean error after back-projection [PAGE 8].

7. Team assignment. DBScan uses an \(\epsilon\) threshold and \(n_{\mathrm{cls}}\in[0,0.5]\), with
\[
c(\epsilon)=|X_{\mathrm{Other}}|+\big||X_A|-|X_B|\big|,
\]
and the source says “The value for ϵ is selected, for which the cost function \(c(\epsilon)=|X(O)ther|+||XA|−|XB||\) is minimal and restricted to form exactly two clusters” [PAGE 4]. The empirical value is \(n_{\mathrm{cls}}=0.2\) [PAGE 5]. Units: \(\epsilon\) is a normalized color L2 distance; \(n_{\mathrm{cls}}\), cluster sizes, and \(c(\epsilon)\) are dimensionless. Breakdown regime: similar jersey colors, artificial vs daylight illumination, occlusion, and referee/goalkeeper presence merge or split clusters. The reported macro accuracy is \(0.91\) and micro accuracy \(0.93\) [PAGE 6], but applying the team constraint worsens final position metrics: on TV12 under sv+pm, \(d_{\mathrm{mean}}\) rises from \(2.10\) m to \(3.39\) m [PAGE 7].

8. Runtime and information units. The paper reports that “All components are real-time capable (> 25 fps) on common hardware ... except for the ﬁeld registration” [PAGE 9]. Thus the processing frequency is \(f_{\mathrm{proc}}\ge 25\,\mathrm{Hz}\) for the real-time modules, with field registration as the bottleneck. No information-theoretic quantity in bits or nats is reported; all probability-like quantities are dimensionless fractions in \([0,1]\), and all accuracies are dimensionless fractions or percentages. Singularity/breakdown regimes are therefore: non-invertible or ill-conditioned \(H\); camera pose outside the synthetic prior; \(\rho\) or \(\zeta\) mis-set; color clusters that are not two dominant clusters; unsynchronized video; and ground-truth sensor noise on the order of 1 m.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage runs from projective geometry and camera calibration through synthetic-data retrieval, Siamese feature matching, tracking-by-detection, density clustering, and Hungarian assignment. The field-registration branch descends from Chen and Little’s synthetic edge-map calibration: a pix2pix field segmentation, a Siamese CNN that compares generated edge images to synthetic edge images with known camera parameters, nearest-neighbor retrieval by L2 feature distance, and Lucas–Kanade refinement [PAGE 3]. That genealogy explains both its strength and its trap. Strength: no manual annotation is needed to train the calibration model, and hyper-parameters can be adapted by changing a few values [PAGE 3]. Trap: the synthetic camera prior is derived from WC14, the only public soccer benchmark, and the paper explicitly notes that existing approaches are evaluated on “a single small-scale dataset” and that generalization was not investigated [PAGE 1, PAGE 6]. The multi-week trap is to tune on WC14, observe high mean IoU, and then assume the same camera geometry holds in another league. Table 2 refutes that: TV12 collapses to mean IoU \(66.5\) with standard deviation \(37.4\), while TV14 is \(85.2\) and TC14 is \(88.7\) [PAGE 6]. A second lineage is tracking-by-detection: CenterTrack is chosen because it performs well on both ISSIA-CNR and Soccer Player, with AP \(90.1\) and \(90.2\), while Faster R-CNN and FootAndBall each fail on one dataset [PAGE 5]. The trap is to confuse detection AP with positional accuracy; the paper itself separates those layers and notes that bounding-box reduction to one point does not substantially affect meter error only because homography error dominates [PAGE 8]. A third lineage is unsupervised team clustering via DBScan, with HSV features and a cost that prefers two similarly sized clusters and few “other” detections [PAGE 4]. The multi-week trap is to treat team labels as ground truth because micro accuracy is \(0.93\); the final evaluation shows team assignment can degrade positions because false team labels break the assignment before the Hungarian matching [PAGE 7]. A fourth trap is temporal. The datasets are at 25 Hz and TV14-S had to be synchronized using the visible game clock because of temporal inconsistencies in the raw video [PAGE 4, PAGE 5]. If synchronization is off by even a fraction of a second, meter errors are inflated by player motion, not by calibration. A fifth trap is metric artifact: the raw mean is inflated by false-positive detections, so the authors use the best 80% of estimates [PAGE 7]. Over multiple weeks, an evaluator can “improve” the score by tightening filters and discarding hard frames; Table 5 shows exactly that pattern, with \(\rho=1.0\) keeping 0.81 of frames at 2.12 m versus \(\rho=3.0\) keeping 0.90 at 2.15 m [PAGE 10]. The retrocausal lesson is that final meter error must be traced backward through the filter ratio, the team constraint, the camera prior, the detector, and the synchronization step; otherwise the genealogy of the number is lost.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive agent model here has three modes. Crisis habit regression is what an analyst does when the aggregate error looks high: fall back to familiar proxies—detector AP, mean distance, WC14 IoU—and stop asking which layer failed. The paper gives direct evidence against that habit: CenterTrack AP is high on both test sets, yet final TV12 sv-only \(d_{\mathrm{mean}}\) is \(4.15\) m without team assignment, and even after sv+pm it is \(2.10\) m [PAGE 5, PAGE 7]. The habit also treats the ground truth as exact, although the paper assumes about 1 m error in the positional data [PAGE 5]. A second habit is to read Table 2 as a universal ranking; but TV12’s mean \(66.5\) with standard deviation \(37.4\) is a warning that the camera-parameter distribution, not the architecture alone, controls the outcome [PAGE 6]. Counter-trap behavior is the opposite failure: over-tighten the vetoes to make the reported error look better. If \(\rho\) is reduced from 3 m to 1 m, TV12 keeps 0.81 instead of 0.90 of frames and changes \(d_{\mathrm{mean}}\) from 2.15 to 2.12 m—a negligible gain for a large coverage loss [PAGE 10]. If team assignment is imposed as a constraint, TV12 sv+pm \(d_{\mathrm{mean}}\) rises from 2.10 to 3.39 m and \(a_{\le2}\) falls from 0.64 to 0.34 [PAGE 7]. The counter-trap is to interpret “fewer frames” as “cleaner data” and to interpret “team-constrained” as “more realistic” when the constraint actually injects clustering error. The clock mode is temporal: the system operates on 25 Hz video, but the paper does not include a tracking module, does not quantitatively evaluate temporal consistency of registration, and lists tracking plus player re-identification as future work [PAGE 4, PAGE 8]. The only clock-aware repair mentioned is synchronization of TV14-S using the visible game clock [PAGE 5]. Recursively, the evaluator must model the analyst modeling the system: if the reward is low \(d_{\mathrm{mean}}\), the analyst can drop hard frames; if the reward is low \(d_{\mathrm{mean}}\) at fixed coverage, the analyst must improve the homography, detector grounding, or matching. The paper’s own design prefers reporting coverage ratio alongside error, which is the correct counter to both habit regression and counter-trap behavior. The remaining clock gap is that a per-frame pipeline can look stable while the match-level trajectory is not; without tracking, the system cannot distinguish a true player movement from a frame-to-frame registration oscillation.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A valid positional claim must pass physical, biomechanical, and environmental vetoes before it can be admitted as evidence. Physical veto: the homography must be invertible and numerically conditioned. If \(\det(H)=0\) or \(\kappa(H)\) is large, \(H^{-1}\) does not define a stable meter coordinate; the frame is vetoed regardless of detector AP. The paper’s registration is a projective \(3\times3\) map with eight degrees of freedom [PAGE 3], and its synthetic retrieval is only as good as the camera prior. The TV12 result—mean IoU \(66.5\), standard deviation \(37.4\)—is prima facie evidence that the physical camera prior is violated for a substantial subset [PAGE 6]. Physical veto also applies to projected players outside \(P\oplus B_\rho(0)\): the source says such a position “normally” indicates homography error [PAGE 4]. Biomechanical veto: the bottom-center bounding-box point is an approximation of the foot contact point. If the player is occluded, blurred by camera motion, too small in scale, or not actually on the field plane, the foot-point assumption fails. The paper acknowledges small players, scale variation, occlusion, and motion blur as soccer-specific detection challenges [PAGE 3]. A detected referee, goalkeeper, substitute, or staff member also violates the field-player assumption; the paper excludes goalkeepers from team assignment and discards unassigned detections [PAGE 4]. Environmental/weather veto: field segmentation and HSV team clustering depend on illumination and color contrast. The shot-boundary dataset explicitly includes “light conditions (artiﬁcial vs. daylight)” [PAGE 9], which is a proxy for the environmental layer that can change edge maps and jersey colors. If the two-cluster cost \(c(\epsilon)=|X_{\mathrm{Other}}|+||X_A|-|X_B||\) cannot be minimized into exactly two dominant clusters, the team label is vetoed [PAGE 4]. Temporal/synchronization veto: TV14-S required synchronization using the visible game clock because the raw video had temporal inconsistencies [PAGE 5]. If the video clock and positional clock are offset, any meter error is contaminated by player displacement; the frame or match segment is not admissible for metric evaluation. Adjudication rule: a frame may be used for unlabeled position evidence only if the shot is main-camera, \(H\) is stable and invertible, all projected players lie in the field-plus-\(\rho\) region, and the player-count ratio is within \(1\pm\zeta\). A team-labeled position requires, in addition, exactly two dominant clusters and stable color separation. The source’s own numbers justify this strictness: self-verification does not catch all obvious errors (Figure 2 I,J), and the pm criterion “demonstrates the high impact of the sports ﬁeld registration” [PAGE 8]. Cross-layer proof sketch: if any of these vetoes fails, the measured distance \(d_i=\|\hat p_i-p_i\|_2\) no longer isolates estimator quality; it becomes a mixture of geometry error, detection error, label error, and clock error. Therefore the only defensible claim is a conditional one: under a passed veto set, the reported meter error is attributable to the remaining layers. Under a failed veto set, the number is not evidence about the pipeline’s positional accuracy.

Each scenario is a reproducible counterfactual check: if the predicted table-level change is not recovered, the claimed causal explanation is vetoed.

Scenario 1: Disable self-verification on TV12, i.e. set \(\rho=\infty\). Prediction: coverage ratio returns to \(1.00\), \(d_{\mathrm{mean}}\) rises to \(3.69\) m, \(a_{\le2}\) falls to \(0.64\), and \(a_{\le3}\) falls to \(0.78\). With \(\rho=3.0\) m, the paper reports ratio \(0.90\), \(d_{\mathrm{mean}}=2.15\) m, \(a_{\le2}=0.69\), \(a_{\le3}=0.84\) [PAGE 10]. Veto condition: if disabling sv does not materially worsen the meter error and coverage, then self-verification is not functioning as a gross-homography-error filter on this dataset.

Scenario 2: Remove the player-mismatch filter at fixed self-verification on TV12 without team assignment. Prediction: coverage rises from \(0.79\) to \(0.90\), \(d_{\mathrm{mean}}\) rises from \(2.10\) m to \(2.52\) m, median rises from \(1.55\) m to \(1.66\) m, \(a_{\le2}\) falls from \(0.64\) to \(0.61\), and \(a_{\le3}\) falls from \(0.80\) to \(0.77\) [PAGE 7]. Veto condition: if removing pm does not increase error or coverage, then the count-consistency condition is not selecting a meaningfully different frame population. The expected mechanism is that pm removes frames where detection count and expected count disagree, which disproportionately includes bad homographies and false-positive-heavy frames.

Scenario 3: Apply the team-assignment constraint under sv+pm on TV12. Prediction: coverage stays at \(0.79\), but \(d_{\mathrm{mean}}\) worsens from \(2.10\) m to \(3.39\) m, median worsens from \(1.55\) m to \(2.99\) m, \(a_{\le2}\) falls from \(0.64\) to \(0.34\), and \(a_{\le3}\) falls from \(0.80\) to \(0.51\) [PAGE 7]. Veto condition: if team-constrained matching does not degrade the metric, then the team-assignment module is not injecting label error into the matching step. The expected mechanism is that the Hungarian matching is split by team before distance assignment, so a wrong team label can force a correct position to be matched to the wrong ground-truth player, yielding a large \(d_i\) even when the unlabeled position was acceptable.

A fourth optional check is camera-prior sensitivity: on TV14, extending focal length and location sampling changes IoU from \(85.2\) to \(87.4\) for the \(U_{\mathrm{focal\ length}}+U_{xyz}\) variant, but on TV12 it drops from \(66.5\) to \(61.1\) [PAGE 6]. Prediction: the extended prior is not uniformly better; it is a distribution shift, not a monotone improvement. Veto condition: if a single extended prior improves every dataset, then the paper’s TV12 failure is not a camera-prior generalization failure. Together these counterfactuals make the paper’s causal story testable: self-verification trades coverage for gross-error rejection; player mismatch trades coverage for count consistency; team assignment can preserve coverage while degrading label-consistent matching; and camera-prior changes are dataset-dependent.
