<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s reasoning chain is a five-layer inference stack: raw tracking/event data are converted into Pitch Control Maps, those maps are temporally labeled by a CNN-LSTM classifier, the labeled sequence is modeled by a Conditional Variational Recurrent Neural Network, the generative model is used as a counterfactual benchmark, and the benchmark is scored through Expected Possession Value. The key derivation is therefore not a single equation but a pipeline of probabilistic state abstractions. First, the authors state that “every pass is viewed as a Bernoulli trial with k representing the outcome of the pass (success: 1, failure: 0)” [PAGE 2 of 8]. That converts a pass outcome into a binary random variable with probability p. The pass model is trained by likelihood maximization, but the paper’s operative object is not the pass label alone; it is the “real valued scalar field for generating a Pitch Control Map” [PAGE 2 of 8]. This is the critical abstraction: the model does not benchmark raw player coordinates directly; it benchmarks a field of controlled space. Thus the unit of analysis is spatial probability mass over pitch coordinates, with time advancing at discrete intervals: “The time interval between each two adjacent Pitch Control Maps is 1 second” [PAGE 3 of 8].

The second layer is conditioning. The authors observe that Vanilla VRNN has weak dependence on context: “when given only the beginning game state, Vanilla can hardly make stable long-term predictions due to the lack of conditioning on the contextual factors such as the changing pattern within each two adjacent frames” [PAGE 4 of 8]. Their solution is to create a categorical control variable a_t from frame-to-frame labels: pushing, backing, or staying. The classifier is not merely an auxiliary model; it supplies the semantic derivative of the Pitch Control Map sequence. In information terms, a_t compresses the observed transition into a low-cardinality symbol. The generative model then learns p(x_t | x_past, a_t, latent state) rather than p(x_t | x_past) alone. This is why the architecture is “conditional”: the prior, posterior, and recurrence are all conditioned on labels. The paper says explicitly, “We condition the prior, posterior and LSTM parameters on the conditional variable when training the model” [PAGE 4 of 8].

The third layer is the objective. The training loss combines a KL divergence term and a structural reconstruction term. The authors state, “The loss function for training our model contains the KL divergence and reconstruction loss” [PAGE 5 of 8]. Formally, the KL term is measured in information units, usually nats if natural logarithms are used or bits if base-two logarithms are used, while SSIM is dimensionless. The reconstruction term is not pixel-wise mean squared error but structural similarity, because “the structural difference rather than each two pixel’s difference between two images is the key factor” [PAGE 5 of 8]. This matters for gray-zone auditing: a model can have high SSIM while still failing tactically if the structural red/blue regions look similar but encode a different spatial-control interpretation.

The fourth layer is benchmark evaluation. Once the model predicts a plausible sequence from the first frame, the paper converts each Pitch Control Map into EPV: “we quantify the value of Pitch Control Maps by utilizing EPV” [PAGE 6 of 8]. The benchmark is then the counterfactual EPV path that a team “should” have produced in that context, while the real sequence is the observed EPV path. The evaluation residual is therefore ΔEPV_t = EPV_real(t) − EPV_benchmark(t). A positive residual means the team underperformed the context-adjusted expectation; a negative residual means the team overperformed it. The paper’s qualitative example is a counterattack: the benchmark expects the attacking team to exploit left and right wings and push quickly, but the real team fails because “the defensive side makes a quick pressure after losing control of the ball and moves back as a whole team instantly” and because “the attacking team wingers haven’t reacted quickly enough” [PAGE 7 of 8].

The main gray-zone audit is that the benchmark is only as valid as its three intermediate assumptions. First, the Pitch Control Map must be a faithful sufficient statistic for space control. The paper inherits this from Spearman et al. and treats the map as encoding both geometry and interaction: “These Pitch Control Maps include not only geometric contextual information of the game but also the information about interactions between two opposite teams and cooperation among players within the same team” [PAGE 2 of 8]. Second, the classifier labels must be reliable. The paper sets a confidence threshold: “sequences with confidence less than 0.95 are believed to be those showing no detectable patterns of pushing or backing” [PAGE 4 of 8]. Third, the generative model must remain stable over long horizons. The reported SSIM falls from roughly 0.954 in reconstruction to 0.850 in prediction from only the first frame for their model [PAGE 6 of 8], showing that temporal compounding remains a real weakness. The deepest audit issue is that SSIM above 0.95 is treated as human-indistinguishable, since “when SSIM is over 0.95, a human cannot detect the difference between two images” [PAGE 5 of 8], but perceptual indistinguishability is not the same as tactical correctness. A generated Pitch Control Map can look structurally similar yet imply a different counterpress or compactness regime, and EPV may then mis-score the possession.

The source uses probabilities, sequence models, and structural image similarity. All probabilistic quantities are dimensionless unless explicitly interpreted as expected counts. The temporal sampling interval is discrete and fixed: “The time interval between each two adjacent Pitch Control Maps is 1 second” [PAGE 3 of 8]. Let Δt = 1 s.

1. Pass success as Bernoulli operator  
The paper states that “every pass is viewed as a Bernoulli trial with k representing the outcome of the pass (success: 1, failure: 0)” [PAGE 2 of 8]. The Bernoulli law is:

\[
p(k=1\mid \sigma,\lambda,x)=p, \qquad p(k=0\mid \sigma,\lambda,x)=1-p,
\]

with p ∈ [0,1]. Units: probability, dimensionless.  
The training likelihood is given as:

\[
L(\sigma,\lambda\mid k,x)=P(k\mid \sigma,\lambda,x).
\]

If logarithms are used, the log-likelihood has information units of nats when using natural logarithms or bits when using base-two logarithms.  
Assumptions: pass success depends only on the input features x and parameters σ, λ.  
Breakdown regime: if x omits relevant physical context, such as pressure, fatigue, surface friction, or defensive reaction, the Bernoulli model is misspecified.

2. Pitch Control Map as a scalar probability field  
The trained pass model is used “to compute the stationary pass probability for an imagined ball location and convert the whole pitch into a real valued scalar field for generating a Pitch Control Map” [PAGE 2 of 8]. A formal version is:

\[
C_t(u)=P_{\text{pass}}(k=1\mid x_t,u),
\]

where u ∈ ℝ² denotes pitch coordinates with spatial units in meters, and C_t(u) ∈ [0,1] is dimensionless controlled-space probability mass. The controlled area for team j can be written as:

\[
A_{j,t}=\int_{\Omega} \mathbf{1}\{C_{j,t}(u) > C_{\text{opp},t}(u)\}\,du,
\]

with units m² if Ω is a physical pitch region.  
Assumptions: space control can be reduced to a stationary pass-success field; interaction effects are encoded implicitly in the field.  
Breakdown regime: if the imagined-ball assumption violates actual ball dynamics, or if the pitch region includes unobserved states such as aerial duels or set-piece constraints, the map becomes a partial abstraction.

3. Expected Possession Value as Markov scoring operator  
The paper defines EPV through possession-to-goal probability:

\[
P_{\text{poss}}(G\mid \text{situation})=P_{\text{poss}}(G\mid \text{ball},\text{match state}).
\]

This equation appears directly in the source as Eq. (3) [PAGE 2 of 8]. The authors justify it with a Markov assumption: “Each possession within the football game is defined as a sequence of game states with the possible next state only depending on the current state, following the theorem of Markov Process” [PAGE 2 of 8].  
Units: probability of scoring, dimensionless; if aggregated over many possessions it can be interpreted as expected goals per possession.  
Assumptions: current state is a sufficient statistic for future scoring probability.  
Breakdown regime: long-range dependence, fatigue, tactical substitutions, or game-state pressure can invalidate the Markov abstraction. The authors also note that the original EPV model is used because the benchmark is image-based and lacks enough context for the advanced EPV model: “we use the original version of the EPV model [Rudd, 2011]” [PAGE 3 of 8].

4. Variational lower bound  
The VAE objective is written as:

\[
\log p(x) \ge -\mathrm{KL}(q(z\mid x)\Vert p(z)) + \mathbb{E}_{q(z\mid x)}[\log p(x\mid z)].
\]

This is Eq. (4) in the source [PAGE 3 of 8]. The KL term has information units, usually nats or bits. The reconstruction term is a log-likelihood and is dimensionless per sample if x is a normalized image representation.  
Assumptions: the approximate posterior q(z|x) is tractable, usually Gaussian, and the prior p(z) is chosen analytically.  
Breakdown regime: posterior collapse, latent underutilization, or overly strong KL regularization can prevent the latent variable from carrying tactical information.

5. Conditional prior and posterior  
The source gives the conditional prior as:

\[
\psi_p^t=[\mu_{0,t},\sigma_{0,t}]=\psi_\tau(\psi_\tau(h_{t-1}),a_t),
\]

which is Eq. (5) [PAGE 5 of 8]. Here h_{t-1} is the LSTM hidden state at t−1 and a_t is the conditional label variable.  
The approximate posterior is written as:

\[
\psi_q^t=[\mu_{z,t},\sigma_{z,t}]
=\psi_\tau(\psi_\tau(\psi_\tau(\psi_{\text{enc}}(x_t)),a_t),\psi_\tau(h_{t-1})),
\]

which is Eq. (6) [PAGE 5 of 8].  
Units: μ and σ are latent-space parameters; σ must be positive; latent variables are dimensionless embeddings.  
Assumptions: the label a_t is informative about the transition from frame t−1 to frame t.  
Breakdown regime: label noise or classifier overconfidence causes the model to condition on incorrect push/back/stay symbols.

6. Conditional sequence loss  
The loss is given as:

\[
L=-\mathrm{KL}(q(z_t\mid x_{\le t},z_{\text{past}})\Vert p(z_t\mid x_{\text{past}},z_{\text{past}}))+L_{\text{ssim}}.
\]

This is Eq. (7) in the source [PAGE 5 of 8]. The first term is information-theoretic, measured in nats or bits. The second term is dimensionless because SSIM is a normalized structural similarity score.  
Assumptions: each timestep contributes equally through averaging, since “the mean of losses across all timesteps is used as the loss of input sequence” [PAGE 5 of 8].  
Breakdown regime: error accumulation over time, especially when only the first frame is given and future frames must be generated recursively.

7. SSIM structural operator  
The paper writes:

\[
\mathrm{SSIM}(x,y)=[l(x,y)]^\alpha [c(x,y)]^\beta [s(x,y)]^\gamma,
\]

which is Eq. (8) [PAGE 5 of 8]. α, β, γ are positive weights. SSIM is dimensionless and typically lies in [0,1] for normalized images.  
The perceptual threshold is stated as: “when SSIM is over 0.95, a human cannot detect the difference between two images” [PAGE 5 of 8].  
Breakdown regime: SSIM can be high while tactical meaning is wrong, because SSIM measures structural appearance, not strategic sufficiency.

8. Benchmark residual operator  
The benchmark sequence is generated from the first frame and conditional labels:

\[
B_{1:T}=G_\phi(x_1,a_{2:T}).
\]

The real sequence is R_{1:T}. EPV is applied to both:

\[
E_t^{\text{real}}=\mathrm{EPV}(R_t), \qquad E_t^{\text{bench}}=\mathrm{EPV}(B_t).
\]

The performance residual is:

\[
\Delta E_t = E_t^{\text{real}} - E_t^{\text{bench}}.
\]

This follows from the paper’s procedure: “we quantify the value of Pitch Control Maps by utilizing EPV” and compare “the real sequence against this benchmark using EPV” [PAGE 6 of 8].  
Units: EPV is probability of scoring from the possession, dimensionless; ΔEPV is a dimensionless probability difference.  
Assumptions: EPV is valid for generated PCMs and the conditional labels are available or reliably inferable.  
Breakdown regime: if future labels are not observable in real time, the benchmark becomes an offline counterfactual rather than a live evaluation tool.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage runs from physics-based pass probability models to Markov possession value, then to deep generative sequence modeling. The authors explicitly build on Pitch Control Maps from Spearman et al., saying that a Pitch Control Map “computes the area controlled by each team within a given game state of the football game” [PAGE 2 of 8]. This lineage treats space control as a measurable tactical state rather than as a narrative judgment. The earlier pass-probability model is genealogically important because it transforms player and ball kinematics into a probability field. The authors describe the inputs as “positions, speed and facing directions of ball and players, and the player ids of passer and receiver” [PAGE 2 of 8]. That means the model inherits a physical-layer dependency: if kinematics are noisy, the derived space-control field is noisy.

The second lineage is EPV. The paper says EPV “quantifies the ball possession value given the ball position and match state” and relies on a Markov process [PAGE 2 of 8]. It also acknowledges that later EPV models added contextual factors, but the authors choose the original EPV because their benchmark is represented by images that “don’t contain enough contextual information for running the advanced version of EPV model” [PAGE 3 of 8]. This is a retro-causal trap: the representational choice, PCM images, limits the scoring model, which in turn limits the benchmark’s validity. The model cannot recover advanced contextual scoring because the state representation has already discarded information.

The third lineage is imitation learning. The authors note that prior work used imitation learning for football performance analysis but that “The State-of-the-art Imitation Learning model in football performance analysis can only set the benchmark for defending when evaluating team performance and it is unable to capture the interactions between two opposite teams” [PAGE 2 of 8]. The current paper tries to overcome this by generative multi-agent modeling of PCM sequences. The key historical shift is from action-level imitation to field-level generative imitation. Instead of predicting player actions one by one, the model predicts the spatial-control consequence of multi-agent interaction.

The fourth lineage is variational sequence modeling. The paper builds on VAE and VRNN. It states that VAE “generates new samples by learning input data’s hidden representation in terms of regularities and patterns” and that VRNN “is an extension of VAE combining VAE by utilizing recurrent neural networks to model sequential data” [PAGE 3 of 8]. The conditional extension is the paper’s novelty: the label sequence a_t conditions prior, posterior, and recurrence. This creates a multi-week experimental trap: if the classifier labels are generated from the same dataset distribution as the VRNN training data, the model may learn dataset-specific transition shortcuts rather than general football tactics.

Several multi-week traps emerge from the genealogy. First, preprocessing can create temporal leakage. The paper uses sliding windows: “the sequences are processed by a sliding window to generate the sequences with same length as 6” [PAGE 3 of 8]. If windows from the same possession appear in both training and test sets, SSIM may overstate generalization. Second, classifier confidence thresholds can create a hidden regime switch. The paper states that low-confidence sequences are “classified as staying” [PAGE 4 of 8]. If many transitions are actually pushing or backing but are labeled staying due to threshold effects, the conditional model learns a biased transition grammar. Third, the SSIM threshold may create a perceptual trap: a model can exceed the human nondetection threshold while still failing in low-frequency tactical contexts, such as counterattacks, because those events are rare and structurally subtle.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The paper implicitly models a recursive belief problem: the benchmark must infer what a team would do if it were playing optimally or typically under the same contextual state. This requires modeling the beliefs of multiple agents. The attacking team believes that open space behind the defense can be exploited. The defensive team believes that rapid compactness can suppress the counterattack. The benchmark model must represent both beliefs simultaneously through the Pitch Control Map. The paper’s qualitative example makes this explicit: the benchmark expects the attacking team to “utilize the open space from left and right wings and quickly push their formation towards the opponent’s half” [PAGE 7 of 8], but the real defense “makes a quick pressure after losing control of the ball and moves back as a whole team instantly” [PAGE 7 of 8].

Crisis habit regression is the tendency of teams under pressure to revert to trained habits. In the source example, the defensive team’s habit is rapid team-wide recovery. The attacking team’s habit failure is delayed winger reaction: “the attacking team wingers haven’t reacted quickly enough in terms of making forward runs when the team wins the ball” [PAGE 7 of 8]. This is a theory-of-mind failure inside the real team, not merely inside the model. The attacking team may believe it has time to progress, but the defensive team’s belief and habit compress the available window.

The counter-trap is to avoid using a static metric that only sees increasing space control. The authors criticize this: “Only using existing metric EPV, it can be inferred that the attacking team is doing good with consistently increasing high EPV values” [PAGE 6 of 8]. But the benchmark reveals that the real defensive response was more efficient than the attacking progression. Thus the recursive theory of mind is: EPV_real must be compared with EPV_counterfactual, not evaluated alone. A team can have high EPV and still underperform if the contextual benchmark expected even higher value.

The clock layer is critical. Because the PCM interval is one second, “The time interval between each two adjacent Pitch Control Maps is 1 second” [PAGE 3 of 8], the model’s temporal resolution is coarse relative to counterattack windows. A counterattack may require decisions faster than one second, especially in transition. If the benchmark predicts at one-second resolution, it may miss the exact moment when the defensive block becomes compact or when the winger should begin the run. Therefore, the recursive belief model must include a clock uncertainty: the shorter the transition window, the less reliable a one-second PCM benchmark becomes. The paper’s own example depends on timesteps 3 and 6 [PAGE 7 of 8], showing that even at coarse temporal resolution the qualitative interpretation can be meaningful, but the exact causal timing remains underdetermined.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is valid when a lower physical layer proves that a generated benchmark is impossible or severely misspecified, even if the upper statistical layer has high SSIM. The paper’s model operates on Pitch Control Maps derived from “positions, speed and facing directions of ball and players” [PAGE 2 of 8]. That gives a physical hook for veto.

Let c_{j,t} be the spatial centroid of team j’s controlled region at time t:

\[
c_{j,t}=\frac{\int_{\Omega} u\, C_{j,t}(u)\,du}{\int_{\Omega} C_{j,t}(u)\,du}.
\]

The units of u are meters, so c_{j,t} has units of meters. If a generated sequence implies team centroid motion faster than a biomechanical maximum v_max, then the sequence should be vetoed:

\[
\frac{\lVert c_{j,t+1}-c_{j,t}\rVert}{\Delta t} > v_{\max}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\text{physical veto}.
\]

Here Δt = 1 s from the paper [PAGE 3 of 8]. The threshold v_max is an external biomechanical constraint, not a ratio invented by the model. If v_max is set conservatively for elite outfield players, a generated centroid shift requiring impossible sprint speed over one second invalidates the benchmark. This veto is especially relevant for counterattack examples where the model predicts rapid wing expansion; if the predicted expansion would require players to teleport or exceed sprint capacity, the benchmark must be rejected or smoothed.

A second physical veto concerns ball motion. The pass probability model uses ball and player kinematics [PAGE 2 of 8]. If the implied ball trajectory in the benchmark requires speed greater than a plausible maximum v_ball,max, then the generated PCM is physically inconsistent. Formally, if d_ball,t is the implied ball displacement over Δt, then:

\[
\frac{d_{\text{ball},t}}{\Delta t} > v_{\text{ball,max}}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\text{ball-dynamics veto}.
\]

This veto is not explicitly provided in the paper, but it follows directly from the physical inputs used to train the pass model. The source does not report ball-speed constraints, so this is an external adjudication rule rather than a paper-specified equation.

Weather and surface conditions form a third cross-layer veto domain. The source does not state that weather, pitch wetness, wind, or surface friction are included as variables. If such exogenous variables materially change pass probability or player acceleration, then the trained PCM operator is conditionally misspecified. A formal veto condition is:

\[
P(y\mid x,w_1)\neq P(y\mid x,w_2)
\quad\text{for materially different weather states }w_1,w_2,
\]

where y is pass success or possession progression and w is an unobserved weather/surface state. In that case, the benchmark’s EPV residual ΔEPV_t may reflect weather-induced shift rather than tactical quality. The paper’s silence on weather means the model cannot be fully vetoed from the source alone, but it also means that weather is a latent confounder. The safest adjudication is: no absolute physical veto from the text, but a conditional veto whenever external match conditions are known to alter passing or movement dynamics.

The final veto proof is perceptual-versus-tactical. SSIM above 0.95 is treated as human-indistinguishable: “when SSIM is over 0.95, a human cannot detect the difference between two images” [PAGE 5 of 8]. However, if the generated PCM has high SSIM but violates physical continuity, rule constraints, or tactical plausibility, SSIM cannot save it. The veto order is therefore: physical feasibility first, then contextual plausibility, then statistical similarity. A benchmark that is physically impossible is invalid regardless of SSIM.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios for using the Conditional VRNN benchmark as an evaluation instrument. They are expressed as checks that can be automated from tracking data, generated PCMs, classifier confidences, and EPV scores.

Veto 1: Physical displacement veto  
Verifier:

\[
V_{\text{phys}}=
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } \max_t \frac{\lVert c_{j,t+1}-c_{j,t}\rVert}{\Delta t} > v_{\max},\\
0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]

Here c_{j,t} is the team-controlled spatial centroid, Δt = 1 s [PAGE 3 of 8], and v_max is a biomechanical maximum speed. This veto is verifiable because the generated sequence can be converted into implied centroid motion, and the resulting speed can be compared with an external speed ceiling.  
Prediction: the fully conditioned model should produce fewer physical vetoes than Vanilla VRNN because conditioning on labels stabilizes transitions. This is consistent with the paper’s finding that Vanilla VRNN performs worse in prediction, especially when “predict the changing patterns from real sequence” is evaluated [PAGE 6 of 8]. However, even the conditioned model may still generate occasional impossible jumps if latent sampling is unstable.

Veto 2: Low-confidence label veto  
The classifier assigns pushing, backing, or staying, and the paper says: “sequences with confidence less than 0.95 are believed to be those showing no detectable patterns of pushing or backing” [PAGE 4 of 8]. A verifiable veto is:

\[
V_{\text{conf}}=
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } \min_{t\ge 2} \max_c P(c\mid x_t,x_{t-1}) < 0.95,\\
0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]

If any conditioning label used by the benchmark falls below the confidence threshold, the benchmark’s tactical interpretation should be downgraded or vetoed.  
Prediction: possessions with low classifier confidence will show less reliable EPV residuals. In such cases, the benchmark may label a sequence as “staying” when the true transition was ambiguous, causing the model to underestimate the value of a rapid push or a defensive recovery. The observable prediction is that low-confidence sequences will have lower agreement between predicted and real EPV changes, especially in transition-heavy possessions.

Veto 3: Counterattack EPV countermonotonicity veto  
The source example shows a case where real defensive compactness invalidates the expected attacking benchmark: the benchmark expects rapid space exploitation, but the defense “moves back as a whole team instantly” and the attack fails to react quickly [PAGE 7 of 8]. A verifiable veto can be defined using EPV and defensive area change. Let A_def,t be the area controlled by the defending team, and let ΔEPV_t = EPV_real(t) − EPV_bench(t). If the benchmark predicts an attacking surge, but the real defense expands control and real EPV fails to rise, the benchmark should be vetoed for that possession:

\[
V_{\text{counter}}=
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } \Delta EPV_t > \tau
\text{ and } A_{\text{def},t+1}-A_{\text{def},t} > 0
\text{ and } EPV_{\text{real}}(t+1)-EPV_{\text{real}}(t) \le 0,\\
0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]

Here τ is a decision threshold chosen from validation data, not a betting line or arbitrary ratio. This veto is verifiable because all terms are measurable: benchmark EPV, real EPV, and defensive controlled area.  
Prediction: the veto should fire most often in counterattack possessions where the defending team recovers quickly. In those cases, the paper’s benchmark is useful not because it blindly rewards the attacking team, but because it reveals that the real attack underperformed the context-adjusted expectation. If the benchmark is poorly calibrated, the veto will fire too often; if it is well calibrated, the veto will fire only when the real defensive layer proves that the predicted attacking surge was counterfactual.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
