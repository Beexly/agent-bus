Trap 3—experimenter drift: in multi-week studies, subtle cuing (Clever Hans effects) can slowly inflate discrimination performance, producing a retroactive illusion of stable perceptual capacity. These traps mean the references on [PAGE 93] are not static data points but longitudinally unstable measures whose interpretation depends on the training epoch under inspection.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: Tolman's core claim is that behavior is guided by expectancies rather than mere habits. Under crisis—overtraining, stress, or reward devaluation—the organism regresses to habitual S-R bonds. In the visual discrimination paradigm cited on [PAGE 93], a rat that has solved a brightness discrimination over many sessions may persist in responding to the formerly rewarded stimulus after contingency reversal, not because it cannot see the difference, but because the habit circuit has captured the decision.

Counter-trap: The experimenter observing reversal failure may infer a perceptual deficit ("the rat cannot discriminate brightness") when the true cause is habit inflexibility. This is a recursive theory-of-mind error: the experimenter models the rat as holding a fixed belief about stimulus contingency, failing to represent that the rat's own control layer has switched from expectancy-based to habit-based regulation.

Clock: The session clock introduces a fatigue layer. Let \(t\) be time within session in minutes. Internal noise variance grows as \(\sigma^2(t)=\sigma_0^2+\alpha t\), where \(\alpha\) is a fatigue-induced diffusion coefficient in contrast-units² per minute. If trials are not counterbalanced for time-of-day and session length, the brightness threshold estimate from Lashley II drifts upward late in the session. Breakdown: when \(\alpha t\gg\sigma_0^2\), the measured threshold is dominated by fatigue rather than sensation. Mistaking the fatigue threshold for the sensory threshold is the clock-induced counter-trap.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto: Brightness discrimination requires photopic luminance above absolute threshold. If stimulus luminance \(L<L_{\text{abs}}\approx1\,\text{cd/m}^2\) (photopic) or enters scotopic/mesopic ranges where rod intrusion occurs, the brightness threshold data in Lashley II ([PAGE 93]) are physically inadmissible because the single-channel cone assumption fails. Photopigment bleaching at \(L\gtrsim10^4\,\text{cd/m}^2\) is a complementary physical veto at the high end.

Biomechanical veto: Rat eye axial length is approximately 4–5 mm; pupil diameter \(D\) ranges roughly 1–4 mm. Diffraction-limited angular resolution is \(\theta_{\text{diff}}\approx1.22\lambda/D\) radians. At \(\lambda=550\,\text{nm}\) and \(D=3\,\text{mm}\), \(\theta_{\text{diff}}\approx2.24\times10^{-4}\,\text{rad}\approx0.77\) arcmin. However, retinal ganglion-cell sampling density in albino rats is lower than in pigmented rats. If the visual figure in Fields ([PAGE 93]) subtends less than the retinal sampling limit, behavioral failure is an optical veto, not a cognitive one.

Weather/laboratory veto: These are indoor vivarium studies, so direct weather effects are negligible, but uncontrolled barometric pressure shifts can alter middle-ear pressure and head posture during visual fixation, indirectly vetoing acuity measures if head position is not standardized. Uncontrolled window lighting introduces a luminance-confound veto.

Adjudication rule: Physical vetoes are absolute; biomechanical vetoes require a correction factor derived from optical modeling; experimental vetoes require protocol re-run. If any veto remains unresolved, the reference data on [PAGE 93] cannot support quantitative modeling without explicit caveat.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Replication of Lashley II brightness discrimination under equiluminant chromatic conditions.
Veto condition: If albino and pigmented rats show no strain difference on an equiluminant task but do differ on the luminance task, the original pigmentation effect in Lashley III ([PAGE 93]) is vetoed as an optical/luminance artifact rather than a true acuity difference.
Prediction: Performance on the equiluminant task will collapse to chance (\(d'\approx0\), \(H\approx F\approx0.5\)) for both strains, confirming that the original effect was carried by luminance contrast.

Scenario 2: Replication of Munn form discrimination with Fourier phase-scrambled stimuli.
Veto condition: If discrimination remains above chance for phase-scrambled stimuli that preserve the amplitude spectrum of the original shapes, the "form" effect in Munn ([PAGE 93]) is vetoed as a low-level Fourier/luminance confound.
Prediction: Rats will rely on local orientation and contrast-edge cues; phase-scrambled stimuli will retain above-chance performance, vetoing a pure global-form interpretation.

Scenario 3: Reversible inactivation (muscimol) of primary visual cortex before versus after overtraining in a brightness task.
Veto condition: If naive rats fail under inactivation (perceptual deficit) but overtrained rats perform normally under the same inactivation, the crisis habit regression is confirmed and a purely perceptual account is vetoed.
Prediction: Overtrained rats will show normal performance despite V1 inactivation, indicating a shift from cortical perceptual control to subcortical/striatal habit control. The apparent "recovery" is the counter-trap: habit, not perception, is driving behavior.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
