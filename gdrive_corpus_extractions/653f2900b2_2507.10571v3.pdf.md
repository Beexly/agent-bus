<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let the input be an RGB image $\mathbf{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}$ and label space $\mathcal{Y}=\{\text{healthy},\text{black-rot},\text{rust},\text{scab}\}$ [PAGE 5-6]. Each vision agent $a\in\{\text{GPT-4o},\text{Qwen}\}$ emits a triple $(\hat y_a, c_a, r_a)$ with confidence $c_a\in[0,1]$ and rationale $r_a$ [PAGE 3]. The confidence-aware orchestrator (Exp I–II) is a map
$$\mathcal{O}_{\text{conf}}:\{(\hat y_a,c_a,r_a)\}\mapsto \hat y^\star=\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\ \Phi\big(\{(\hat y_a,c_a,r_a)\}\big),$$
weighting by self-reported confidence with no image access [PAGE 6]. Its documented singularity is consensus failure: "when both fine-tuned agents confidently agree on an incorrect prediction, the orchestrator lacks a dissenting signal and cannot intervene" [PAGE 9].

Calibration operator (ECE), verbatim:
$$\mathrm{ECE}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{|B_m|}{n}\,\big\|\mathrm{acc}(B_m)-\mathrm{conf}(B_m)\big\|,$$
over bins $B_m$, $n$ samples [PAGE 5]. Overconfidence Ratio:
$$\mathrm{OCR}=\frac{|\{i:\hat y_i\neq y_i\wedge c_i>0.9\}|}{|\{i:c_i>0.9\}|}.$$
Consistency Gap: $\mathrm{CG}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \mathbb{I}[P_i^{(1)}\neq P_i^{(2)}]$ [PAGE 5]. Units are dimensionless probabilities on $[0,1]$; ECE and OCR are in probability units, CG in bits-equivalent indicator frequency. Empirical values [PAGE 23]: GPT ECE $=0.293$, OCR $=0.416$, CCC $=0.361$ ($p<10^{-4}$); Qwen ECE $=0.453$, OCR $=0.508$, CCC $=0.126$, CG $=0.009$.

Image-RAG operator: CLIP ViT-B/32 embeds $\mathbf{I}$ to $\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{512}$, $\mathbf{e}_i=\text{L2\_normalize}(\text{ViT}_{\text{CLIP}}(\mathbf{I}_i))$, similarity $\mathrm{sim}(\mathbf{e}_i,\mathbf{e}_j)=\cos\theta_{ij}$ [PAGE 6, Eq.1]. Retrieval $R_k=\arg\max_k\{\cos(\mathbf{e}_q,\mathbf{e}_i)\}$ [Eq.2] over FAISS IndexFlatIP. Weighted-vote confidence:
$$\mathrm{conf}(c)=\frac{\sum_{i\in R_k,\,y_i=c}s_i}{\sum_{j\in R_k}s_j},\quad s_i=\cos(\mathbf{e}_q,\mathbf{e}_i)$$
[Eq.3], dimensionless, sums to 1 across classes. Breakdown regime: IndexFlatIP is exact with $O(N)$ query complexity [PAGE 6]; latency grows linearly in corpus size, motivating ANN indexes at scale. Bayesian hyperparameter search used TPE maximizing Expected Improvement $\mathrm{EI}(x)$ via a likelihood ratio over $f(x)$ (validation loss) [PAGE 4]; the fine-tuned optimum reached val-loss $1.0\times10^{-5}$ (dimensionless cross-entropy) at LR $1.094\times10^{-5}$, 15 epochs [PAGE 5, A6].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from classical ensemble theory to trust-calibrated orchestration. The paper explicitly contrasts its method with "majority voting, mean averaging or confidence-weighted fusion," which "operate under the assumption that model outputs are statistically independent and equally reliable" [PAGE 13]. The intellectual ancestors are the calibration literature (ECE originates with Guo et al.-style reliability diagrams, here specialized to agents), uncertainty quantification surveys (He and Jiang 2023 [PAGE 17]), and the RAG lineage for medical/scientific decision support (Wada et al. 2025; Ke et al. 2025; Tozuka et al. 2025 [PAGE 17-18]). The distinctive genealogical move is to elevate "trust as a first-class component of agentic reasoning" rather than "an external interpretability add-on" [PAGE 12], positioning the orchestrator as a meta-reasoning layer per Sapkota et al.'s "AI agents vs. agentic AI" taxonomy (2026) [PAGE 17].

The multi-week trap embedded in this lineage is the transfer of hyperparameters: the authors avoided direct GPT-4o optimization because "the computational and financial cost of conducting such a process over multiple trials renders it impractical" [PAGE 4], instead transferring ResNet-50 Bayesian-optimal hyperparameters (10 epochs, batch 16) to GPT-4o. The trap: a config optimal for a ResNet-50 CNN ($O(10^7)$ params, dense gradients) need not be optimal for a frozen-vision large multimodal model, yet it "yielded a substantially lower validation loss (0.0088)" than default (0.0617) [PAGE 22, A2] — a validation-loss reduction that does not monotonically imply test-accuracy gain, a classic overfitting-to-validation hazard. The second trap is quantization drift: Qwen used "unsloth/Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct-bnb-4bit" with LoRA rank $r=16$, $\alpha=16$ in 16-bit while the "quantized backbone remained frozen" [PAGE 4]. Over a multi-week campaign of 50 trials (30 pruned by MedianPruner), the frozen 4-bit backbone caps achievable fidelity — a silent ceiling that a naive reading of the 95.63% accuracy would miss.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Model the agents as strategic-ish responders whose "crisis habit" is regression to a prior confident label under re-evaluation pressure. The paper quantifies exactly this. GPT refused the re-evaluation recommendation in 12.5% of cases (20/160), "returning the same prediction as before. However, in only 3 out of those 20 cases did this result in a correct prediction" [PAGE 10]. Qwen ignored re-evaluation in 29.38% of cases (47/160), "yet only 5 out of 47 such instances led to a correct outcome" [PAGE 10]. Interpreted through recursive ToM: the orchestrator models the agent's belief, issues a corrective nudge; the agent's meta-response is either revision (trust the orchestrator's model of my error) or entrenchment (defend prior). The empirically dominant strategy — revision — is correct, since entrenchment yields correctness only 15% (GPT) and 10.6% (Qwen) of the time.

The counter-trap is overcorrection: "GPT changed correct answers to incorrect ones 3 times (1.88%)... Qwen did so 22 times (13.75%)" [PAGE 11]. Here the orchestrator's theory-of-mind misfires: it injects retrieved "evidence" that flips a correct agent belief to wrong. Qwen's 13.75% overcorrection equals its ignore-rate symmetry, signaling an agent that is simply high-variance under prompt perturbation — consistent with CG $=0.009$ (near-total indifference to prompt formulation) and the lowest ECE-rank. The clock variable is the re-evaluation latency: Experiment III shows "a heavier tail in inference time distribution, yet remains within real-time thresholds" and "1.3× the inference time of zero-shot baselines" [PAGE 11]. The strategic equilibrium: trust-aware arbitration is worth ~37.5 accuracy points for a 1.3× time multiplier — a favorable exchange rate, but one that degrades if the re-evaluation loop is triggered on too many samples. The paper notes the loop "was triggered in 100% of instances due to low trust profiling scores" [PAGE 13], i.e., the threshold gate saturated — a design smell where the learned threshold collapsed to always-on, negating the intended selectivity.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Three physical/computational veto checks against the reported claims.

(1) Monotonic-gain veto. Claim: trust orchestration "captured over 77% of the possible gain" [PAGE 12]. Proof check: gain fraction $=(85.63-48.13)/(97.50-48.13)=37.50/49.37=0.7596$. This is $<0.77$, so the "over 77%" phrasing is falsified by the paper's own Table 1 numbers (0.7313, 0.8750, 0.8563, 0.9750). The 77.94% figure is a different quantity (relative lift over baseline) and is correct. Veto: the two 77% figures are conflated. Status: partial numerical veto on the Discussion sentence, not on the abstract.

(2) Retrieval-complexity veto. IndexFlatIP is exact $O(N)$ per query [PAGE 6]. With $N=512$ reference embeddings this is trivially real-time; the paper honestly concedes "For larger-scale deployment, approximate nearest neighbor indexing may be integrated" [PAGE 6]. No physical veto at this scale; the 1.3× latency claim is consistent with $O(N)$ exact search on 512 items plus one bounded re-evaluation pass.

(3) Consensus-failure veto. The operator $\mathcal{O}$ has a hard singularity: when both agents emit $(\hat y_{\text{wrong}}, c\to 1)$, the confidence-weighted argmax is locked onto the wrong class and "trust metrics" cannot separate a confidently-correct from a confidently-wrong agent without external grounding. The paper admits "residual failure cases remain, especially when both agents confidently agree on a wrong label. This scenario exposes the limits of current trust metrics to fully capture uncertainty in high-confidence false predictions" [PAGE 11]. This is an irreducible veto on the ECE/OCR/CG suite: all three are computed offline on a held-out profile set (512 training images presented label-free [PAGE 5]) and thus cannot condition on the current image's true correctness. Status: structural veto upheld — trust metrics are necessary but not sufficient; only Image-RAG provides per-instance external evidence.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario A — Retrieval poisoning. If the FAISS reference set contains mislabeled exemplars of rust/black-rot (the acknowledged visually-similar confusion pair [PAGE 6]), Eq.3 weighted voting concentrates $\mathrm{conf}(c)$ on the wrong class with high similarity mass. Prediction: re-evaluation would increase, not decrease, overcorrection; GPT's 1.88% overcorrection would climb toward Qwen's 13.75% regime. Falsifiable: perturb 10% of reference labels and re-measure orchestrator accuracy — expect a drop below 85.63%.

Scenario B — OCR threshold sensitivity. OCR is defined at the fixed cutoff $c>0.9$ [PAGE 5]. Counterfactually lowering the cutoff to $c>0.8$ admits Qwen's min confidence 0.800 [PAGE 22, A1] into the denominator, changing OCR $=0.508$. Prediction: OCR is not a threshold-invariant trust signal; the orchestrator's down-weighting of Qwen would strengthen or weaken purely as an artifact of the cutoff. Verifiable: recompute OCR at 0.8/0.85/0.95 and confirm monotonic drift.

Scenario C — Orchestrator disagreement leverage. Orchestrator beat Qwen in 21/22 disagreements (95.45%) but only 16/36 vs GPT (44.44%) [PAGE 10]. Counterfactual: replace GPT with a second weaker agent; the orchestrator's near-coin-flip vs GPT shows its advantage is concentrated against poorly-calibrated agents. Prediction: system accuracy is upper-bounded by $\approx$ the better agent's accuracy plus a small arbitration premium, and cannot exceed the fine-tuned ceiling (97.50%) without better agents or a visual orchestrator — exactly the authors' future-work concession that "o3-pro, o4-mini, or other variants with visual understanding could potentially improve orchestration" [PAGE 16].
