Let the action annotation set be
\[
\mathcal A=\{(t_i,c_i,v_i)\}_{i=1}^{N_a},\quad
N_a=110{,}458,\quad
t_i\in[0,T],\quad
c_i\in\{1,\ldots,17\},\quad
v_i\in\{\text{shown},\text{unshown}\},
\]
with \(t_i\) in seconds, \(c_i\) a class index, and \(v_i\) the binary visibility tag. The source states: “In total, we annotate 110,458 actions, on average 221 actions per game, or 1 action every 25 seconds” [PAGE 3], and it adds a “binary visibility tag that states whether the associated action is shown in the broadcast video or unshown, in which case the action must be inferred by the viewer” [PAGE 3]. For camera shots, let
\[
\mathcal C=\{(s_j,e_j,k_j,\tau_j)\}_{j=1}^{N_c},
\]
where \(s_j,e_j\) are start/end times in seconds, \(k_j\in\{1,\ldots,13\}\) is the camera class, and \(\tau_j\) is the transition type. The paper reports “158,493 camera change timestamps” [PAGE 4] and 13 camera possibilities [PAGE 4]. For replays, let \(\mathcal R=\{(r_m,a_m)\}\) pair a replay interval \(r_m\) with an action timestamp \(a_m\); the paper reports 32,932 replay shots linked to their actions [PAGE 4].

For action spotting, a predicted spot \((\hat t_j,\hat c_j)\) is a true positive at tolerance \(\delta\) if it matches an unmatched ground truth of the same class:
\[
\mathrm{TP}_j(\delta)=
\mathbf 1\!\left[\hat c_j=c_{m(j)}\ \land\ |\hat t_j-t_{m(j)}|\le\delta\right],
\]
with one-to-one assignment \(m(j)\). Then
\[
\mathrm{mAP}(\delta)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal C|}\sum_{c\in\mathcal C}\mathrm{AP}_c(\delta),
\qquad
\mathrm{Average\text{-}mAP}
=\frac{1}{60-5}\int_{5}^{60}\mathrm{mAP}(\delta)\,d\delta,
\]
where \(\delta\) is in seconds and the score is reported in percent. This formalizes the source statement: “A predicted action spot is positive if it falls within a given tolerance δ of a ground-truth timestamp from the same class,” and “the Average-mAP is the AUC of the mAP computed at different tolerances δ ranging from 5 to 60 seconds” [PAGE 5]. For camera segmentation over 13 classes,
\[
\mathrm{mIoU}
=\frac{1}{13}\sum_{k=1}^{13}
\frac{|P_k\cap G_k|}{|P_k\cup G_k|},
\]
a dimensionless fraction reported in percent, where \(P_k\) and \(G_k\) are predicted and ground-truth frame sets for class \(k\). For camera boundary detection, the same spotting mAP is used with “a single tolerance δ of 1 second” [PAGE 5]. For replay grounding,
\[
\mathrm{Average\text{-}AP}
=\frac{1}{60-5}\int_{5}^{60}\mathrm{AP}(\delta)\,d\delta,
\]
without class averaging, matching the statement that it is “computed as described for the spotting task but without averaging over the classes” [PAGE 5].

Operators and assumptions are source-specific. MaxPool and NetVLAD classify non-overlapping 20-second segments, then use a sliding window with stride 1 frame and NMS; NetVLAD uses 64 clusters [PAGE 5]. AudioVid pools “20-second chunks of ResNet features, as well as VGGish synchronized audio features, subsampled at 2 fps” [PAGE 5]. CALF handles “2-minute chunks of ResNet features” and outputs at most 15 predictions per chunk across 17 classes [PAGE 6]. Camera segmentation baselines include a basic 1D-CNN with “3 layers of 1D CNN with a kernel of 21 frames” [PAGE 7], CALF segmentation, and threshold detectors Content, Histogram, and Intensity; CALF detection uses “24-second input chunks of ResNet features” and “at most 9 detections per chunk” [PAGE 7]. Replay grounding uses Siamese networks; NetVLAD compares replay and chunk features with a cosine similarity loss “zeroed out when smaller than 0.4” [PAGE 8], while CALF concatenates replay-shot and chunk features along time and sets segmentation classes to 1 and detections to 1 [PAGE 8].

Assumptions and breakdown regimes:
- \(\delta\to 0\) is a label-noise regime, not a pure model regime: human-level action spotting is only “80.5%” [PAGE 16]. Very small \(\delta\) therefore measures annotation sharpness as much as model skill.
- \(\mathrm{AP}_c\) is undefined or unstable when a class has zero or very few test positives; the paper notes the dataset “is unbalanced, with some of the most important actions in the less abundant classes” [PAGE 3], and Table 2 shows rare-class counts as small as 8 and 14 [PAGE 7].
- Unshown actions are a separate singular regime: they “represent 18% of the actions” [PAGE 4] and “the audio features appear less useful on unshown instances” [PAGE 6].
- Logo transitions are a camera-boundary breakdown regime because “each logo is different and algorithms must adapt to a wide variety thereof” [PAGE 4]; “Intensity and Content are particularly bad on logos, while Histogram still performs well” [PAGE 7].
- Replay grounding has a context-window optimum, not monotonic improvement: “All the methods yield their best results with chunk sizes around 60 seconds” [PAGE 8], and CALF+S2 falls from 41.8 at 60 seconds to 14.4 at 240 seconds [PAGE 8].
- Source-internal consistency check: page 2 prose says ActivityNet has “849 hours of untrimmed videos” [PAGE 2], while Table 1 lists 648 hours [PAGE 4]. This is a metadata inconsistency, not a metric equation, and should be flagged in any automated extraction.
