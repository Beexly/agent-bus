<THINK_TRACE>
The source is a descriptive, single-team GPS observation study of 10 PFC Levski Sofia field players across four Bulgarian First League matches. The first multi-hop issue is scope: the abstract says the physical demands are “highly specific to the different positions on the field” [PAGE 1], and the methods say “4 official matches during 4 continuous weeks” were recorded [PAGE 2]. The same methods state “A total of 10 players from PFC Levski Sofia who participate in the First League” [PAGE 2], with 2 full-backs, 2 central defenders, 3 central midfielders, 2 wingers, and 1 striker. That means the striker position has \(n=1\), so any striker-level variance is undefined and position contrasts involving the striker are fragile.

The second hop is the measurement chain. The abstract says data were collected “using a 10-Hz GPS system” [PAGE 1], while the methods identify “GPS devices (Barin Sport Pro)” placed “between the players' shoulder blades” [PAGE 2]. The paper reports distance, sprints, accelerations, de-accelerations, maximum speed, and “HR zone” running distances, but it does not provide the numerical speed thresholds, HR thresholds, sprint-duration criteria, acceleration thresholds, or filtering methods. This is a gray-zone: the figures are labeled “HR zone” [PAGE 3] while the text also calls zone 5 “the highest-speed action of the players” [PAGE 3]. Heart-rate zones and speed zones are not interchangeable operators; without threshold definitions, the reported zone distances are not exactly reproducible.

The third hop is internal consistency. The abstract claims “The central midfielders ran the greatest total distances, and the edge defenders and forwards produced the highest rates of high-speed running, sprinting, and high-intensity acceleration distances” [PAGE 1]. Yet the results say “The wingers of the team made the most sprints in the match with 19 and the midfielders the least” [PAGE 2] and “The centre of defence also recorded the most accelerations and de-accelerations 125/116 and 127/84” [PAGE 2]. Later, the paper says “the defensive midfielder having travelled the greatest distance 11394 metres and the central striker at least 4645 metres due to his shorter playing time” [PAGE 4]. These statements are not necessarily contradictory if “central midfielders” includes offensive and defensive midfield roles, but the paper does not reconcile its position taxonomy. The “central striker at least 4645 metres” is explicitly confounded by exposure time, so raw total distance cannot rank work rate without minutes normalization.

The fourth hop is match-clock and exposure. The paper notes that “taking into account the recent changes and the ‘VAP’ (video assistant referee) the playing time in the whole match is 117:25 minutes” [PAGE 2]. Inclusion required players who “had played a minimum of 60 minutes in the study match” [PAGE 2], but 60 minutes is a wide window relative to 117:25. A player with 60 minutes and a player with 117 minutes are not comparable in total distance, sprint count, acceleration count, or zone distance. The paper gives team total distance as “90048 Metres” [PAGE 4] and says “About 9000 meters is the average distance per player” [PAGE 4], but it does not report per-player minutes for every case, so exposure-normalized rates cannot be reconstructed from the source text alone.

The fifth hop is inferential. The paper concludes that “the number of sprints and distances covered at high intensity (zones 4 and 5) are differentiated and will be specific according to the characteristics of the playing position” [PAGE 5], and it asserts “these are statistically significant differences” [PAGE 5]. But no test statistic, effect size, confidence interval, or multiplicity control is reported. With one team, four matches, and a striker \(n=1\), the claim is best treated as a descriptive pattern, not a stable causal position effect. The authors themselves state: “This study has limitations as the team tactics, the technical ability of the players, the importance of the match, the result that the game generates different features during the matches are not included” [PAGE 5]. That limitation is decisive: match state, opponent, score, and tactics are plausible confounders of high-intensity distance and sprint counts. The abstract also says “We did not find strong relationships between GPS metrics and the final match score” [PAGE 1], which further cautions against converting GPS volume directly into competitive superiority.

The gray-zone audit therefore yields a qualified conclusion: the source is useful for generating position-specific hypotheses and for illustrating GPS workload description, but it does not license precise threshold-free, exposure-free, weather-free, or score-free position templates. The formal operators below are the minimal definitions needed to make the source’s metrics explicit and to identify where they break down.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The paper does not report formal equations. The following are standard measurement operators required to reproduce its reported GPS quantities; they are not claimed as source-reported formulas. SI units are used unless noted.

1. GPS sampling operator. Let the receiver position be \(\mathbf{r}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^3\), sampled at \(t_k=k\Delta t\). The source’s 10-Hz system implies \(\Delta t=0.1\,\mathrm{s}\) [PAGE 1], with devices placed “between the players' shoulder blades” [PAGE 2]. The sampled trajectory is \(\{\mathbf{r}_k,\mathbf{v}_k,\mathbf{a}_k\}_{k=1}^{N}\). Assumption: the device is rigidly coupled to the thorax and satellite geometry is adequate. Breakdown: as \(\Delta t\to0\) with position noise, finite-difference velocity \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_k=(\mathbf{r}_{k+1}-\mathbf{r}_{k-1})/(2\Delta t)\) and acceleration \(\hat{\mathbf{a}}_k=(\mathbf{r}_{k+1}-2\mathbf{r}_k+\mathbf{r}_{k-1})/\Delta t^2\) amplify noise without bound; at 10 Hz, acceleration is especially sensitive to receiver noise and body oscillation. The paper reports no dilution-of-precision, satellite count, or filtering regime, so the acceleration/de-acceleration counts cited as “125/116 and 127/84” [PAGE 2] have an unquantified uncertainty floor.

2. Path-length distance. For a player with exposure interval \([0,T_p]\), total distance is
\[
D(T_p)=\int_0^{T_p}\|\mathbf{v}(t)\|\,dt \quad [\mathrm{m}],
\]
estimated by
\[
\hat{D}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\|\mathbf{r}_{k+1}-\mathbf{r}_k\| \quad [\mathrm{m}].
\]
The source’s team total is “90048 Metres” [PAGE 4], and the defensive midfielder is reported at “11394 metres” while the central striker is at “4645 metres due to his shorter playing time” [PAGE 4]. Assumption: path length is comparable only at equal or modeled exposure \(T_p\). Breakdown: \(D\) is not a rate; comparing raw \(D\) across unequal \(T_p\) confounds work volume with playing time. A legitimate exposure-normalized rate is
\[
R_D=\frac{D(T_p)}{T_p} \quad [\mathrm{m\,min^{-1}}],
\]
which is singular as \(T_p\to0\) and unstable near short exposures. The paper’s inclusion rule of “a minimum of 60 minutes” [PAGE 2] does not remove this problem because the maximum is 117:25 minutes [PAGE 2].

3. Zone-residence distance. For a thresholded physiological or kinematic variable \(x(t)\), define the zone indicator
\[
Z_j(t)=\mathbf{1}\{x_{\mathrm{lo},j}\leq x(t)\leq x_{\mathrm{hi},j}\},
\]
and zone distance
\[
D_j=\int_0^{T_p}Z_j(t)\|\mathbf{v}(t)\|\,dt \quad [\mathrm{m}].
\]
If \(x(t)\) is heart rate, \(D_j\) is an HR-zone distance; if \(x(t)\) is speed, \(D_j\) is a speed-zone distance. The paper uses “HR zone” labels [PAGE 3] but also describes zone 5 as “the highest-speed action of the players” [PAGE 3]. That is an operator mismatch unless the source defines a joint HR-speed threshold. The source does not give \(x_{\mathrm{lo},j}\), \(x_{\mathrm{hi},j}\), or hysteresis rules. Breakdown: at \(x(t)=x_{\mathrm{lo},j}\) or \(x(t)=x_{\mathrm{hi},j}\), \(Z_j(t)\) is discontinuous; small threshold changes can reclassify large distances. Without thresholds, the zone-distance operator is non-identifiable from the text alone.

4. Sprint and acceleration counts. Define a sprint count as
\[
N_{\mathrm{sprint}}=\sum_k \mathbf{1}\{v_k\geq v_{\mathrm{sprint}}\ \text{for duration}\ \geq \tau_s\},
\]
and an acceleration count as
\[
N_{\mathrm{acc}}=\sum_k \mathbf{1}\{a_k\geq a_{\mathrm{acc}}\}.
\]
The source reports “the most sprints in the match with 19” [PAGE 2] but does not state \(v_{\mathrm{sprint}}\) or \(\tau_s\). It cites Sweeting et al. on “When Is a Sprint a Sprint?” [PAGE 6], which reinforces that the definition is contested. Breakdown: counts are threshold-sensitive and sampling-sensitive; doubling the sampling rate can change the number of threshold crossings under noise. The acceleration counts “125/116 and 127/84” [PAGE 2] are therefore descriptive labels, not calibrated mechanical loads.

5. Zone-distribution information. Let \(p_j=D_j/\sum_j D_j\). The Shannon entropy
\[
H=-\sum_j p_j\log_2 p_j \quad [\mathrm{bits}]
\]
quantifies dispersion across zones. The paper’s Figure 6 reports “7%” in the 3-4-5 pulse-rate zone and compares it with “10.2%” from the 2010 World Championship [PAGE 4]. Assumption: \(D_{\mathrm{total}}>0\) and zones are mutually exclusive and exhaustive. Breakdown: if \(\sum_j D_j=0\), \(p_j\) and \(H\) are undefined; if zones overlap or thresholds are missing, \(H\) is not comparable across studies. The 7% versus 10.2% contrast is a descriptive proportion, not a causal statement about league quality.

6. Position-effect probability model. A verifiable model would be
\[
\log D_{im}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu+\beta_{\mathrm{pos}(i)}+\gamma_m+u_i,\sigma^2),
\]
where \(D_{im}\) is distance for player \(i\) in match \(m\), \(\beta_{\mathrm{pos}}\) is a position effect, \(\gamma_m\) is a match random effect, and \(u_i\) is a player random effect. Posterior probabilities such as \(P(\beta_{\mathrm{pos}}>\beta_{\mathrm{pos}'}\mid\mathrm{data})\) are dimensionless. Assumption: exchangeability and adequate repeated observations. Breakdown: with one team, four matches, and \(n=1\) striker, the posterior for striker position is dominated by the prior and the single player effect; position and player identity are not separable.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage runs from the institutional adoption of wearable tracking to modern position profiling. It states: “Two decades ago, the International Football Federation (FIFA) authorised the use of electronic performance tracking systems (GPS) in official competitions” [PAGE 1], and it notes that GPS “began its first applications on athletes in team games in 2006 (Aughey, 2011)” [PAGE 1]. The paper also separates external load, “indicating the distance travelled and the speed of the player” [PAGE 1], from internal load, “heart rate (HR) and subjective rating of perceived exertion (RPE)” [PAGE 1]. That distinction is the scientific inheritance: early GPS validity work enabled match-volume description; later work added high-speed and acceleration thresholds; contemporary work attempts position-specific templates. The cited Mohr, Krustrup and Bangsbo (2003) and Sweeting et al. (2017) lines mark two ancestral constraints: fatigue-related match performance and the definitional instability of “sprint.”

Retrocausally, the risk is reading today’s position-template expectations back into a four-match, single-team sample. The paper’s own abstract says the demands are “highly specific to the different positions” [PAGE 1], and its discussion says the high-intensity differences “will be specific according to the characteristics of the playing position” [PAGE 5]. But the design cannot separate position from player identity, opponent, match state, or tactical instruction. The multi-week trap is that “4 continuous weeks” [PAGE 2] sounds like a longitudinal design, yet four matches are still a short window for estimating stable position effects. Fixture congestion, recovery status, opponent strength, and score effects can dominate match-to-match variance. The paper admits “the importance of the match, the result that the game generates different features during the matches are not included” [PAGE 5]. A retrocausal genealogy should therefore treat the study as a descriptive ancestor of position profiling, not as evidence that a fixed position template is causally established.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive problem is that three agents are modeling one another: the analyst models the coach’s position template, the coach models the player’s role execution, and the player models what the coach rewards. Crisis habit regression appears when noisy GPS data trigger a fallback to stereotype: central defenders are labeled low-output because “Central defenders achieved the lowest values for all metrics” [PAGE 1], while the paper also says “The centre of defence also recorded the most accelerations and de-accelerations 125/116 and 127/84” [PAGE 2]. The stereotype ignores the distinction between volume, high-speed distance, and acceleration counts. The counter-trap is to replace one stereotype with another: for example, treating the striker’s “4645 metres due to his shorter playing time” [PAGE 4] as proof of low work rate, when the exposure clock is the more parsimonious explanation. The clock is the third agent: the 117:25-minute match duration [PAGE 2] and the 60-minute inclusion floor [PAGE 2] mean that every total-distance comparison is partly a comparison of minutes played. A recursive, counter-trap-aware reading would model playing time explicitly, report per-minute rates with uncertainty, and avoid telling players that a raw total-distance rank is a character judgment. The player’s counter-response—over-running to chase volume—can itself distort tactical discipline, which the paper values in its discussion of midfield “offering, covering, pressing, supporting the attack, balance” [PAGE 5].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto. The source’s measurement is GPS at 10 Hz [PAGE 1], with devices “placed between the players' shoulder blades” [PAGE 2] and the instruction that “Players were instructed to avoid handling the device once it was turned on” [PAGE 2]. No satellite geometry, calibration, or validity statistics are reported. If the position-specific differences are smaller than the unquantified GPS error, the physical layer vetoes strong rank claims. The paper reports maximum speed “32.19 km/h” [PAGE 2] and many small zone distances, such as “8 meters” for the left defender in zone 5 [PAGE 3]; small distances are especially vulnerable to sampling and threshold noise.

Biomechanical-layer veto. Acceleration and de-acceleration counts are not equivalent to mechanical load. The paper reports “125/116 and 127/84” for the centre of defence [PAGE 2] but gives no body mass, limb length, footwear, surface traction, or change-of-direction technique. Without those covariates, the biomechanical interpretation of acceleration counts as tissue load is vetoed. The paper’s statement that “speed-power work in the training process can be considered dominant” [PAGE 5] is a training-philosophy inference, not a biomechanical measurement.

Weather and surface veto. The match environment is singular: “Georgi Asparuhov stadium with a size of 105x68 meters, natural grass surface and air temperature of 13 degrees Celsius” [PAGE 2]. No wind, humidity, rain, or grass moisture is reported. High-intensity distance and sprint counts are weather- and surface-sensitive; therefore the “First League” profile cannot be generalized to hot, wet, artificial-turf, or windy conditions without a new measurement layer. The weather layer vetoes extrapolation beyond the reported micro-environment.

Tactical and match-state veto. The paper explicitly excludes “team tactics, the technical ability of the players, the importance of the match, the result” [PAGE 5]. Since high-intensity distance depends on game state and opponent defensive density—the paper itself invokes “the density of the defensive unit of the opposing team” [PAGE 4]—any claim that position alone explains the zone profile is vetoed until match state and opponent are modeled. The cross-layer adjudication is therefore: descriptive position differences are plausible; causal or universal position templates are not proven.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
