Let \(N=233\) be the analyzed sample and let \(G_i\) denote a demographic group indicator. The source’s raw proportion operator is

with units of dimensionless probability and reported as percentages in Table 1 [PAGE 3, PAGE 4]. For example, the source reports male \(=82.0\%\) and White \(=69.5\%\) [PAGE 1]. \[
\mathbb E[Y\mid R=1,G]\neq \mathbb E[Y\mid G],
\]
so the breakdown regime is non-ignorable nonresponse. The paper cannot identify non-respondents: “we cannot identify the non-respondents of our survey sample or characteristics about these non-respondents” [PAGE 4].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis-habit regression here is the habit of collapsing a contested organizational question into a binary “significant or not significant” result. Under sparse data, that habit can produce two errors: dismissing the raw female–male salary and discrimination gaps because tests failed to reject, or overclaiming that the raw gaps are proven population parameters. The source itself resists both: it says “we cannot make any generalizations from this result” about the racial-discrimination comparison [PAGE 17], and it says “more research is needed” to understand racial differences in EDI experience [PAGE 18]. The counter-trap is to preserve the difference between a descriptive sample gap and a population effect while still treating large, repeated raw contrasts as actionable signals. For example, females and males had similar agreement about fair processes for rewards and compensation, but females “strongly agreed” less often about equitable support, equitable workload, having a voice, and being taken seriously [PAGE 2, PAGE 9]. That is a theory-of-mind gap: the same formal process can be experienced differently by different groups. The clock matters because the survey is time-indexed: pre-COVID recall versus post-COVID satisfaction. The source reports that females reported more positive workplace changes but lower satisfaction due to the pandemic, and it explicitly says “we cannot conclusively comment on what is driving this trend” [PAGE 15, PAGE 18]. A recursive model should therefore hold three clocks separately: the retrospective 12-month pre-COVID clock, the survey-fielding clock in 2021, and the post-COVID organizational-change clock. Without longitudinal data, no trend, causation, or “crisis regression” can be inferred from one snapshot.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is not applicable in the physical, biomechanical, or weather sense. The source measures personal demographics, job characteristics, salary, EDI perceptions, discrimination experiences, and COVID-related workplace changes; it does not measure force, velocity, load, injury, surface, temperature, wind, or any biomechanical or meteorological variable [PAGE 2, PAGE 23, PAGE 24]. Formally, if the outcome is \(Y_i=f(G_i,X_i,Z_i,\varepsilon_i)\) with \(Z_i\) a physical/biomechanical/weather layer, the paper estimates only \(\mathbb E[Y\mid G,R=1]\) with a limited survey covariate set. Because \(Z_i\) is unmeasured and unmanipulated, the physical-layer veto \(V_{\text{phys}}\) is not identified. Any attempt to use weather, biomechanics, or physical performance to override the EDI findings would be a category error: salary, discrimination, workload, voice, and belonging are organizational and survey constructs, not biomechanical outcomes. The only admissible veto layers are statistical and measurement layers: nonresponse, small strata, midpoint imputation, top-coding, self-defined discrimination, and aggregation of heterogeneous racialized groups [PAGE 4, PAGE 6, PAGE 17].
