The source’s central multi-hop problem is not “do streaks occur?” but “what would count as evidence for a hot hand, and what breaks that inference?” The first hop is definitional. The review distinguishes two interpretations: serial dependence between successive attempts, and non-stationarity in an athlete’s success probability [PAGE 539]. The source states that “both common interpretations of the hot hand belief, either sequential dependence between successive attempts or the non-stationarity claim, have been extensively examined” [PAGE 539]. That matters because a test tuned to autocorrelation can miss a time-varying success probability, and a test tuned to non-stationarity can miss dependence. The second hop is the vote-count trap. The review reports “only two more non-supportive than supportive studies (13 compared to 11, respectively)” [PAGE 526], but immediately warns that demonstrations are “rare and often weak” because of model, definition, aggregation, and sport-specific constraints [PAGE 526]. Thus, counting studies is not a valid likelihood-ratio; the design and power of each study must be audited. The third hop is statistical power. Miyoshi’s simulations found that Gilovich-style tests “could detect, on average, only 12% of all the hot hands phenomena in the simulated records” [PAGE 545]. Wardrop’s simulation work similarly found that the runs test and test of fit had little power unless the hot-state success probability was much larger than the base rate [PAGE 545]. Therefore, failure to reject a Bernoulli null is weak evidence of absence when the hot period is short or the effect is small. The fourth hop is confound auditing. The review’s task-segregation discussion notes that basketball and baseball are noisy because of shot selection, defensive pressure, pitcher ability, and strategic adjustment, while golf, darts, and bowling remove some team-play confounds [PAGE 547]. Yet even in cleaner tasks, Clark found that observed golf streaks “were related to the difficulty of the golf courses rather than to any inherent tendency of players to be streaky” [PAGE 542]. Frame et al.’s bowling result is partly attributed to lower seeds bowling continuously while higher seeds waited [PAGE 543]. Berry and Wood’s kicking analysis controlled for distance, turf, indoor or outdoor status, and weather conditions [PAGE 549]. These are not decorative caveats; they are alternative causal pathways that can generate apparent streaks without any athlete-level hot state. The fifth hop is aggregation and definition drift. Wardrop showed that aggregating the Boston Celtics free-throw data produces the fan-perceived pattern, but the source calls this “a cognitive statistical illusion” rather than evidence for the hot hand [PAGE 542]. Forthofer’s 17 “streak shooters” became 10 after Wardrop removed shaky cases [PAGE 541-542]. Hales defines hotness by existence rather than statistical rarity [PAGE 548], which shifts the question from model comparison to epistemology. The gray-zone audit is therefore this: the review supports a limited, contingent conclusion. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(X_t\in\{0,1\}\) be the outcome of attempt \(t\), with \(1\) denoting success. The canonical sequential-dependence operator is
\[
\Delta=\mathbb{P}(X_t=1\mid X_{t-1}=1)-\mathbb{P}(X_t=1\mid X_{t-1}=0),
\]
where \(\Delta\) is dimensionless probability or, when multiplied by 100, percentage points. The hot-hand hypothesis is \(\Delta\gt 0\); the Bernoulli null assumes \(X_t\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)\) independently with fixed \(p\in(0,1)\). The review describes the standard statistical description as “p(hit/hit)>p(hit/miss), usually combined with the runs count test” [PAGE 548]. Assumptions: exchangeable trials, fixed base rate, no task-difficulty drift, and no defensive or weather confound. Breakdown regimes: \(\Delta\) is unidentified when there are no successes after successes or no successes after misses; zero cells make the conditional probabilities unstable. If \(p=0\) or \(p=1\), the Bernoulli model is degenerate and logit-based tests separate perfectly, causing coefficient divergence.

The runs operator is
\[
R=\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{X_t\ne X_{t-1}\}+1,
\]
with \(R\) in counts and \(N\) in trials. Under the Bernoulli null with \(n_1\) successes and \(n_0\) failures,
\[
\mu_R=\frac{2n_1n_0}{n_1+n_0}+1,\qquad
\sigma_R^2=\frac{2n_1n_0(2n_1n_0-n_1-n_0)}
{(n_1+n_0)^2(n_1+n_0-1)},
\]
and
\[
z_R=\frac{R-\mu_R}{\sigma_R}.
\]
The \(z_R\) statistic is dimensionless. Singularity: \(\sigma_R=0\) when \(n_1=0\), \(n_0=0\), or \(N=1\); then \(z_R\) is undefined. The review notes that the runs test has “little power to detect non-stationarity” in regime-shifting models [PAGE 545], so a non-significant \(z_R\) is not a strong veto of a transient hot state.

The first-order autocorrelation operator is
\[
\rho_1=
\frac{\sum_{t=2}^{N}(X_t-\bar X)(X_{t-1}-\bar X)}
{\sum_{t=1}^{N}(X_t-\bar X)^2},
\]
dimensionless, with breakdown when all outcomes are identical and the denominator is zero. The review reports that in one data set the “overall mean was .02, and none of the values was statistically significant” [PAGE 537], but this is a dependence test, not a direct test of piecewise non-stationarity.

The non-stationarity model can be written
\[
X_t\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_t),\qquad
p_t=
\begin{cases}
p_B, & t\notin \mathcal{H},\\
p_H, & t\in \mathcal{H},
\end{cases}
\]
with \(p_H\gt p_B\), \(p_B,p_H\in(0,1)\), and \(\mathcal{H}\) a finite hot interval. The effect size is \(p_H-p_B\), in probability units. Breakdown: if \(p_H-p_B\) is small relative to sampling error, or if \(|\mathcal{H}|\) is short, conventional tests have low power; Miyoshi’s result of “only 12%” detection is the relevant calibration [PAGE 545]. The review warns that “the simple binomial model may not be a proper normative model for evaluating the validity of the hot hand belief” [PAGE 544].

A Markov-switching operator is
\[
\mathbb{P}(S_t=H\mid S_{t-1}=C)=q_{CH},\qquad
\mathbb{P}(S_t=C\mid S_{t-1}=H)=q_{HC},
\]
with \(q_{CH},q_{HC}\in[0,1]\) dimensionless transition probabilities and state-specific success probabilities \(p_H,p_C\). Breakdown: if \(p_H=p_C\) the states are non-identifiable; if \(q_{HC}=q_{CH}=0\) the process reduces to two disconnected regimes; label switching and flat likelihood regions can make estimation unstable. The review describes a regime-shifting model in which a player has “a fixed probability of switching back and forth between ‘hot’ and ‘cold’ regimes” [PAGE 545].

The logistic operator used in baseball studies is
\[
\operatorname{logit}\mathbb{P}(Y_{ij}=1)=\alpha_i+\beta X_{i,j-1}+\gamma^{\top}Z_{ij},
\]
where \(Y_{ij}\) is the outcome of at-bat \(j\) for player \(i\), \(X_{i,j-1}\) is previous success, and \(Z_{ij}\) contains situational covariates. \(\beta\) is in logit units per previous success; it is dimensionless on the logit scale. Breakdown: perfect separation drives \(\beta\to\infty\); small within-player variation gives very low power. The review states that the logistic model used in one baseball study had “almost no power to detect streak hitting at the individual player level” [PAGE 532]. These operators, units, assumptions, and singularities are the formal core; any claim that ignores them is a toy inference.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from Tversky and Kahneman’s “belief in the law of small numbers” [PAGE 538] through Gilovich, Vallone, and Tversky’s 1985 basketball report, which reframed fan intuition as a testable conditional-probability and runs problem [PAGE 536]. The review says Gilovich et al. found “no evidence of the hot hand phenomenon in any of their data sets” [PAGE 536], and that players’ shot probabilities were “usually somewhat lower after having made shots than after having missed shots” [PAGE 537]. The next genealogical branch is the rejoinder and critique literature: Larkey, Smith, and Kadane argued for contextually manageable chunks and singled out Vinnie Johnson, but Tversky and Gilovich replied that “the 7 out of 7 sequence by Johnson turned out not to have happened” [PAGE 541]. That correction is a retrocausal trap: a celebrated positive case was contaminated by coding error. The third branch is aggregation and Simpson’s paradox. Wardrop’s reanalysis showed that pooled Celtics free-throw data can make fans’ perception look correct, but the source calls this a “mental equivalent of Simpson’s paradox” and “a cognitive statistical illusion” [PAGE 542]. The fourth branch is the adaptive-heuristic turn. Burns argued that streak-following can allocate the ball to better shooters, with an advantage of “about one extra basket per seven or eight games” [PAGE 546]; Gula and Raab argued that the belief should be judged by behavioral adaptiveness as well as normative validity [PAGE 540-541]. The fifth branch is task segregation: golf, darts, horseshoes, bowling, and tennis were brought in to reduce basketball’s defensive and shot-selection confounds [PAGE 547]. Smith found that “world-class pitchers do have modest hot and cold spells” [PAGE 543], while Dorsey-Palmateer and Smith found bowlers whose strike proportion was higher after consecutive strikes [PAGE 543]. The sixth branch is the power critique: Wardrop, Miyoshi, Frame et al., and Dorsey-Palmateer and Smith all showed that standard tests can miss realistic non-stationarity [PAGE 544-545]. The multi-week traps follow directly. First, treating a weekly or monthly streak as proof of a stable trait confuses within-season variation with repeatable skill; Albright found some season-specific streakiness but not consistent four-year streakiness [PAGE 539]. Second, pooling players creates Simpson-style reversals [PAGE 542]. Third, choosing the hot-hand definition after seeing the data invites definition drift: dependence, non-stationarity, and Hales-style “existence” are not interchangeable [PAGE 548]. Fourth, regression to the mean and course or opponent difficulty can manufacture multi-week “slumps” and “surges” [PAGE 542]. Fifth, publication and file-drawer asymmetry favor dramatic positive cases over null replications, which is why the review’s 13-to-11 count is not a stable posterior [PAGE 526]. The genealogical lesson is that the debate evolved from a simple fallacy claim to a model-comparison problem with adaptive-behavior and task-segregation branches; any multi-week inference that ignores that lineage is historically naive.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive model has three layers. At layer 0, the athlete experiences an internal state: confidence, arousal, rhythm, or felt “hotness.” The review notes that athletes believe in the hot hand because they have experienced it, and that success and self-efficacy are linked to performance [PAGE 535]. At layer 1, the coach or teammate infers “this player is hot” and allocates attempts toward that player. At layer 2, the opponent infers that the coach will allocate toward the hot player and adjusts defense. At layer 3, the coach anticipates that overplay and must decide whether the hot-hand cue remains valid relative to base rates, matchup, and tactical spacing. This is where crisis habit regression appears: under time pressure and emotional load, agents regress to the low-cost layer-1 heuristic. The review’s warning is precise: “Passing the ball to the hot player, who is guarded closely by the opposing team, may be a non-optimal strategy if other players who do not appear hot have a better chance of scoring” [PAGE 546]. The counter-trap is therefore not “never pass to a hot player.” It is “do not let the opponent’s model of your hot-hand belief become your decision rule.” If the defense overplays the perceived hot player, the counter-trap is to use the attention as a decoy and attack the resulting imbalance. The clock layer changes the inference. Adams examined inter-shot intervals and found the mean Hit-Hit interval was slightly longer than the Hit-Miss interval, opposite to a simple time-dependent hot-hand expectation [PAGE 540]. Berry and Wood examined a timeout interval before pressure kicks and found evidence consistent with an “icing” effect, while cautioning about the small sample of 38 icing attempts [PAGE 549]. Thus the clock is not a neutral variable: short within-game intervals, shot-clock pressure, timeouts, and long breaks can all change arousal, routine, and defensive adjustment. A recursive agent should therefore represent three quantities: the athlete’s internal state, the opponent’s belief about that state, and the time structure that allows or blocks adaptation. The crisis-habit failure is to collapse all three into “he is hot, give him the ball.” The counter-trap is to keep the belief as one cue among base rate, defensive attention, and clock. The review’s conclusion that the hot hand should be treated as “information to be used together with other cues” [PAGE 549] is the correct recursive policy.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The veto rule is formal. Let \(Y_t\) be success, \(H_t\) previous success, and \(C_t\) a vector of physical, biomechanical, and weather covariates. The naive hot-hand contrast is
\[
\Delta_{\text{marg}}=
\mathbb{P}(Y_t=1\mid H_t=1)-
\mathbb{P}(Y_t=1\mid H_t=0).
\]
If success probability depends on \(C_t\), then
\[
\Delta_{\text{marg}}=
\mathbb{E}\{p(C_t)\mid H_t=1\}-
\mathbb{E}\{p(C_t)\mid H_t=0\}
+\mathbb{E}\{\Delta(C_t)\},
\]
where the first term is covariate imbalance and the second is the conditional hot-hand effect. If the first term is nonzero and the second is zero, a hot-hand claim is vetoed. This is the physical and biomechanical veto: fatigue, continuous bowling effort, shot selection, defensive pressure, golf course difficulty, and weather can all make \(H_t\) correlate with \(p(C_t)\). The review provides concrete adjudications. In golf, Clark’s additional analysis showed that “the observed streaks were related to the difficulty of the golf courses rather than to any inherent tendency of players to be streaky” [PAGE 542]. In bowling, Frame et al.’s winners won more than 50 percent of subsequent games, but the review notes this “could be partly attributed to the effort exerted by the lower seeds who had to bowl continuously while the higher seeds were waiting” [PAGE 543]. That is a physical-effort veto. In horseshoes, Smith argued that the task is cleaner because “every pitch is made from the same distance at regular intervals” [PAGE 543], which reduces biomechanical and strategic variance; that is why the modest hot-hand evidence there is more credible, not because horseshoes are magical. In football kicking, Berry and Wood used a multi-variable model accounting for kicker, distance, turf type, indoor or outdoor games, and weather conditions to isolate pressure and timeout effects [PAGE 549]. That is the correct weather-veto design: estimate the conditional effect after stratifying or adjusting for environmental covariates. In basketball, the task itself is confounded by shot distance, angle, defensive pressure, and shot selection [PAGE 547]. Therefore, a basketball streak that disappears after controlling for shot quality and defender proximity is not evidence of a hot hand. The cross-layer veto is not a denial of all hot states; it is a priority rule: physical, biomechanical, and weather explanations must be ruled out before an athlete-level non-stationary state is inferred. The review’s task-segregation discussion supports exactly that priority [PAGE 547-548].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: known-regime power veto. Construct a synthetic record with \(N=100\), \(p_B=0.50\), \(p_H=0.70\), and a hot window of length \(L=10\). Apply the runs test and the Gilovich-style test of fit. Prediction: if the implementation reproduces Miyoshi’s calibration, detection will be near the reported “only 12%” level [PAGE 545]. Veto rule: any claim that a non-significant test disproves the hot hand is vetoed unless the claimant reports a power curve showing adequate detection for this regime. This is verifiable by pre-registered simulation and exact seed reproduction.

Scenario 2: temporal-locality veto. Stratify shots by inter-shot interval \(\tau\) and estimate
\[
\Delta(\tau)=\mathbb{P}(Y_t=1\mid H_t=1,\tau)-
\mathbb{P}(Y_t=1\mid H_t=0,\tau).
\]
The locality hypothesis predicts that \(\Delta(\tau)\) should be larger at short \(\tau\). Prediction from Adams: in the reviewed NBA data, the mean Hit-Hit interval was \(17{:}04\) s and the Hit-Miss interval was \(16{:}39\) s, a direction opposite to the simple time-dependent expectation and not significant [PAGE 540]. Veto rule: if \(\Delta(\tau)\) does not increase as \(\tau\) decreases, the “hot hand only within short time spans” claim is vetoed. This is verifiable by interval-stratified reanalysis with confidence intervals.

Scenario 3: environmental-confound veto. In golf or kicking data, fit a model with a hot-state indicator plus course difficulty, weather, turf, distance, and player identity. Prediction from Clark: the apparent golf streakiness should vanish or shrink after conditioning on course difficulty, because the review says the streaks “were related to the difficulty of the golf courses rather than to any inherent tendency of players to be streaky” [PAGE 542]. Prediction from Berry and Wood: timeout and pressure effects must be estimated while accounting for weather and distance [PAGE 549]. Veto rule: if the hot-state coefficient is non-significant after these covariates are included, the marginal streak claim is vetoed. This is verifiable by coefficient stability across stratified and adjusted models. Across all three scenarios, the central prediction is the same: the hot-hand debate is not settled by a single significance test; it is settled by model comparison under realistic power, temporal resolution, and confound control. The review’s final position—limited evidence, small effect, open question [PAGE 525, 548, 550]—is the correct adjudication.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
