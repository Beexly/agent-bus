GSE/GSI frame: this paper is a tracking-by-detection system whose central claim is that a bi-directional motion-memory mechanism can repair identity continuity under short occlusion without appearance features. The multi-hop derivation starts from the online constraint: “the online tracking mode uses only the current frame and past frames for matching” [PAGE 1]. Therefore the tracker cannot inspect the future to verify a re-identification; it must maintain a belief state, called a “stranded area,” for tracks that fail the first match [PAGE 1], [PAGE 6]. The first hop is detection: an anchor-free DLA-34 backbone produces heatmap peaks, center offsets, width and height, edge offsets, and two motion-vector branches [PAGE 5], [PAGE 7]. The second hop is first matching: backward motion vectors predict where current detections were in the previous frame, then a distance matrix is formed and greedy matching is performed with confidence ordering [PAGE 6], [PAGE 9]. The third hop is the gray zone: if a detection is unmatched, it may be new, or it may be a previously occluded object returning. The paper chooses the latter hypothesis by storing unmatched tracks in the stranded area and updating their positions with forward motion vectors [PAGE 6], [PAGE 10]. The fourth hop is deletion: each stranded track receives a life value that is reduced over time and removed at zero [PAGE 10]; the initial value is 20 frames because “more than 70% of the occlusion durations in the MOT17 dataset are less than 20 frames” [PAGE 12]. The fifth hop is evaluation: MOTA is dominated by detection errors, while IDF1 and IDs are more sensitive to association continuity; the source itself says “detection performance has a greater impact on MOTA than tracking performance” [PAGE 11].

The gray-zone audit reveals several non-cosmetic issues. First, the distance veto is described as follows: “When the distance between two objects is larger than one of their bounding box area, it is changed to the infinity” [PAGE 9]. This is dimensionally inconsistent if taken literally: Euclidean distance is in pixels, while bounding-box area is in square pixels. A formal implementation must use a characteristic length such as the square root of the area, or a width/height-derived scale, otherwise the threshold changes with object size in a non-physical way. Second, the proposed association is greedy, not the Hungarian maximum-weight matching described in the related-work discussion [PAGE 4]. Greedy matching is order-dependent, so ties and crowded crossings can produce different identities depending on confidence sorting. Third, the method deliberately avoids appearance features: “we focus on exploring effective and efficient matching algorithms without using appearance features of objects” [PAGE 2]. That makes the system fast but removes a powerful veto against identity swaps when two pedestrians cross or when the camera moves. Fourth, the life value is a clock, not a semantic occlusion model: an occlusion longer than the stranded-memory window is structurally unrecoverable, no matter how accurate the forward motion vector is. Fifth, the synthetic occlusion experiment increases random masking and reports that “the performance gain of the bi-directional tracking algorithm is increasing with the increase of occlusion rate” [PAGE 15], which is plausible but not identical to real occlusion geometry, camera motion, or crowd-induced detection dropout.

The key derivation for the reader is therefore a conditional one. If the hidden object reappears within the life window and its forward-predicted center remains inside the distance threshold, the second match can recover the old ID. If either condition fails, the system reverts to its default habit: assign a new ID. That is the crisis habit regression. The counter-trap is the stranded area plus forward motion. The clock is the life-value decay. This triad explains both the paper’s gain and its boundary conditions. The paper reports 63.4 percent MOTA, 55.3 percent IDF1, and 20.1 FPS on MOT17 [PAGE 1], [PAGE 16], but the ablation against CenterTrack is much narrower: 60.3 versus 59.7 MOTA, 61.5 versus 61.0 IDF1, and 0.9 versus 1.2 IDs [PAGE 13]. Thus the headline result and the controlled association gain must not be conflated. The controlled gain is real but modest; the occlusion sweep is where the mechanism’s intended regime is clearest.

\hat{\mathbf c}_{i}^{t-1}=\mathbf c_i^t+\mathbf v_i^{t\to t-1},
\]

with units \(\mathrm{px}\). The stranded-area forward update over a horizon \(\Delta\) frames is

\[
\hat{\mathbf c}_{i}^{t+\Delta}=\mathbf c_i^t+\Delta\,\mathbf v_i^{t\to t+1},
\]

also in \(\mathrm{px}\). The distance matrix is

\[
D_{ij}=\left\|\hat{\mathbf c}_{i}^{t-1}-\mathbf c_j^{t-1}\right\|_2,
\]

in \(\mathrm{px}\). The source’s veto rule is quoted exactly: “When the distance between two objects is larger than one of their bounding box area, it is changed to the infinity” [PAGE 9]. Literally this compares \(\mathrm{px}\) with \(\mathrm{px}^2\); a dimensionally coherent repair is to define a characteristic length \(\ell_i=\sqrt{A_i}\in\mathrm{px}\) and use

\[
\tilde D_{ij}=
\begin{cases}
D_{ij},  & D_{ij}\leq T_{ij},\\
+\infty, & D_{ij}>T_{ij},
\end{cases}
\qquad
T_{ij}\in\{\ell_i,\ell_j,\max(\ell_i,\ell_j),\min(\ell_i,\ell_j)\},
\]

depending on implementation. The paper’s prose does not resolve which variant was used. Greedy matching sorts detections by confidence \(s_j\in[0,1]\) and selects

\[
j^*(i)=\arg\min_{j\in\mathcal U}\tilde D_{ij},
\]

accepting the match only if \(\tilde D_{ij^*}<\infty\). This is greedy, not a global Hungarian assignment; the paper describes maximum weighted matching in related work [PAGE 4] but implements distance-based greedy matching [PAGE 8], [PAGE 9].

The heatmap label is described as “a two-dimensional normal distribution” centered at the object center, with radius \(r\) obtained from width and height and hyperparameter set to 0.7 [PAGE 7]. A formal reconstruction is

\[
Y_{xy}=\exp\left[-\frac{(x-c_x)^2}{2\sigma_x^2}-\frac{(y-c_y)^2}{2\sigma_y^2}\right],
\qquad
\sigma_x,\sigma_y \text{ derived from } r,
\]

with \(Y_{xy}\in[0,1]\) dimensionless. The heatmap loss uses Focal Loss [PAGE 7], with \(\alpha=2\) and \(\beta=4\) [PAGE 12]:

\[
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{heat}}
=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{xy}
\begin{cases}
(1-\hat p_{xy})^{\alpha}\log \hat p_{xy}, & y_{xy}=1,\\[4pt]
\hat p_{xy}^{\alpha}(1-y_{xy})^{\beta}\log(1-\hat p_{xy}), & y_{xy}<1.
\end{cases}
\]

Here \(N\) is the number of detected objects, \(\hat p_{xy}\in[0,1]\) is predicted probability, and \(y_{xy}\in\{0,1\}\) is ground truth. The channel attention is a squeeze-excitation form [PAGE 7]:

\[
\mathbf z'=\sigma\!\left(W_2\,\delta\!\left(W_1\mathbf z\right)\right),
\]

where \(\sigma\) is a sigmoid and \(\delta\) is a nonlinearity. The spatial attention concatenates global average and global maximum pooling along channels and applies a \(7\times7\) convolution plus sigmoid [PAGE 7]:

\[
\mathbf M_s=\sigma\!\left(\mathrm{Conv}_{7\times7}\left(
\left[\mathrm{AvgPool}_c(\mathbf F);\mathrm{MaxPool}_c(\mathbf F)\right]\right)\right).
\]

The total loss is a weighted sum of six branch losses [PAGE 8]:

\[
\mathcal L=\sum_{k}\lambda_k\mathcal L_k,
\]

with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{size}}=0.1\) because “the scale of object sizes is very much larger compared with that of the motion vectors and other outputs” [PAGE 8]. The stranded-area clock is discrete:

\[
L_i(t+1)=\max\!\left(0,L_i(t)-1\right),
\qquad
L_i(t_0)=20\ \mathrm{frames},
\]

and “The objects will be removed when their life values become 0” [PAGE 10]. The metric equations on [PAGE 11] are standard and the displayed bodies are omitted in extraction:

\[
\mathrm{MOTA}=1-\frac{\sum_t\left(FN_t+FP_t+IDSW_t\right)}{\sum_t GT_t},
\qquad
\mathrm{IDF1}=\frac{2\,IDTP}{2\,IDTP+IDFP+IDFN}.
\]

All are dimensionless ratios. Breakdown regimes are exact: \(D_{ij}=+\infty\) removes the edge; an all-infinite row leaves the detection unmatched; \(D_{ij}=0\) creates tie ambiguity resolved only by confidence order; heatmap peaks below threshold produce no detection; \(L_i=0\) deletes the track; occlusion duration exceeding the life window makes second matching structurally impossible; and wrong-sign or scale-corrupted motion vectors move the predicted center outside the threshold, producing a forced new ID. The source’s own boundary is visible in Table 4: at 30 percent synthetic occlusion, bi-directional IDF1 is 15.8 versus 8.3 for single-directional, and IDs are 9.07 versus 13.57 [PAGE 15]. That is a relative improvement inside a regime where both methods have already degraded sharply.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from radar tracking and Kalman filtering in the 1960s to tracking-by-detection. The paper opens by saying “Kalman filters are used to track radar-searched targets in earlier methods” [PAGE 1], then moves through SORT, which removes appearance extraction for real-time speed [PAGE 3], DeepSORT, which reintroduces appearance and cascade matching [PAGE 3], JDE, which integrates feature extraction into detection [PAGE 3], and CenterTrack, which uses CenterNet and greedy matching [PAGE 3]. The proposed method is a descendant of this lineage but deliberately strips appearance features again [PAGE 2], replacing them with bi-directional motion memory and a stranded area. The attentional up-sampling module descends from DLA-Net, CBAM-style channel and spatial attention, and the anchor-free decoder tradition [PAGE 3], [PAGE 6], [PAGE 7]. The paper explicitly contrasts its attention module with deformable convolution, saying deformable convolution’s per-kernel offsets make parallel optimization difficult and increase training time [PAGE 3]; Table 2 reports 19.86M parameters and 1.13 minutes per epoch for the attention model versus 20.47M and 3.86 minutes for deformable convolution [PAGE 13].

The retrocausal trap is that later success stories can make the lineage look more deterministic than it was. CenterTrack’s greedy nearest-neighbor matching was fast but brittle under occlusion; DeepSORT’s cascade matching was more robust but appearance-heavy; the present paper tries to recover cascade-like memory without appearance. The multi-week traps are methodological. First, online/offline leakage: the paper is online [PAGE 1], so any comparison that grants future frames to the proposed method invalidates the claim. Second, life-value tuning: the initial value 20 is justified by the MOT17 occlusion-duration distribution, not by a universal physical law [PAGE 12]. A multi-week evaluation on longer occlusions would expose this. Third, synthetic masking: the occlusion sweep in Table 4 is a controlled stress test, but random masks do not reproduce real crowd occlusion, camera ego-motion, or lighting change. Fourth, speed comparisons: 20.1 FPS is reported for the proposed method [PAGE 16], but FPS is hardware- and implementation-dependent, so cross-paper speed rankings are weak evidence. Fifth, metric dominance: MOTA is detection-dominated, while IDF1 and IDs are association-sensitive [PAGE 11]; a method can improve association and still look modest on MOTA. Sixth, leaderboard context: GSDT reports 66.2 MOTA and 68.7 IDF1 but only 4.9 FPS, while the proposed method reports 63.4 MOTA, 55.3 IDF1, and 20.1 FPS [PAGE 16]. That is a speed-accuracy trade, not a universal dominance claim. The correct genealogical reading is that this paper is a motion-only, real-time branch of the tracking-by-detection tree, optimized for short occlusions, not a general re-identification solution.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The tracker’s recursive theory of mind is a belief state over a hidden pedestrian. In the crisis frame, the baseline habit is nearest-neighbor greed: when an object is occluded, the detector produces no box, the track is dropped, and on reappearance the greedy matcher treats the returning object as new. The paper’s own example is target-65, which is “almost completely obscured by target-43 in the middle frame,” and CenterTrack “give[s] it a new ID 109 when target-65 re-appear[s]” [PAGE 13], [PAGE 14]. That is crisis habit regression: the system falls back to the only identity rule it has left, spatial proximity in the current frame.

The counter-trap is the stranded area. Instead of deleting the unmatched track immediately, the tracker stores it and keeps updating its position with the forward motion vector, “simulating the movement of a target behind an occlusion in the actual world” [PAGE 9], [PAGE 10]. When the object reappears, the second matching stage tries the stranded track before assigning a new ID; “Only the object that fails to match in the second matching stage, it will be considered as a newly emerged object and be assigned a new ID” [PAGE 6]. This is a recursive belief update: the tracker does not observe the hidden object, but it maintains a counterfactual trajectory for it. The belief is updated each frame, and the second match is a test of that belief against the next observation.

The clock is the life value. The stranded area is not infinite memory; it is a decaying hypothesis. The paper says “the objects are given a life value when they enter the stranded area, and the score is reduced over time,” with removal at zero [PAGE 10]. The initial value of 20 frames is calibrated to the fact that most MOT17 occlusions are shorter than 20 frames [PAGE 12]. This creates a precise cognitive boundary: if the object returns before the clock expires and the forward prediction is accurate enough, the old identity is recovered; if the clock expires first, the belief is deleted and the returning object is treated as new. The system’s theory of mind is therefore shallow but explicit: it models persistence, not intent, gait, or appearance. That is why it is fast, and that is why it fails under long occlusion, camera motion, or crowded crossings where two pedestrians share the same predicted spatial corridor.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto 1: occlusion duration. The stranded-area clock is \(L_i(t_0)=20\) frames and decrements to deletion [PAGE 10], [PAGE 12]. If the true occlusion span \(\tau_{\mathrm{occ}}\) satisfies \(\tau_{\mathrm{occ}}>L_i(t_0)\), the track is removed before reappearance, so second matching cannot recover the ID. This is a hard veto, not a tuning preference. The paper’s own justification is distributional: “more than 70% of the occlusion durations in the MOT17 dataset are less than 20 frames” [PAGE 12]. The complement is the failure regime.

Physical veto 2: spatial threshold. The second match requires the forward-predicted center to remain within the distance veto of the returning detection. The source says distances larger than the bounding-box area are set to infinity [PAGE 9]. If camera ego-motion, crowd flow, or a pedestrian turn adds displacement error \(\mathbf e\), the predicted center error grows with the horizon \(\Delta\). Under a constant-velocity assumption, a lateral acceleration \(a\) contributes approximately \(\tfrac{1}{2}a\Delta^2\) in pixels; this is a kinematic error model, not a fake ratio. When that error exceeds the effective threshold, the edge becomes infinite and recovery is vetoed.

Biomechanical veto: a pedestrian is not a rigid translating point. Turning, stopping, crouching, or limb motion changes the bbox center and shape. The paper uses only center distance and motion vectors, with no appearance feature [PAGE 2]. Therefore a biomechanically plausible turn during occlusion can make the forward vector point to the wrong corridor. The stranded track may then match a different pedestrian or fail to match the correct one. This is especially dangerous when two people cross, because the nearest predicted center may belong to the wrong body.

Weather and sensor veto: the MOT17 benchmark includes “fixed camera tracking, mobile camera tracking, night tracking, and indoor tracking” [PAGE 12]. Rain, glare, darkness, and motion blur reduce detector confidence. If no heatmap peak passes threshold, there is no detection to match, and the stranded-area prediction has no observation to test. The paper’s appearance-free design means there is no independent re-identification veto under such conditions [PAGE 2]. The method can still recover a short dropout if the object returns within 20 frames and the motion vector remains accurate; it cannot adjudicate identity when the observation itself is missing or corrupted.

Adjudication rule: a claimed recovery is valid only when three conditions hold simultaneously: the occlusion is shorter than the life window, the forward-predicted center stays inside the effective distance threshold, and the detector produces a box for the returning object. If any condition fails, the correct prediction is an ID switch, a new ID, or a missed track, not a successful recovery.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: life-value counterfactual. Fix the detector, motion branches, and matching threshold; vary only the initial stranded life value \(L_0\in\{5,20,50\}\) on the same MOT17 clips with known occlusion spans. Verifiable prediction: \(L_0=5\) will reduce recovery for occlusions longer than 5 frames and increase IDs relative to \(L_0=20\); \(L_0=50\) will recover more long occlusions but will increase false re-identifications in crowded scenes because stale tracks survive too long. The source’s own \(L_0=20\) is explicitly tied to the MOT17 occlusion-duration distribution [PAGE 12], so this is a direct test of that calibration.

Scenario 2: synthetic occlusion sweep replication. Re-run the Table 4 protocol at 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 percent random masking, holding detection architecture and training fixed. Verifiable prediction: bi-directional matching should improve IDF1 and reduce IDs relative to single-directional matching as occlusion rate rises. The paper reports at 30 percent: IDF1 15.8 versus 8.3 and IDs 9.07 versus 13.57 [PAGE 15]; at 0 percent: IDF1 61.5 versus 60.2 and IDs 0.88 versus 1.04 [PAGE 15]. A replication that reverses this ordering would veto the claimed occlusion mechanism.

Scenario 3: motion-vector corruption and threshold-unit test. Corrupt only the forward motion vector by sign flip or by scaling it 2 times, leaving all else fixed. Verifiable prediction: second-match recovery should collapse and IDs should rise, because the stranded track is moved away from the true reappearance corridor. Separately, replace the source’s literal area-based veto [PAGE 9] with a characteristic-length threshold \(\sqrt{A}\) in pixels and compare match sets. Verifiable prediction: the literal area rule will behave inconsistently across large and small bounding boxes because it compares pixels with square pixels; the repaired length threshold will produce scale-consistent veto behavior. If the two implementations produce identical match sets on all clips, the unit concern is empirically inert for that dataset; if they diverge, the source’s prose is underspecified and the divergence identifies the operative threshold convention.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
