The source’s core probabilistic operator is logistic regression. For a feature vector \(x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) and parameter vector \(\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\),
\[
p_{\theta}(y=1\mid x)=\sigma(\theta^{\top}x)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\theta^{\top}x)},\qquad y\in\{0,1\}.
\]
Here \(p_{\theta}\) is a dimensionless probability in \([0,1]\). The fitted loss is
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[y_i\log p_{\theta}(y=1\mid x_i)+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_{\theta}(y=1\mid x_i))\right]+\lambda\|\theta\|_{q},
\]
with \(q\in\{1,2\}\) and \(\lambda\in\{0,1,5,10,25,50,100,250,500,1000\}\) selected on a development window [PAGE 5]. The log-loss is in nats when the logarithm is natural. Assumptions: independent game samples, correct feature scaling, and a linear log-odds relation. Breakdown regimes: perfectly separable data drive \(\|\theta\|\to\infty\), \(p_{\theta}\to 1\), and the Hessian toward singularity; strongly collinear features make the Hessian ill-conditioned; L1 regularization is non-differentiable at zero; a poorly scaled L2 penalty can over-shrink informative coefficients.

The unigram feature operator is
\[
x_{a,u}=\log(1+c_{a,u}),
\]
where \(a\in\{\text{home},\text{away}\}\), \(u\) is a unigram, and \(c_{a,u}\) is the count of that unigram in weekly tweets assigned to side \(a\). The source requires the pair to occur in at least \(0.1\%\) of the weekly tweets for that game before the game takes place [PAGE 6]. The value is dimensionless but nat-scale in information terms. Breakdown: \(c_{a,u}=0\) gives \(x_{a,u}=0\); rare-token thresholds change the support discontinuously; a burst of one token can dominate a sparse game sample.

The CCA operator is defined for paired views \(x_1\in\mathbb{R}^{m_1}\) and \(x_2\in\mathbb{R}^{m_2}\). It seeks
\[
\max_{w_1,w_2}\ \rho(w_1,w_2)=
\frac{w_1^{\top}\Sigma_{12}w_2}
{\sqrt{w_1^{\top}\Sigma_{11}w_1}\sqrt{w_2^{\top}\Sigma_{22}w_2}},
\]
with the projected components \(z_i=W_i^{\top}x_i\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\) and a diagonal correlation matrix in the projection space [PAGE 6]. Assumptions: \(\Sigma_{11}\) and \(\Sigma_{22}\) are positive definite, the paired samples are aligned by game, and \(k\) is below the effective rank. Breakdown: if \(\Sigma_{11}\) or \(\Sigma_{22}\) is singular, the ratio is undefined; if \(m_i\gg N\), sample covariance is rank-deficient; constant or duplicated features collapse components; \(k>\operatorname{rank}(\Sigma)\) makes the projection non-identifiable. The source’s own warning is that the unigram view is “extremely high-dimensional” [PAGE 6].

The rateS operator is
\[
\operatorname{rateS}(v_{\mathrm{old}},v_{\mathrm{curr}},\Delta)
=
\operatorname{sign}(v_{\mathrm{curr}}-v_{\mathrm{old}})
\left\lfloor
\frac{|v_{\mathrm{curr}}-v_{\mathrm{old}}|}{\Delta}
\right\rfloor
\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\},
\]
where \(v_{\mathrm{old}},v_{\mathrm{curr}}\in\mathbb{Z}\) are tweet counts and \(\Delta\in\mathbb{N}\) is a count threshold [PAGE 6–7]. The source reports, “we found that the rateS feature worked best with vold = vprev and ∆= 500” [PAGE 7]. Units: counts per week for \(v\), counts for \(\Delta\); the output is an ordinal level. Breakdown: \(\Delta=0\) is undefined; negative volumes are outside the count interpretation; the floor operation saturates at \(\pm2\), so large changes become indistinguishable.

The rateP operator is
\[
\operatorname{rateP}(v_{\mathrm{old}},v_{\mathrm{curr}},\theta)
=
\operatorname{sign}(v_{\mathrm{curr}}-v_{\mathrm{old}})
\left\lfloor
\frac{|v_{\mathrm{curr}}-v_{\mathrm{old}}|}{\theta\,v_{\mathrm{old}}}
\right\rfloor
\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\},
\]
with \(\theta\in(0,1]\) dimensionless [PAGE 6–7]. Assumptions: \(v_{\mathrm{old}}>0\), because the denominator is \(\theta v_{\mathrm{old}}\). Breakdown: \(v_{\mathrm{old}}=0\) gives division by zero and an undefined feature; \(\theta=0\) is excluded by the domain; \(v_{\mathrm{curr}}=v_{\mathrm{old}}\) gives sign \(0\) and level \(0\). Any implementation must mask or impute zero-history cases rather than silently producing infinities.

The temporal-window operator is
\[
W_{\mathrm{weekly}}=[s_{\mathrm{prev}}+12\,\mathrm{h},\ s_{\mathrm{next}}-1\,\mathrm{h}],
\quad
W_{\mathrm{pregame}}=[s_{\mathrm{next}}-24\,\mathrm{h},\ s_{\mathrm{next}}-1\,\mathrm{h}],
\quad
W_{\mathrm{postgame}}=[s_{\mathrm{prev}}+4\,\mathrm{h},\ s_{\mathrm{prev}}+28\,\mathrm{h}],
\]
where \(s\) denotes game start time in hours [PAGE 3]. Assumptions: start times are known; game duration is bounded enough that postgame windows do not overlap the next pregame window. Breakdown: missing end times, delayed games, doubleheaders, and overtime can blur the boundaries; the source notes that other thresholds gave similar results [PAGE 3, footnote 7].

Evaluation is reported as a dimensionless proportion,
\[
\widehat{A}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}[\widehat{y}_i=y_i],
\]
where \(N\) is the number of games. The source reports an average postgame classification accuracy of \(67\%\) [PAGE 4] and states that the final online strategy “correctly predicted the winner 63.8% of the time” over 12 weeks and 177 games [PAGE 8]. Assumptions: test games are exchangeable enough for a single proportion to be meaningful; breakdown: weekly nonstationarity, class imbalance, and feature-selection churn make a single aggregate hide regime switches.

The genealogy runs from “wisdom of crowds” aggregation [PAGE 1] through Twitter-based prediction of polls, elections, disease spread, and financial markets [PAGE 1], into sports forecasting models such as Harville, Stern, Glickman and Stern, Knorr-Held, and Gimpel [PAGE 2, references]. The paper’s technical ancestry also includes Hotelling’s canonical correlation analysis [PAGE 10, reference 13] and multi-view regression via CCA [PAGE 10, reference 14]. The retrocausal lesson is that the 2010–2012 dataset encodes assumptions from older forecasting cultures: weekly cadence, stable team identities, and the belief that aggregate text is a leading indicator rather than a reactive trace. The source itself warns that earlier sentiment-based work “only had Twitter data from one season (2009)” and did not use it in primary forecasting experiments [PAGE 2]. That is a lineage trap: a one-season result can look causal because the season’s regime is fixed, but multi-week replay exposes nonstationarity.

Multi-week traps are explicit in the paper. The season is 17 weeks with roughly one game per team per week [PAGE 2], so every model update sees very few independent samples. Online tuning uses weeks \([1,k-3]\) for training and weeks \([k-2,k-1]\) for development [PAGE 5]; with such short windows, a feature set that wins two weeks can lose the next two. The source observes that “the best feature set changes very frequently” [PAGE 8]. That churn is the central multi-week trap: selection itself becomes a nonstationary process, and the analyst can mistake a weekly fluctuation for a stable signal. Weeks 1–3 and week 17 are excluded for different reasons—thin history and atypical late-season games [PAGE 5, footnote 8]—which means the evaluated regime is already a filtered subset. A second trap is volume growth: yearly tweet counts rise sharply across the three seasons [PAGE 3, Table 2], so rate features can encode platform adoption rather than team-specific attention. A third trap is lexicon drift: postgame language shifts with wins, losses, and referee controversy [PAGE 4], so a classifier trained on early weeks may learn a sentiment habit that is stable for fans but not for forecasting. The genealogical verdict is that the paper is a bridge between crowd-aggregation traditions and online sports forecasting, but its short-season design makes every multi-week conclusion provisional.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive loop has three agents: fans model the team, the model models the fans, and the evaluator models the model. Crisis habit regression appears in the postgame classifier. After a loss, fan language concentrates on blame and negative evaluation; the source finds that “we surmise that fans are more likely to comment on the referees (“away: refs”) after their team loses than after a win” [PAGE 4]. The classifier can therefore learn a habit: negative tokens predict loss, positive tokens predict win, and the model appears accurate because it has learned the fans’ crisis script rather than the game’s causal structure. The average postgame accuracy is \(67\%\) [PAGE 4], which is useful for sentiment lexicon building but not proof that pregame tweets forecast the contest.

The counter-trap is leakage through reaction. If postgame tweets enter a pregame prediction, the model sees the outcome after the fact. The paper’s clock is designed to block this: weekly, pregame, and postgame windows are separated [PAGE 3], and the evaluation is “strictly online” with no future information [PAGE 5]. The recursive theory-of-mind point is that a model can simulate fan anticipation before the game, but it cannot know whether a volume spike means “confidence,” “anxiety,” “injury rumor,” or “a rival fanbase mocking the team.” The model’s theory of fan mind is underdetermined without the physical layer. The clock resolves part of the ambiguity by enforcing causal order: pregame text can only use information available before kickoff; postgame text is a reaction, not a forecast. The remaining trap is that weekly volume itself is recursive—fans respond to the previous game, the previous game responds to physical events, and the model responds to the fans. A model that treats volume as an exogenous signal will overfit the loop. A model that treats volume as a noisy measurement of attention, conditioned on team identity and clock, is better licensed.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Let \(Y\) be the game outcome, \(X\) the tweet-derived features, and \(Z\) the physical/biomechanical/weather state: temperature \(T\) in K, wind speed \(W\) in m/s, humidity \(h\) in percent, precipitation in mm/h, travel distance in km, rest interval in days, and injury status as a categorical vector. The tweet pipeline observes \(X\), not \(Z\). A structural veto applies when \(Z_t\notin\operatorname{supp}(Z_{\mathrm{train}})\), because then the conditional \(P(Y\mid X)\) learned in training is not licensed at test time. Twitter volume cannot intervene on wind, temperature, injury, or fatigue; therefore \(do(X=x)\) does not physically fix \(Y\) while \(Z\) changes. The correct adjudication is: if a physical variable crosses the training support, the tweet-only prediction is vetoed regardless of model confidence.

The source supports this caution indirectly: it lists “extreme gameday temperatures” among factors that can distort outcomes [PAGE 2, footnote 4], and its feature table is dominated by game statistics rather than environmental measurements [PAGE 5, Table 4]. The physical veto proof is simple. If \(Z\) changes the play-calling distribution and the injury state, then \(Y\) changes through mechanisms that \(X\) does not measure. A tweet spike may correlate with a storm, a rivalry, or a prime-time slot, but correlation is not an intervention. Biomechanical vetoes include a starting quarterback injury, an offensive-line injury cluster, or a short-rest road trip; weather vetoes include high wind, heavy precipitation, and extreme heat or cold. Units matter because thresholds must be stated in physical scales, not in tweet counts. The admissibility condition is \(Z_t\in\operatorname{supp}(Z_{\mathrm{train}})\) and no material injury-state change; otherwise the model must abstain or be reconditioned with physical covariates. This is a cross-layer veto, not a sentiment adjustment.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
