Recursive theory of mind enters at two orders. First-order: the organism represents \(E[M_L \mid M_E]\), the expected state of the load end given effort-end commerce. Second-order (arguably absent in nonhuman animals in this context, but present in the human experimenter): the experimenter represents the animal’s representation, \(E_{\text{exp}}[E_{\text{animal}}(M_L \mid M_E)]\), and manipulates the contingency to test whether the animal’s behavior tracks the represented relation or merely the sensory consequence.

If an animal continues to press a lever after the load end is disconnected, it may be exhibiting habit regression, not tool understanding. The diagnostic is the transfer test: can the animal apply the same lever relation to a novel tool geometry (different arm lengths, novel material) without re-learning from scratch? Successful transfer supports a represented lever operator \(\mathcal{T}\); failure supports chain-like sensory chaining.

Thus the “clock” is not metaphorical but a real temporal integration window. Breakdown regime: when \(\Delta t = t_L - t_E > \tau_{\text{crit}}\), mutual information \(I(M_E; M_L)\) collapses because the organism’s perceptual system tags the events as causally unrelated. Species differences in \(\tau_{\text{crit}}\) (apes likely exceeding rats) may account for part of the phylogenetic gradient Tolman describes [PAGE 118], though the rat footnotes [PAGE 118] caution against treating the boundary as absolute.

Biomechanical capacity is second-order: if the animal cannot generate the required force, the expectancy remains unexecuted. Environmental persistence is third-order: if the tool degrades mid-session, the expectancy is violated retrospectively. The veto stack demonstrates that Tolman’s cognitive “expectations” [PAGE 118] are necessary but not sufficient; they are gated by physical and biomechanical boundary conditions.
