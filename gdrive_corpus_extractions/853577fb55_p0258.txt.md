<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The investigation into optical illusions forms a critical lineage in the history of experimental psychology, marking the transition from philosophical introspection to rigorous psychophysics.

**Historical Lineage:**
The roots trace back to Johannes Müller’s doctrine of specific nerve energies, which established that the quality of a sensation depends on the nerve stimulated, not the external object. Hermann von Helmholtz advanced this by introducing the concept of "unconscious inference" [PAGE 258], arguing that perception is a probabilistic hypothesis testing mechanism, akin to scientific induction. In this view, the brain unconsciously calculates distances and angles based on past experiences. Zöllner's discovery of his eponymous illusion in 1860 provided a devastating counter-example to pure empiricism; the illusion persists even when the observer intellectually knows the lines are parallel.

Max Wertheimer and the Gestalt school later weaponized these failures of local analysis. They argued that the whole (the configuration of the Zöllner figure) dictates the parts, opposing the elementalism of Wundt. However, William James, writing in 1890, occupies a unique pivot point. He recognizes the flaw in Helmholtz’s intellectualism—if the premises are unconscious and variable, how can they be consistently measured? [PAGE 258] He pushes the explanation down to the "primitive stage of perception," to the raw physiology of the retina and optic nerve.

**Multi-Week Traps and Epistemic Hazards:**
A recurring trap in the genealogy of perception science is the **"Cognitive Capture" loop**. Whenever a physiological explanation hits a wall (e.g., explaining why we don't just see a smeared image due to saccades), researchers historically leapfrogged to "cognitive" explanations—top-down expectations, memory schemas, or semantic knowledge. 
In modern contexts, this manifests as the over-reliance on Bayesian Brain models where priors are treated as magical explanatory variables. The trap is circular: "Why do you perceive X?" "Because your prior said so." "How did you get that prior?" "From perceiving X before."
James explicitly warns against this "spurious account" [PAGE 258]. To fall into this multi-week trap today is to mistake correlation for causation in neuroimaging data—seeing activation in the prefrontal cortex during an illusion task and claiming it "causes" the illusion, ignoring that the PFC might simply be monitoring the conflict generated by a purely subcortical/sensory error.
