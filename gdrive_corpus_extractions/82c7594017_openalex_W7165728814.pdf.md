The derivation begins with the design, not the conclusion. The source is a 13-page randomized crossover study of 12 male collegiate American football players. Participants completed a separate Baseline session and then, in counterbalanced order, a physical-fatigue (PF) and physical-mental mixed-fatigue (PMF) session, with at least a 1-week washout and total data collection within 4 weeks [PAGE 3]. The first inferential hop is therefore: because the design compares post-fatigue measurements against a separate Baseline session rather than same-visit pre/post measures, any observed effect is a bundle of (a) the fatigue protocol, (b) session/day variability, and (c) repeated testing effects. The authors explicitly concede the latter: the design “does not account for potential day-to-day biological variability in landing biomechanics” [PAGE 11]. This is a major gray-zone constraint on causal strength.

The second hop is induction validity. PF used cycle ergometry: 1 min unloaded warm-up, 50 W initial load, +50 W every 3 min to 200 W, then maintenance until the fatigue threshold. Successful induction was defined as RPE ≥ 17 or heart rate ≥ 85% of age-predicted maximum [PAGE 3]. PMF added a Stroop task with four Chinese color words, 50% incongruent stimuli, 1,000 ms stimulus duration, 1,000 ms blank interval, and 1,500 ms response window, with staff alerts for errors or omissions [PAGE 3]. The achieved protocols were similar in duration: 24.5 ± 3.2 min for PF and 24.3 ± 3.6 min for PMF, with p > 0.05, and both exceeded the HR/RPE criteria [PAGE 5]. Thus the physical-load layer is credible. But the cognitive layer is not independently verified: “cognitive task performance was not monitored” [PAGE 10], and “subjective mental fatigue was not assessed using a specific scale (e.g., VAS or NASA-TLX)” [PAGE 11]. So “PMF” here means a composite protocol, not a purified mental-fatigue construct.

The third hop is the outcome hierarchy. The paper measured hip, knee, and ankle angles at initial contact and peak vGRF, peak angles, range of motion, joint contribution ratios, continuous relative phase (CRP) indices, and kinetics including peak vGRF, time to peak vGRF, loading rate, and lower limb stiffness [PAGE 4]. The significant findings are specifically ankle-local: decreased ankle dorsiflexion at peak vGRF (p = 0.003, F = 7.393, Eta2 = 0.402; Baseline vs PMF p = 0.011), increased ankle inversion at peak vGRF (p = 0.004, F = 7.385, Eta2 = 0.402; Baseline vs PMF p = 0.004), and increased peak ankle inversion (p = 0.005, F = 6.834, Eta2 = 0.383; Baseline vs PMF p = 0.012) [PAGE 5]. Hip/knee kinematics, RoM, joint contribution strategy, MARP/DP coordination indices, and global impact indicators did not significantly differ [PAGE 5; PAGE 7; PAGE 8]. There was a main effect for T_vGRF (p = 0.029, F = 4.189, Eta2 = 0.276), but Bonferroni pairwise comparisons did not survive the adjusted threshold (p > 0.0167) [PAGE 5]. This distinction matters: a main effect without a pairwise difference is not evidence of a specific PF-vs-PMF or PMF-vs-Baseline contrast.

The fourth hop is mechanism. The authors propose that PMF interferes with central resource allocation and sensorimotor integration, reducing pre-activation and reactive control of the ankle dorsiflexors and evertors, producing a “less stable and more injury-prone joint position during the landing buffering phase” [PAGE 8]. They further propose that the kinetic chain redistributed loads to “mask the distal deficits caused by PMF, thereby maintaining overall landing stability” [PAGE 8]. But this compensation is inferred, not directly measured: no EMG, EEG, musculoskeletal modeling, or corticomuscular coherence was collected. The authors admit that “discussions regarding compensatory strategies remain somewhat speculative without direct physiological evidence” [PAGE 11]. Thus the mechanism is plausible but underdetermined.

The fifth hop is injury inference. Increased inversion and reduced dorsiflexion are associated with ankle-sprain and Achilles-loading risk in the cited literature, but this study measured no injury outcomes. The strongest defensible claim is local kinematic risk potential, not observed injury. The paper itself says the ankle may assume a “potentially more injury-prone position” [PAGE 11]. The gray-zone audit therefore rejects three overclaims: (1) that PMF globally degraded coordination—false, since MARP/DP and contribution ratios were null [PAGE 5; PAGE 8]; (2) that PMF increased global impact load—false, since peak vGRF, LR, and Kleg were null [PAGE 8, Table 4]; (3) that elite compensation proves safety—unsupported, because “stability” may mask local loading [PAGE 10]. The remaining defensible claim is narrower: in this laboratory protocol, in this small male collegiate sample, PMF was associated with ankle-specific kinematic changes without global kinetic changes.

The source’s measurement operators are defined on the landing phase from initial contact to maximum knee flexion. Initial contact is the threshold event:
\[
t_{\mathrm{IC}}=\inf\{t\ge 0:\mathrm{vGRF}(t)>10\,\mathrm{N}\},
\]
and the landing phase is normalized from 0 to 100% between \(t_{\mathrm{IC}}\) and the time of maximum knee flexion [PAGE 4]. Kinematic data were sampled at 200 Hz and force data at 1,000 Hz; kinematic coordinates were low-pass filtered at 10 Hz and force data at 50 Hz using a fourth-order Butterworth filter [PAGE 3; PAGE 4].

The two explicit kinetic equations are:
\[
\mathrm{LR}=\frac{\mathrm{Peak\ vGRF}}{T_{\mathrm{vGRF}}},
\]
where vGRF is normalized to body weight (BW) and \(T_{\mathrm{vGRF}}\) is in milliseconds, giving \(\mathrm{LR}\) in BW/ms as tabulated [PAGE 4; PAGE 8]; and
\[
K_{\mathrm{leg}}=\frac{\mathrm{Peak\ vGRF}}{\Delta L},
\]
where \(\Delta L\) is the vertical displacement of the hip joint center from initial contact to the lowest point of the center of mass, corresponding to maximum knee flexion, giving \(K_{\mathrm{leg}}\) in BW/m [PAGE 4]. The source states: “Lower limb stiffness (Kleg, unit: BW/m) was determined as the ratio of peak vGRF to the change in lower limb length (ΔL)” [PAGE 4]. Note the operational gray zone: the variable is named a change in lower limb length, but \(\Delta L\) is actually hip-center vertical displacement, not a direct limb-length measurement.

The source also reports two dimensionless joint-contribution ratios: knee-hip flexion contribution ratio \(=\) peak knee flexion / peak hip flexion, and knee-ankle flexion contribution ratio \(=\) peak knee flexion / peak ankle dorsiflexion [PAGE 4]. These are source-defined descriptive ratios, not independent mechanistic equations; they are reported as null across conditions [PAGE 7, Table 2].

For inter-joint coordination, the paper defines amplitude-frequency normalization of joint angles and angular velocities, then a relative phase angle \(\phi\) via a four-quadrant phase diagram, then:
\[
\mathrm{CRP}(i)=\phi_{\mathrm{distal}}(i)-\phi_{\mathrm{proximal}}(i),
\]
\[
\mathrm{MARP}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\mathrm{CRP}(i)\right|,
\]
and DP as the standard deviation of CRP over the time series:
\[
\mathrm{DP}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathrm{CRP}(i)-\overline{\mathrm{CRP}}\right)^2}.
\]
These definitions are given on [PAGE 4] and [PAGE 5]. The source says “MARP reflects the average coupling relationship (in-phase/anti-phase) of a joint pair, while DP represents the variability of the CRP curve throughout the time series” [PAGE 4]. Units: CRP, MARP, and DP are angular-phase quantities; the paper’s tables report numerical values without printing degrees in Table 3, so the unit should be treated as degrees by dimensional inference, with the caveat that the source does not print the unit marker there [PAGE 8, Table 3]. The normalization equations (3 and 4) are partially garbled in the supplied text extraction; the defensible reading is amplitude-frequency normalization of angular velocity and min-max normalization of angle. I do not invent a replacement formula where the source typography is corrupted.

Statistical operators are also formal: one-way repeated-measures ANOVA for normally distributed data, Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests, Mauchly’s sphericity test with Greenhouse-Geisser correction if violated, Bonferroni pairwise correction, and Friedman with Wilcoxon signed-rank post hoc for non-normal data [PAGE 5]. The reported effect size is \(\eta^2\), dimensionless; \(F\) and \(\chi^2\) are dimensionless test statistics. The Bonferroni pairwise threshold for three conditions is \(0.05/3=0.0167\), which the paper uses when it says “p > 0.0167” [PAGE 5].

Singularity and breakdown regimes:
1. \(\mathrm{LR}\) diverges as \(T_{\mathrm{vGRF}}\to 0^{+}\). The reported values are finite—62.50 ± 20.90 ms at Baseline, 54.20 ± 12.40 ms PF, 51.70 ± 17.00 ms PMF [PAGE 8, Table 4]—but very short or noise-triggered peak times would make LR unstable. A 10 N threshold for initial contact can also shift \(T_{\mathrm{vGRF}}\) if baseline noise crosses threshold.
2. \(K_{\mathrm{leg}}\) diverges as \(\Delta L\to 0^{+}\). A stiff or barely displacing landing, or marker/model error in hip-center displacement, makes the ratio unstable. The reported Kleg values are finite (4.14 ± 0.98, 4.61 ± 1.18, 4.78 ± 1.40 BW/m) [PAGE 8, Table 4].
3. CRP/MARP/DP break down if the normalization denominator is zero, i.e., if a joint angle has zero range or angular velocity has zero maximum absolute value; phase is then undefined. DP also becomes unstable or degenerate at \(N=0\) or \(N=1\), and discontinuity in Euler-angle extraction can create artificial phase jumps. The source does not report a gimbal-lock or phase-unwrapping audit [PAGE 4; PAGE 5].
4. ANOVA inference breaks if normality or sphericity assumptions fail without correction; the paper reports use of Shapiro-Wilk, Levene, Mauchly, Greenhouse-Geisser, Friedman, and Bonferroni, but the small \(n=12\) makes variance estimates fragile [PAGE 5].
5. The crossover design breaks causally if washout is insufficient or if training status changes across the 4-week collection window. The source specifies at least 1 week washout and 4 weeks total [PAGE 3], which reduces but does not eliminate carryover and maturation threats.

The scientific lineage runs from peripheral fatigue to central-resource competition to joint-specific injury-risk mechanics. The paper’s introduction states that PMF “involves not only diminished function of the peripheral muscular system but also, more critically, impairs neuromuscular control efficacy through central mechanisms,” because “simultaneous physical and cognitive tasks compete for limited central processing resources” [PAGE 2]. The PMF protocol itself is genealogically tied to Barzegarpoor et al. (2020) and de Lima-Junior et al. (2023), with the Stroop procedure adapted from Wang et al. (2026) [PAGE 3]. The coordination lineage is dynamical-systems based: CRP, MARP, and DP are adapted from Ren et al. (2022b) to quantify hip-knee and knee-ankle coupling [PAGE 4]. The injury lineage is local-ankle versus global-kinetic: increased inversion is linked to ankle-sprain risk, and reduced dorsiflexion to anterior translational force and Achilles tendon loading [PAGE 10]. The paper’s novelty is not inventing a new fatigue theory; it is placing a dual-task PMF protocol inside a landing-biomechanics and inter-joint-coordination design.

The retrocausal trap is to read the later “adaptive compensation” conclusion back into the data as if compensation were directly observed. The source itself warns that “discussions regarding compensatory strategies remain somewhat speculative without direct physiological evidence” [PAGE 11]. A second retrocausal trap is to treat the null global kinetics as proof that PMF is benign. The paper explicitly rejects that inference: “Even if global kinetic indicators remain unchanged, the redistribution of local joint loads could potentially elevate injury risk” [PAGE 10]. A third trap is to treat the null MARP/DP findings as proof that coordination is unaffected. The authors argue that inter-joint coordination indices “might be less sensitive to fatigue than single-joint kinematic indicators” and may require longer or higher-intensity interventions to reveal changes [PAGE 9]. Thus the historical lineage supports a layered interpretation: central interference may be real, but the current assay may be too coarse or too compensated to detect it globally.

The multi-week traps are embedded in the design. The washout is “at least 1 week” and data collection is compressed into 4 weeks [PAGE 3]. The Baseline is a separate session, not a same-visit pre-fatigue control, which the authors concede “does not account for potential day-to-day biological variability” [PAGE 11]. Participants abstained from strenuous exercise and caffeine for 24 h, alcohol for at least 1 week, and testing time was held within a 2 h window to control circadian variation [PAGE 2; PAGE 3]. Those controls help, but they also mean the observed PMF effect is a narrow acute laboratory effect, not a season-long or in-game cumulative-fatigue effect. The single-team, all-male, \(n=12\) sample further restricts retrocausal generalization: the paper states the sample was “all high-level male collegiate American football players from a single championship team” and lacked female athletes [PAGE 10]. The historical lesson is that elite adaptation can preserve global performance while hiding local risk; the multi-week lesson is that a 4-week crossover with separate baseline cannot resolve cumulative exposure or day-to-day variance.

The crisis-habit-regression interpretation is that highly trained landing is a strongly automatized skill. The paper argues that the landing task “is common in American football competition and training” and that ample practice may make execution rely “more on basic control mechanisms at the spinal and subcortical levels rather than on movement adjustments requiring high-level cognitive involvement” [PAGE 9]. The paper reports “potential injury risk,” not injury incidence, and its discussion of compensation is speculative without direct physiological evidence [PAGE 1; PAGE 11]. The recursive theory-of-mind point is that the athlete may not consciously perceive the ankle inversion, the coach may see only the stable global landing, and the researcher may infer a compensatory strategy that was never measured. Each agent has a different observable: the athlete has proprioceptive and effort cues; the coach has gross movement; the researcher has marker/force-plate traces. The model should not collapse those perspectives into one.

The cognitive stimulus clock is 1,000 ms display plus 1,000 ms blank, with a 1,500 ms response window [PAGE 3]. The landing clock is three successful trials with 30 s rest [PAGE 4]. The washout clock is at least 1 week, and the total study clock is 4 weeks [PAGE 3]. The circadian clock is controlled within 2 h, and the pre-test abstinence clock is 24 h for exercise/caffeine and 1 week for alcohol [PAGE 2; PAGE 3]. These clocks define the claim’s scope: this is an acute, laboratory, post-fatigue effect, not a chronic exposure model. A recursive decision rule follows: trust the local ankle signal more than the global stability signal for risk screening, but do not upgrade that local signal into an injury claim without a longitudinal clock and direct neuromuscular measures.

Scenario 1: Same-session pre/post PMF crossover. Counterfactual: instead of comparing post-fatigue to a separate Baseline session, measure the same athletes before and immediately after the identical PMF protocol within one visit, then repeat the landing task. Verifiable prediction: post-PMF should show smaller ankle dorsiflexion at peak vGRF and larger ankle inversion at peak vGRF than pre-PMF, using the same repeated-measures analysis and Bonferroni threshold. If the directional effect is absent or reversed, the causal claim that PMF specifically alters ankle kinematics is vetoed. This scenario directly addresses the paper’s admitted weakness that the separate-baseline design “does not account for potential day-to-day biological variability” [PAGE 11]. It also tests whether the observed Baseline-vs-PMF differences survive same-day control.

Scenario 2: Direct neuromuscular mechanism test. Counterfactual: add surface EMG of tibialis anterior and peroneus longus preactivation and co-contraction, plus EEG-EMG corticomuscular coherence, before and after PMF. Verifiable prediction: if PMF acts through central interference and reduced ankle protection, post-PMF should show reduced dorsiflexor/evertor preactivation or increased antagonist co-contraction, with a central coherence change. If kinematics change but EMG/EEG show no relevant change, the central-interference mechanism is vetoed and the effect must be reclassified as peripheral, mechanical, or marker/model artifact. If EMG fully explains the kinematic shift without any central signature, the paper’s “central mechanisms” language is downgraded. This is verifiable because the source itself calls for “electromyographic (EMG) coherence analysis” and “corticomuscular functional coupling” [PAGE 10].

Scenario 3: Higher-ecological-load landing test. Counterfactual: repeat PMF with a more complex landing—side-step, cutting, or a longer dual-task protocol—and pre-register global kinetics and CRP indices as outcomes. Verifiable prediction: if the paper’s “compensatory preservation” is task-specific, the higher-load task should produce a significant increase in peak vGRF, LR, or Kleg and/or a significant change in MARP/DP. If global kinetics rise while ankle-local differences disappear, the local-specific claim is vetoed. If local ankle differences persist without global changes even under higher load, the compensation account gains support. This scenario tests the paper’s own “ceiling effect” concern that inter-joint coordination indices may require “longer duration or higher intensity interventions” to show change [PAGE 9]. Each scenario has a clear pass/fail prediction and a clear veto condition, so it is verifiable rather than narrative.
