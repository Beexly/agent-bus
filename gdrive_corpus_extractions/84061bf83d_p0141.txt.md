The passage’s “need reduction” language echoes Hull’s postulate that reinforcement is contingent upon drive reduction, while the reference to “conditioned-reflex learning” in the summary nods to Pavlovian antecedents. The historical arc continues through Spence’s Hullian gradient theory, Amsel’s frustration framework (where nonreward generates competing tendencies analogous to s_comp), and Crespi’s incentive-contrast experiments, which demonstrated that abrupt shifts in reward magnitude produce performance disruptions not predicted by simple drive strength alone. Later developments in incentive salience (Berridge) and opponent-process theory (Solomon & Corbit) split Hull’s unitary “reinforcement” into liking/wanting and affective opponent processes, thereby complicating the assumption that “more food” linearly equals “more habit strength.”

Multi-week traps embedded in this genealogy: extinction over repeated sessions does not erase habits but suppresses them, permitting spontaneous recovery after delays; renewal effects return responding when the context returns to the acquisition environment; and partial-reinforcement extinction effects (PREE) make habits trained under intermittent reward more resistant to extinction than continuously reinforced ones. Hull’s extrapolation that zero incentive still yields more than half of maximal work is vulnerable to these multi-week traps: a chimpanzee tested weeks after satiation may show renewal of rope-pulling in the original cage despite zero banana, precisely because contextual stimuli C retain habit strength independent of the consummatory component. Conversely, successive negative contrast—shifting from large to small banana pieces—can suppress performance below the level predicted by the small-piece asymptote, violating the simple positive growth function ∆s_HR = 𝒢(I). These traps warn that the source’s single-session, single-subject linear window [0.64, 3.77] iu is not a durable law of behavior across weeks.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Model the chimpanzee’s recursive contingency representation. Level 0: “Pull rope → weighted car enters cage → banana obtained.” Level 1: “Experimenter controls banana size; large banana predicts high work worth, small banana predicts low.” Level 2 (crisis): under normal hunger drive D, the expected value of pulling is high; under incentive absence I = 0, the subject faces a prediction error between the learned contingency (rope → banana) and the current outcome (rope → no banana).

Crisis habit regression occurs when drive intensifies: the organism regresses to the strongest previously reinforced habit. Here, rope-pulling is that habit. In the absence of food, residual contextual conditioning C can briefly sustain pulling, consistent with Hull’s extrapolation W(0) > ½W(3.77). This is not rational economic choice; it is habit regression under drive pressure, where the stimulus compound evokes the act more persistently than competing tendencies—until depletion of the incentive component tips s_eff below θ_R.

The counter-trap is Hull’s own extrapolation: it assumes that because the local line W(I) = αI + β has a large intercept β, removing the incentive leaves >half the maximal work intact. This overestimates residual motivation because it ignores the Chapter XV depletion of the incentive stimulus component and ignores s_comp. The counter-trap predicts that early extinction trials will show high responding (confirming β) but that the decline will be steeper than a naive linear-intercept model suggests, because s_eff is actively depleted by nonreinforcement.

The clock is the trial counter n in the extinction equation ds_HR/dn = −λ. Each unreinforced pull advances the clock toward n_ext, the singularity where s_HR crosses θ_R and the response terminates. The recursive loop closes as follows: the subject’s expectation of banana modulates pull vigor; the absence of banana generates prediction error; prediction error depletes s_eff; depleted s_eff reduces vigor; reduced vigor produces fewer pulls; fewer pulls accelerate the extinction clock. The experimenter’s manipulation of I is therefore both a motivational variable and a clock-setting variable: larger I slows extinction by increasing reinforcement rate; I = 0 starts the clock at its fastest rate.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: The apparatus imposes a hard ceiling. Weighted car mass M_car, rope-pulley friction coefficient μ, incline angle φ, and pull distance d define the mechanical work requirement W_app = (M_car g sinφ + μM_car g cosφ) d. If W_motivational(I,D) exceeds W_app,max—the maximum work the car-and-rope system can transmit before slip or structural failure—the observed output is capped by physics, not motivation.

Biomechanical layer: Chimpanzee grip force F_grip,max, arm power P_max, and fatigue time constant τ_fat bound performance. Observed work over a session is W_bio = ∫₀^T P(t) dt with P(t) ≤ P_max e^(−t/τ_fat). Even under maximal hunger drive and large banana incentive, W cannot exceed W_bio,max.

Weather/ambient layer: Though the source is a laboratory preparation, ambient temperature and humidity act as vetoes on drive D and muscle contractility. Heat suppresses hunger drive (reducing D below “normal”) and degrades sustained power output; cold increases metabolic demand and may alter need reduction per unit banana consumed.

Adjudication rule:

W_obs = min{ W_mot(I, D), W_bio,max(D, T_amb), W_app,max(μ, φ, M_car) }.

If increasing I from 0.64 to 3.77 iu produces no increase in W_obs, the veto is physical or biomechanical (apparatus ceiling or muscular saturation), not a failure of the incentive law. Singularity occurs when required rope tension exceeds static friction plus the chimpanzee’s maximum anchorage force; then W_obs = 0 regardless of D or I. This veto structure protects against attributing flat performance curves to “lack of drive” when the car is simply too heavy to move.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with falsification conditions and predictions.

Scenario A — Sub-threshold incentive sweep.
Veto condition: Hold D normal and measure W across I ∈ [0, 0.64] iu. If W remains on the same affine line W = αI + β extrapolated from [0.64, 3.77] all the way to I = 0 with unchanged α and β, then the claim of local linearity plus contextual depletion is falsified; the breakdown regime S4 begins at I = 0 rather than below 0.64.
Prediction: W(I) deviates below the linear extrapolation near I ≈ 0.64 iu; response probability drops sigmoidally toward zero as I approaches a positive threshold I_thr > 0, consistent with s_eff < θ_R at sufficiently low incentive.

Scenario B — Extinction probe at zero incentive.
Veto condition: After asymptotic training at I = 3.77 iu, switch to I = 0 and record the first unreinforced trial and subsequent extinction curve. If initial W(0) is not ≥ ½W(3.77), or if responding does not terminate within a finite n_ext consistent with s_HR crossing θ_R, then Hull’s extrapolation and Chapter XV depletion claim are weakened.
Prediction: Initial responding at I = 0 satisfies W(0) ≳ ½W(3.77) due to contextual habit β; W(n) = W(0) exp(−n/τ) with τ determined by λ; termination occurs at n_ext where s_HR(n_ext) < θ_R. Verifiable by counting trials to cessation.

Scenario C — Satiety drive manipulation.
Veto condition: Pre-feed the chimpanzee so D ≈ 0 while presenting I = 3.77 iu. If work output and ∆s_HR match the hungry-animal baseline, then the drive-dependence axiom and need-reduction mediation are falsified.
Prediction: W ≈ 0 and ∆s_HR ≈ 0 despite large incentive; any residual pulling reflects only s_comp > θ_R (non-food motivation) and should extinguish rapidly because ℛ ∝ ΔE = 0. Verifiable by comparing pre-fed vs. food-depressed work curves and habit-strength increments.
