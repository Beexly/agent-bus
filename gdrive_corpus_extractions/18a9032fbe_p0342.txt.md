- Ogive operator: p = G(sER), where G(x) = Φ((x − μp)/σp), Φ = standard normal CDF, μp [potential units], σp > 0 [potential units]. p is a dimensionless probability measure on [0,1]. The excerpt does not specify normal versus logistic, so μp and σp are not empirically fixed by the text.
- Composite: p(N) = Φ(( sE∞(1 − e^(−λN)) − sL − μp ) / σp ).

Singularity and breakdown conditions:
- N = 0: sE = 0, so sER = −sL. If sL > 0 then p is at the lower asymptote (≈0 for symmetric ogive centered near zero).
- Threshold crossing: sE(N) = sL + μp implies sER = μp and p = 0.5 for a symmetric ogive; this is the inflection region of the sigmoid.
- Upper asymptote: p(∞) = Φ((sE∞ − sL − μp)/σp). If sE∞ ≤ sL + μp (approximately), p never rises materially above the lower asymptote; the sigmoid breaks down into a null learning curve despite unlimited N.
- Identifiability singularity: different (sE∞, λ, sL) tuples yield identical p(N), so the excerpt's two functions do not uniquely determine the parameters without independent threshold or asymptote data.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage: Hull's 1943 Principles of Behavior is deductive neobehaviorism. The reinforcement count N descends from Thorndike's law of effect and law of exercise, where repeated reinforcement stamps in a connection. The excitatory potential sE grows with N in a Pavlovian-Tolmanian tradition of expectation formation. The reaction threshold sL draws on Sherringtonian reflex physiology and psychophysics: a response is evoked only when effective potential exceeds a limit threshold. The ogival function descends from the normal-ogive tradition (Thurstone) and from cumulative-normal assumptions in sensory discrimination, here repurposed to map internal potential onto the probability of an observable response. Figure 72's sigmoid superficially resembles Ebbinghaus learning curves, but Hull derives the S-shape from postulates rather than fitting it descriptively.

Later lineage projects forward into Hull-Spence gradient theory, signal-detection theory (where the criterion plays the role of sL and sensitivity maps to the slope of the evocation function), and drift-diffusion models (where a boundary threshold governs response initiation). Retrocausal trap: modern reinforcement-learning readers may project temporal-difference value updates onto sE(N), but Hull's N is a cumulative reinforcement counter with no forgetting, no prediction-error term, and no explicit time variable; reading it as a value function is anachronistic.

Multi-week traps: (1) Stationarity trap—sL is treated as fixed across weeks, but fatigue, circadian drift, and satiation make the threshold time-varying sL(t), so a curve fit in week one mispredicts week four. (2) N-versus-clock trap—two subjects with identical N but different inter-reinforcement intervals receive identical predictions, violating spacing effects and making real-time scheduling a latent confound. (3) Identifiability trap—the mediating construct sEz (caption) or sER (body) is unobserved, so infinite parameterizations fit the same asymptotic p(N), and the chapter cannot pin down sER from the learning curve alone. (4) Asymptotic trap—fixed sE∞ implies a hard ceiling; spontaneous recovery or renewed gains after a contingency change cannot be generated by the static two-function composition without adding parameters.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: Within Hull's habit-family hierarchy, the dominant response has the highest sE, but if a crisis (fear, pain, fatigue, or deprivation) raises sL above sER for that response, activation regresses to lower-strength competing habits. The target response probability p collapses even though total responding may continue, so the smooth monotonic composition sE(N) → p is violated by state-dependent threshold elevation.

Counter-trap: A modeler observing a late-training performance drop may infer extinction (decline in sE) when the actual cause is a raised sL or increased response cost. The counter-trap is misattributing central habit decay to peripheral threshold gating; the observed p can fall while sE continues to grow, because sER = sE − sL has crossed below the evocation zone.

Clock: The x-axis operator is N (reinforcement count), not elapsed time. Treating Figure 72's axis as sessions or clock time is a category error: massed and spaced contingencies with equal N are predicted to yield identical p, yet real-time intervals affect fatigue, sL(t), and consolidation. Recursive theory of mind enters when the subject models the experimenter's contingency: if the subject expects reinforcement to be withheld, expectancy effectively raises sL, producing a self-fulfilling drop in p before any actual change in N occurs. Over weeks, the subject's evolving model of the experimenter makes sL a moving target, so the original p(N) curve becomes a drifting forecast; crisis-habit regressions appear as sudden discontinuities that the static sigmoid cannot anticipate.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Formal layer-gated observation: p_obs(N) = p_Hull(sER(N)) · V_phys · V_bio · V_weather, where each gate V ∈ [0,1] is dimensionless. Hard veto: if any gate equals 0, then p_obs = 0 regardless of how large p_Hull is.

Physical veto: The organism must possess an intact effector pathway to produce the observable consequent event that anchors sER. Even if sER ≫ sL so that p_Hull ≈ 1, a severed output pathway or lesioned motor cortex sets V_phys = 0, yielding no response. Hull's anchoring claim therefore fails at the measurement layer whenever the physical layer is compromised.

Biomechanical veto: Evoked response requires force, speed, and topography within musculoskeletal limits. High sER cannot evoke a lever press faster than actuator bandwidth or with force below the response requirement; V_bio depends on contraction velocity, available metabolic energy, and response topography. If required response speed exceeds maximum shortening velocity, V_bio = 0 and p_obs = 0 despite central potential far above threshold.

Weather veto: Ambient temperature and humidity alter nerve conduction velocity and muscle viscosity. Cold effectively raises sL and lowers the growth rate λ; V_weather shifts the ogive rightward and flattens its slope. Under extreme conditions, sE(N) − sL remains negative for many reinforcements, so p_Hull stays near zero even as N accumulates.

Veto proof: The central two-function composition is sufficient only when all gates equal 1. Breakdown regime: p_Hull predicts p > 0.5 while p_obs = 0 because V_bio = 0 (effector immobilized) or V_phys = 0 (pathway lesioned). This is not an ogive failure but a layer-omission failure; any model that equates p directly with sER without these gates is falsified by a single high-potential, zero-observation trial.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario A — Deprivation-state threshold veto. Counterfactual: two groups equated on cumulative N and therefore on sE(N) history, but Group 1 is satiated (high sL) and Group 2 is deprived (low sL). The fixed-sL Hull composition predicts identical p(N). Verifiable veto: if the satiated group shows a right-shifted evocation ogive with p ≈ 0 at low N while the deprived group already shows p > 0.5 at the same N, then threshold is not fixed and the fixed-threshold composition is falsified. Stronger prediction: if sL > sE(N) for every trained N, the satiated group exhibits p = 0 (hard veto) while central sE continues to grow, proving threshold gating independent of reinforcement history. Measurement: estimate sL from the ogive midpoint in each deprivation state.

Scenario B — Biomechanical immobilization veto. Counterfactual: continue reinforcement so sE(N) remains high and sER ≫ sL, then transiently block the peripheral effector (e.g., local anesthetic of the response musculature) without changing N. Prediction: p_Hull remains near its upper asymptote, but p_obs = 0 because V_bio = 0; EMG or central readiness proxies show motor command is present while no movement occurs. Upon block removal, p recovers to the Hullian prediction without additional reinforcements. Verifiable by simultaneous central-potential proxy and response recording; vetoes any account that identifies p directly with sER absent a peripheral gate.

Scenario C — Massed versus spaced reinforcement-time veto. Counterfactual: equate total N across conditions but manipulate inter-reinforcement interval (massed short ITI vs spaced long ITI). Because N is the sole input to sE(N), the model predicts identical p(N) curves. Verifiable veto: if spaced training yields higher p at the same N, or a different ogive slope/midpoint, then real-time decay of sE or threshold drift sL(t) between trials is omitted. Prediction: massed condition shows suppressed p at early N due to fatigue-induced sL(t) elevation; spaced condition matches the Figure 72 sigmoid more closely. Verifiable by holding N constant, varying session spacing, and fitting the composite equation separately; a significant spacing × N interaction falsifies the pure N-count operator.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
