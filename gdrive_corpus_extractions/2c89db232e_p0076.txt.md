The debate over universal versus domain-specific motivational drivers has a deep historical lineage stretching from 19th-century energetics to modern predictive processing. Early physiologists like Cannon (homeostatic theory) and later Hebb proposed that "drive" was a unified, physiological scalar—essentially an internal tension state reducing to a single variable, $D$. Hull’s radical attempt at a unitary equation for action intensity ($sEr = V_D \cdot D \cdot K$) was the culmination of behaviorist mathematical formalization. However, this unitary drive hypothesis has been historically falsified in stages. First, by the discovery of "appetitive" behaviors not strictly tied to homeostatic deficits (e.g., play, exploration). Second, by neuropsychological double dissociations: lesions affecting specific drives while sparing others (e.g., lateral hypothalamus vs. ventromedial). Third, by cognitive psychology demonstrating that beliefs about deprivation can substitute for actual deprivation, breaking the direct biological link assumed by Hull's $D$.

Hull’s caution on page 67—that motivation might be "so heterogeneous that no single equation can represent the motivational potentiality of any two needs"—marks a rare moment of theoretical epistemic humility in a rigidly deductive system. This hesitation is genealogically linked to earlier critiques by Köhler and Wolfgang Köhler’s Gestalt psychologists, who argued that behavior emerges from holistic perceptual fields, not summed local excitatory states. It also anticipates the later modular/adaptive landscape view of evolutionary psychology (Tooby & Cosmides), which treats motivations as distinct evolved algorithms triggered by specific ecological cues rather than generalized arousal. The trap here is "motivational monism"—the belief that all goal-directed behavior reduces to a common currency (whether calories, dopamine, or information entropy). Modern affective neuroscience rejects this: hunger circuits (ghrelin/leptin pathways) are anatomically and neurochemically distinct from fear circuits (amygdala/CRF) or social bonding circuits (oxytocin/vasopressin). Recognizing this heterogeneity allows us to construct multi-dimensional motivational manifolds, where different drives operate under different scaling laws, noise profiles, and temporal discounting functions. A multi-week analysis trap occurs when one assumes that because two drives manifest similar surface behaviors (e.g., high locomotor activity), they share a common underlying computational structure; Hull explicitly warns against this conflation, noting that habit strength interacts differently with each drive type.
