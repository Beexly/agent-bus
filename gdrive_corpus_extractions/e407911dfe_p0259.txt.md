When the clock is short, the offense's model of the defense collapses toward "defense will pursue the feint," while the defense's model of the offense collapses toward "offense will abandon hesitation and run the obvious plunge."

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof.

The play on [PAGE 245] is subject to a hierarchy of vetoes across physical layers. At the safety layer, any condition that risks cervical or musculoskeletal injury vetoes execution regardless of tactical advantage. At the physical layer, weather and field conditions impose hard constraints. Rain or snow reduces the coefficient of friction \(\mu\) between cleats and turf. The right tackle's drive block requires \(\mu \ge \mu_{crit}\) to generate sufficient horizontal impulse \(\mathbf{J}_{rt}\) without slipping. If \(\mu < \mu_{crit}\), the right tackle cannot "make a hole for the runner," and the full back's plunge path is obstructed. Wet ball conditions similarly degrade grip, raising the probability that \(R_{rhb}=0\) or \(R_{fb}=0\); the lateral pass becomes a fumble risk.

At the biomechanical layer, the full back's hesitation and plunge require intact hip and knee extension. If the full back is fatigued or injured such that his time-to-peak-force \(t_{ptf}\) exceeds the defensive arrival time \(\tau_{tackle}\), the plunge is vetoed by physiology. The quarterback's feint requires lateral agility; a slow or injured quarterback fails to hold the defensive left end, collapsing \(P_{decoy}\). At the environmental layer, wind affects the lateral pass \(\mathbf{p}_{rhb \to fb}\), especially in an era without modern aerodynamic ball design; strong crosswinds can cause the pass to sail outside the catchable radius.

Adjudication protocol: vetoes are evaluated in order—safety first, then physical feasibility (weather/field), then biomechanical readiness, then tactical advantage. If any layer returns a veto, the play is not called. The center's cautionary note ("Center must be careful in making pass to right half back" [PAGE 245]) is itself an acknowledgment of the snap-pass layer's vulnerability: a bad snap vetoes the entire double pass before any blocking assignment matters.
