The paper maps image-plane pixel positions to a ground-plane world coordinate system through a homography, with field corners assigned metric coordinates and the origin at the bottom-left corner [PAGE 4]. The first gray-zone is therefore geometric: the homography is valid only for points on the ground plane. The paper itself warns that jumping players produce “wrong homographic transformation estimates” [PAGE 3]. Thus any player-position estimate is already conditional on foot-point visibility and ground contact. The next hop is background subtraction: a 16-mode Gaussian mixture is learned recursively, with adaptive learning rates for illumination changes and camera motion [PAGE 4-5]. If the model absorbs a stationary player or the net, the foreground mask ceases to be a player detector. The mask is then thresholded and opened to remove spectators and sand noise [PAGE 5]. The mask contributes a cue weight by counting non-zero pixels in particle subwindows [PAGE 9]. If the mask is empty over the true player, that cue goes to zero; if all cues go to zero, the normalized fusion in Eq. 1.5 has a denominator singularity. This is not a hypothetical edge case: the paper repeatedly identifies occlusion, sand texture, shadow, and net contamination as dominant conditions [PAGE 2-5].

The color path adds another conditional chain. HSV histograms are built from foreground-masked calibration crops, then compared by Bhattacharyya distance [PAGE 8-9]. The distance is dimensionless and bounded in principle, but it becomes non-discriminative when both players on a team wear nearly identical colors. The paper explicitly allows particle-set switching between same-team players during evaluation [PAGE 13-14], so the reported player accuracy is a position accuracy, not a strict identity accuracy. This is a critical gray-zone audit point: a tracker can be “correct” by proximity while the identity assignment is wrong. The 10-frame safeguard, which resamples the weaker cloud when two sets track the same player for more than 10 frames [PAGE 10], is a temporal counter-trap against this failure, but it is not a proof of identity.

The particle filter adds a resampling hop. Roulette-wheel resampling uses Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.2 m, and top-weight particles are resampled with 0.04 m [PAGE 9]. The state estimate is the weighted average of the cloud. The integral-histogram method replaces the random cloud with a rigid grid, e.g. 6 rows by 7 columns with particles in the lower three rows [PAGE 10]. For the ball, the derivation is candidate-to-trajectory-to-intersection. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source provides a set of operators that map pixels to player states, player states to cue weights, cue weights to particle weights, and ball candidates to contact frames. The equations below preserve the paper’s notation where possible. Units are stated; where the paper mixes pixel counts and metric coordinates, the normalization step is identified. Page anchors are given for each operator.

1. Homography operator. The image plane is mapped to the world ground plane by a linear projective transformation:
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_w \sim H \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_p,\qquad \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_p=(u,v,1)^T,\quad \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_w=(X,Y,1)^T .
\]
The paper states: “The mapping of the image plane into the real world ground plane is done through a linear projective transformation (homography)” [PAGE 4]. World coordinates are in metres; pixel coordinates are in pixels; \(H\) is scale-ambiguous and dimensionless only after a chosen normalization. The paper assigns field corners \((0,0),(0,8),(8,16),(0,16)\) [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regimes: \(\det H=0\); four calibration corners collinear or not distinct; players jumping so the foot point is not on the ground plane; severe perspective extrapolation at the back court. The paper explicitly identifies “wrong homographic transformation estimates” for jumping players [PAGE 3].

2. Background GMM and foreground mask. The background is modeled with 16 Gaussian modes:
\[
p(I_t(\mathbf{p})\mid \mathcal{B})=\sum_{m=1}^{16}\pi_m\,\mathcal{N}\!\left(I_t(\mathbf{p});\mu_m,\Sigma_m\right),
\]
with \(\pi_m\) dimensionless, \(\mu_m\) in intensity units, and \(\Sigma_m\) in intensity-squared units. The paper says “we first calculate a background using 16 Gaussian modes” and adapts the learning rate for “involuntary camera motion or sudden illumination changes” [PAGE 4-5]. The foreground mask is a thresholded difference followed by an opening filter:
\[
M_t(\mathbf{p})=\mathrm{Open}\!\left(\mathbb{1}\!\left[\,|I_t(\mathbf{p})-B_t(\mathbf{p})|>\tau\,\right]\right).
\]
\(\tau\) is in intensity units; \(M_t\) is a binary dimensionless mask. The paper notes that sand texture changes “due to wind or footsteps” and that the opening filter reduces “noise, mainly from the spectators and the sand” [PAGE 5]. Breakdown regimes: background contamination by a stationary player or the net; learning rate too low for illumination change or too high for stable backgrounds; \(\tau\) too high, removing limbs and ball; \(\tau\) too low, admitting sand and spectators. If \(M_t\) is empty over the true object, the mask cue in Eq. 1.4 collapses to zero.

3. Color operator and Bhattacharyya distance. For reference histogram \(h^{ref}\) and hypothesized histogram \(h^{hyp}\), the paper defines
\[
D(h^{ref},h^{hyp})=\sqrt{1-\frac{\sum_{b\in B}\sqrt{h_b^{ref}h_b^{hyp}}}{\sqrt{\sum_{b\in B}h_b^{ref}\cdot \sum_{b\in B}h_b^{hyp}}}} .
\]
This is Eq. 1.1 [PAGE 8]. Histogram bin values are counts or probability masses; \(B\) is the number of bins; \(D\) is dimensionless and, for normalized histograms, lies in the unit interval. The paper uses HSV histograms and states that this histogram gives “a unique ‘fingerprint’ for each subwindow” [PAGE 8]. Breakdown regimes: zero histogram mass in the denominator; identical team colors making \(D\) non-discriminative; illumination shift moving pixels between bins; calibration contamination by the net or background; too many bins making small changes jump bins, or too few bins blurring player identity. The paper found a 110-bin compromise [PAGE 9].

4. Color weight and subwindow coupling. The per-subwindow color weight is
\[
w_{color,k}=c\,e^{-20D_k^2},
\]
with \(c=100\) in the paper’s implementation [PAGE 9]. This is Eq. 1.2. The combined color weight couples the upper subwindows multiplicatively and the lowest subwindow additively:
\[
w_{color}=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{N_S}w_{color,k}\right)+w_{color,k=0}.
\]
This is Eq. 1.3 as described on [PAGE 8-9]: upper subwindows are “tightly” coupled by multiplication, while the lowest subwindow is “loosely coupled” by addition because the legs often spread and the lowest subwindow may not contain leg information. Units are dimensionless. Breakdown regimes: any \(w_{color,k}=0\) collapses the product; the lowest-subwindow additive term can dominate if it is large; the spatial prior is invalid when the player’s legs are wide apart or occluded.

5. Mask and movement weights. The paper uses a shared non-zero-pixel count form:
\[
w_{mov,mask}=\frac{1}{S_{width}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_S} NZP_k .
\]
This is Eq. 1.4 [PAGE 9]. \(NZP_k\) is a dimensionless count of non-zero mask pixels in subwindow \(k\); \(S_{width}\) is a pixel width. The raw term has pixel^{-1} units after division by width, but Eq. 1.5 normalizes by the per-frame maximum, so the effective cue weight is dimensionless. Breakdown regimes: all \(NZP_k=0\); the ball or player is occluded; the movement mask is dominated by spectators or camera motion; the mask is contaminated by sand.

6. Cue fusion. The total weight of the \(j\)-th particle for the \(i\)-th player is
\[
w_j^i=\sum_{cue}\alpha_{cue}\,\frac{w_{j,cue}^i}{\max_j w_{j,cue}^i}.
\]
This is Eq. 1.5 [PAGE 9-10]. The paper gives \(\alpha_{color}=1\), \(\alpha_{mask}=0.4\), \(\alpha_{mov}=0.1\) [PAGE 10]. All \(\alpha\) and normalized cue weights are dimensionless. Breakdown regime: if \(\max_j w_{j,cue}^i=0\) for a cue, the ratio is a \(0/0\) singularity; if all cues are zero, the particle set has no information and resampling becomes effectively uniform. The color cue dominates by design, so color failure propagates directly into identity failure.

7. Particle-filter resampling. The state is in metres. The paper uses Gaussian resampling noise with standard deviation \(0.2\) m and, for particles in the upper \(0.1\%\) of the maximum set weight, \(0.04\) m [PAGE 9]. A schematic transition is
\[
\mathbf{x}_t^{(j)}\sim \sum_{i}\tilde{w}_{t-1}^{(i)}\,\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^{(i)},\sigma^2 I\right),
\]
with \(\sigma=0.2\) m or \(0.04\) m. The paper’s safeguard is: “if two clouds track the same player for more than 10 frames, the cloud with the smaller average state color weight will be resampled uniformly in the field” [PAGE 10]. Breakdown regimes: \(\sigma\) too small causes sample impoverishment; \(\sigma\) too large causes cloud splitting and player confusion; more particles can increase split probability, which the paper observes as lower accuracy [PAGE 15].

8. Integral-histogram grid. The paper uses a rigid grid, e.g. 6 rows by 7 columns with particles in the lower three rows [PAGE 10]. The grid is scaled by the homography to account for perspective. Particles are additionally weighted by grid position: “the particles in the center columns are higher weighted than the ones in the outer columns by a quadratic factor” [PAGE 10]. If \(g_j\) denotes that dimensionless positional weight, the effective weight is \(g_j w_j^i\). The exact quadratic coefficient is not printed in the source. Breakdown regimes: wrong grid area due to homography error; too many horizontal columns biasing the predicted state away from the between-the-legs reference; near-player confusion when two players share colors.

9. Ball candidate operator. The ball has 21 cm diameter [PAGE 11]. Candidates are searched in movement-difference blobs. The inner and outer squares are 40x40 and 60x60 pixels [PAGE 11]. The acceptance rule is
\[
N_{inner}>Q,\qquad N_{outer}\le Q+R,
\]
with \(R\) about 5 pixels [PAGE 11]. \(N\) values are dimensionless pixel counts. Breakdown regimes: the ball merges with a limb, net, or another blob, so \(N_{outer}\) exceeds \(Q+R\); motion blur or spin destroys the isolated-blob signature; the ball is occluded; the homographic scale is wrong for the ball’s image size.

10. Ball trajectory equations. The paper uses
\[
y(n)=a_2n^2+a_1n+a_0,\qquad x(n)=mn+b,
\]
where \(n\) is frame index, \(x,y\) are image coordinates in pixels, \(a_2\) has units pixels/frame², \(m\) has units pixels/frame, and \(a_1,a_0,b\) have corresponding pixel units [PAGE 11]. The paper states that horizontal motion follows straight paths and vertical motion follows parabolic paths [PAGE 11]. A trajectory initializes when at least three candidates less than 20 pixels apart form a straight line [PAGE 11]. A trajectory terminates after more than three missing frames [PAGE 11]. Breakdown regimes: occlusion longer than three frames; non-parabolic image motion due to spin, drag, or perspective; false candidates forming a spurious straight line; parallel trajectories causing wrong selection; interpolation extending a trajectory into a fabricated intersection.

11. Contact-frame operator. Contact frames are taken from the y-intersections of parabolic trajectories, because the paper finds that y-intersections are more accurate than x-intersections [PAGE 12]. The first serve contact is taken as the initial frame of the first trajectory [PAGE 12]. A contact is scored correct if the frame number differs by less than 10 frames from the manual reference [PAGE 15]. At 25 frames/second, 10 frames is 0.4 s. Breakdown regime: if trajectories are missing or terminated early, no intersection exists; if trajectories are extended, intersections may be fabricated; the initial-frame convention can absorb or create error for the serve.

12. Evaluation operators. For players, a frame is correct if the estimated position differs from the reference by less than 0.5 m or less than 20 pixels [PAGE 13]. For the ball, correctness is Euclidean distance less than 20 pixels [PAGE 14]. For contacts, correctness is frame difference less than 10 frames [PAGE 15]. These tolerances are thresholds, not continuous scores. The paper also allows switching of particle sets between players of the same team [PAGE 13-14], so the player metric is position accuracy under identity-permutation tolerance. The reported averages are 74.6% for particle-filter front players, 58.8% for particle-filter back players, 82.6% for integral-histogram front players, and 61.6% for integral-histogram back players [PAGE 14]. Ball tracking is 54.2% for the Chen-based method and 42.1% for Hough; contacts are 48.9% [PAGE 15].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage runs through Bayesian tracking, adaptive background modeling, color-based probabilistic tracking, integral histograms, and physics-based ball tracking. The player tracker inherits from Kalman and particle filtering [PAGE 2], with the particle filter described by Gordon et al. and the resampling scheme attributed to roulette-wheel selection [PAGE 9]. The color cue inherits from Pérez et al.’s color-based probabilistic tracking and from Mauthner et al.’s beach-volleyball integral-histogram work [PAGE 3, PAGE 8]. The background model inherits from Zivkovic and van der Heijden’s adaptive Gaussian mixture method [PAGE 4]. The integral-histogram acceleration inherits from Porikli [PAGE 10]. The ball tracker inherits from Chen et al.’s physics-based volleyball tracking [PAGE 11]. The paper thus sits in a retrocausal chain: later replication retroactively audits an earlier “over 90%” claim. The abstract states that “Tracking results of over 90% from the literature could not be confirmed” [PAGE 1], and the discussion states that “we could only track little more than half the balls in the game, compared to over 90% as presented in their work” [PAGE 17]. The genealogy is therefore not a simple accumulation of accuracy; it is a correction of transferability.

Several multi-week traps follow from that genealogy. The first is literature-accuracy transfer: treating a favorable-scene result as a general beach-volleyball result. The second is parameter folklore: assuming more particles or more histogram bins must improve tracking. The paper finds the opposite for particles and only a weak tendency for 10 bins to be better than 5 or 15 [PAGE 15-16]. The third is calibration leakage: using raw-image reference histograms that include the net, which the paper warns would make the algorithm expect the net at the same height or subwindow for a back player [PAGE 16-17]. The fourth is camera-angle inertia: defaulting to a back-of-field camera even though the paper argues that a side placement could “drastically increase the tracking results” by reducing mutual occlusion and net occlusion [PAGE 17]. The fifth is task conflation: treating ball-tracking percentage and contact-frame percentage as the same quantity, although they use different denominators and different tolerances [PAGE 15]. The sixth is identity laundering: allowing same-team particle-set swaps in evaluation [PAGE 13-14], which can make position accuracy look better than identity accuracy. These traps are multi-week because each one can survive a short test on favorable clips and only appear under occlusion, illumination change, back-court perspective, or same-color teams.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Operationally, the recursive theory of mind here has three nested models. The tracker models the player: fast direction changes, jumping, bent running, wide legs, and mutual occlusion. The paper encodes that model by rejecting velocity-dependent resampling as disadvantageous for abrupt movements [PAGE 3], by down-weighting the lowest subwindow because the legs may spread [PAGE 8], and by adding a 10-frame anti-confusion safeguard [PAGE 10]. The analyst models the tracker: if accuracy is poor, the analyst may assume that more particles, more bins, or a more complex filter must help. That is the crisis habit regression. The paper’s evidence contradicts it: “a greater number of particles does not increase the tracking accuracy as might be expected, but on the contrary decreases the tracking accuracy” [PAGE 15]. The integral-histogram method also performs better with smaller particle numbers, and higher horizontal density can bias the state away from the between-the-legs reference [PAGE 16]. The benchmark models the analyst: the evaluation allows same-team swaps [PAGE 13-14], so the benchmark can reward positional proximity while hiding identity error. That recursive loop can inflate trust in a tracker that is not actually maintaining player identity.

The counter-trap is to replace the crisis habit with structural checks. First, use foreground-masked calibration so the net and background do not enter the reference histogram [PAGE 16-17]. Second, prefer the rigid-grid integral histogram over the classical particle filter when the scene has mutual occlusion and same-color players; the paper reports a clear superiority, with front-player gains of about 6% and back-player gains of about 3% [PAGE 16]. Third, use the proposed confidence measures: distance of a predicted state from the other three states, and cloud spread of a particle set [PAGE 18]. Fourth, change the capture geometry when possible; the paper argues that a side camera would reduce occlusion and widen the usable perspective [PAGE 17].

The clock layer is explicit in the paper. The player tracker uses a 10-frame same-player-confusion threshold before resampling the weaker cloud [PAGE 10]. The ball tracker terminates a trajectory after more than three missing frames [PAGE 11]. The contact evaluation tolerates a 10-frame difference, which is 0.4 s at 25 frames/second [PAGE 15]. The ball is hard to resolve at 25 frames/second because spin and velocity blur the color and shape [PAGE 11]. These clocks are not cosmetic: they define the temporal resolution at which the system can distinguish a real contact from an interpolated intersection. A recursive counter-trap must therefore align the analyst’s trust horizon with the tracker’s actual clock, not with the abstract’s single accuracy number.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is a condition under which no downstream weighting or smoothing can recover a correct state because the upstream observation is absent, contaminated, or geometrically invalid. The paper supplies at least six such veto layers. The first is geometric-optical. The homography assumes a ground-plane point, but jumping produces “wrong homographic transformation estimates” [PAGE 3], and the back court has lower resolution under perspective [PAGE 4]. If the foot point is occluded or off-plane, the world-coordinate state is vetoed regardless of particle count. The second is background-weather. Sand texture changes “due to wind or footsteps” [PAGE 5], and sunny and shadowy regions change illumination. If the GMM learning rate is misadapted, the foreground mask either absorbs the player or admits sand and spectators. The third is color-photometric. Same-team uniforms make the Bhattacharyya distance non-discriminative, and sand or skin-like colors can contaminate the foreground. The paper’s own warning about the net entering a back player’s reference histogram is a calibration veto: if the net is in the reference histogram, the spatial subwindow prior expects the net at the same height [PAGE 16-17]. The fourth is biomechanical. Players are “mostly bent, jumping or running” [PAGE 16], and the lower extremities move widely enough that the lowest subwindow may not contain the legs [PAGE 8]. A color histogram built on a between-the-legs reference cannot remain valid through every limb configuration. The fifth is ball-physical. The ball is 21 cm in diameter and is recorded at 25 frames/second; spin and velocity blur its color and roundness [PAGE 11]. If the ball merges with a limb or the net, the outer-square isolation test rejects it [PAGE 11]. If it is missing for more than three frames, the trajectory terminates [PAGE 11], so no intersection can be recovered. The sixth is trajectory-temporal. Contact frames are y-intersections of parabolas [PAGE 12]; if trajectories are extended by interpolation, an intersection can be fabricated. The paper’s 48.9% contact result [PAGE 15] is therefore a cross-layer product, not a standalone detector score.

The veto proof is conditional but strong. If the foreground mask over the true object is empty, Eq. 1.4 gives zero for mask and movement. If the color histogram denominator is zero or the color cue is non-discriminative, Eq. 1.5 can be dominated by noise or become singular when the per-cue maximum is zero. If the ball candidate is rejected by the outer-square count, no trajectory is initialized. If the trajectory has more than three missing frames, no valid intersection remains. These are not tuning failures alone; they are observation-availability failures. The paper’s empirical pattern supports the veto: front players are tracked at 74.6% and 82.6%, while back players are tracked at 58.8% and 61.6% for the two methods [PAGE 14]. The front-back gap is exactly what a geometry-and-occlusion veto predicts. Ball tracking at 54.2% and 42.1% and contact estimation at 48.9% [PAGE 15] likewise show that the physical-video layer caps the achievable result. No claim of “over 90%” can be adjudicated without passing these veto layers.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: side-camera counterfactual. The paper hypothesizes that placing the camera at the side of the field rather than at the back “could drastically increase the tracking results of both the players and the ball” because mutual occlusion and net occlusion would be reduced [PAGE 17]. Verifiable prediction: with the same tracker, same calibration procedure, and same manual-reference protocol, side-camera clips should reduce the front-back accuracy gap, increase ball trajectory recall, and lower contact-frame error relative to back-camera clips. A practical veto threshold is that the side-camera condition must improve the back-player accuracy and ball-contact accuracy beyond the paper’s observed back-camera values of 61.6% integral-histogram back-player accuracy and 48.9% contact accuracy [PAGE 14-15]. If it does not, the camera-placement hypothesis is vetoed for that scene distribution. This is a verifiable reward because the outcome is a frame-level count within the stated tolerances.

Scenario 2: foreground-mask calibration ablation. The paper states that using the foreground mask for reference histograms prevents the net from being included as false color and prevents the algorithm from expecting the net at the same subwindow height [PAGE 16-17]. Verifiable prediction: replacing foreground-masked reference histograms with raw-image reference histograms should increase back-player drift and same-team confusion, especially when the net crosses the player’s bounding box; the foreground-masked condition should produce higher back-player accuracy and fewer identity swaps. The veto condition is simple: if the raw-image condition is statistically indistinguishable from or better than the masked condition on back-court clips, then the claimed calibration-leakage mechanism is not supported. This counterfactual is verifiable by a paired ablation with the same videos and the same particle/grid parameters.

Scenario 3: parameter-and-algorithm counterfactual. The paper reports that increasing particles from 30 to 50 does not improve and can decrease particle-filter accuracy [PAGE 15], that the integral histogram is faster and more accurate than the classical particle filter [PAGE 16], and that the Chen-based ball tracker reaches 54.2% while Hough reaches 42.1% [PAGE 15]. Verifiable prediction: under a fixed evaluation set, \(P=50\) should not outperform \(P=30\) for the particle filter; the integral histogram should retain a runtime advantage, with reported times of 2.41 to 4.42 s per second of video versus 5.67 to 9.17 s for the particle filter [PAGE 16]; and the Chen-based ball method should exceed Hough on the same candidate stream. If \(P=50\) systematically wins, or Hough systematically beats the Chen-based method, or the integral histogram loses its runtime advantage, the corresponding mechanism is vetoed. These predictions are measurable as percent frames within tolerance, trajectory recall, contact error within 10 frames, and normalized runtime.
