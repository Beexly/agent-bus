<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source contains no physical, biomechanical, or weather measurements. Its only physical-layer input is the binary outcome of a game: winner and loser. The operator is \(A\mapsto s(A,\alpha)\), where \(A\) is the directed count matrix [PAGE 4–6]. Therefore a formal veto proof is available: if two physical, biomechanical, or weather conditions produce the same win-loss graph \(A\), the source’s score \(s\) is identical. The method cannot distinguish them. Any claim that the ranking proves greater force, better biomechanics, superior fatigue resistance, or weather resilience is outside the operator. The paper’s own list of possible extensions—“margin of victory, home-field advantage, progress of the season, and so forth” [PAGE 16]—confirms that those layers are not in the current model. The introduction even asks whether “margins of victory” or home games should count differently [PAGE 2], but the method does not encode them.

The physical-layer veto is therefore: do not infer physical causation from an ordinal path-count score. A team can have a high \(s_i\) because it beat opponents with high win scores; that is a network fact, not a measurement of force, acceleration, injury load, or weather. The biomechanical veto is stronger: there are no biomechanical variables in \(A\), so no biomechanical contrast is identifiable. The weather veto is likewise exact: weather is not a vertex, edge, weight, or covariate in the source. If weather changed outcomes but the resulting win-loss graph were held fixed, the ranking would not change. If weather changed the graph, the source would attribute the change to the graph, not to weather. The only way to bring weather in would be to modify edge weights or add covariates, which the paper says is possible in principle [PAGE 16] but does not do.

The bison example does not rescue a physical-layer claim. It is a dominance-interaction graph correlated with breeding success [PAGE 14], and the Lorenz curve is a plot of “the fraction of mating events that involve bison of a given rank or higher, as a function of rank” [PAGE 15]. That is a social/biological association, not a biomechanical force measurement. The cross-layer adjudication is thus a veto, not a synthesis: the source may rank teams or animals from pairwise wins, but it cannot adjudicate physical/biomechanical/weather causation. Any model that uses this source to claim otherwise should be rejected at the layer boundary.

1. Spectral-bound veto. Candidate claim: “Use \(\alpha=1.05\lambda_{\max}^{-1}\) from the final network and the ranking is still valid.” Prediction: the Neumann series diverges; the residual \(\|(I-\alpha A^T)w-k_{\mathrm{out}}\|_2\) cannot be driven to zero by the convergent path-count interpretation, and the inverse is at or beyond the spectral boundary. Verifiable test: compute \(\rho(A)\), check \(\alpha\rho(A)\). If \(\alpha\rho(A)\ge1\), veto the candidate ranking. The source states convergence only for \(\alpha<\lambda_{\max}^{-1}\) [PAGE 7], so this veto is directly source-grounded. A second version: if actual \(\lambda_{\max}\) were greater than or equal to the random-graph \(\lambda_{\max}\) used in Eq. (10), then the ex ante \(\alpha\) would fail the same test. Prediction: in the source’s seasons, actual \(\lambda_{\max}\) is about 20% lower than the random value [PAGE 10], so the Eq. (10) \(\alpha\) passes; a season that reverses that relation should be flagged.

2. Clock-leakage veto. Candidate claim: “This is a weekly ranking system, and we can use the final-season \(\alpha\) and final-season adjacency matrix to rank week \(t\).” Prediction: future games leak into the week-\(t\) score. For a team whose future win over a strong opponent is included, its week-\(t\) score is inflated by path contributions that did not exist at week \(t\). Verifiable test: truncate \(A\) at each week, recompute the ranking with the schedule-based \(\alpha\), and compare with the candidate’s week-\(t\) ranking. If the candidate’s ranking changes when future edges are removed, the clock has been violated. The source explicitly wants to decide \(\alpha\) “before the start of the season” [PAGE 8], so this is a legitimate veto. Prediction: rankings for teams with no future games should be stable; rankings for teams with future wins over high-score opponents should move when future edges are removed. If the candidate’s weekly ranking is identical to the final ranking for such teams, reject it as retrospective.

3. Small-sample retrodiction veto. Candidate claim: “The method is about 80% accurate, so it should be about 80% accurate in every season and every subset.” Prediction: the source’s own Table V contradicts a universal 80% claim. For 2001, the method is \(7/15=0.47\); for 2002, \(16/18=0.89\) [PAGE 14]. A binomial/Wilson interval for \(7/15\) is roughly \(0.25\) to \(0.70\) at 95% confidence, so 0.80 is outside that interval. Verifiable test: recompute retrodictive accuracy on the exact Table V subsets and on a broader all-games set; if the candidate’s claimed accuracy lies outside the interval for the relevant season, veto the claim. Prediction: the 2001 subset will reject an 80% assertion, while the pooled multi-year figure may be near 0.70–0.80 but with wide season-to-season variation. This veto protects against using the paper’s broad “about 80%” sentence [PAGE 8] as a precise, season-invariant guarantee.
