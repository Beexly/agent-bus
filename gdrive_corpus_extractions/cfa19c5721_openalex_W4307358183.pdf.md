Eminem explicitly turns Trump’s populism on its head: “like him in politics, I’m usin’ all of his tricks” [PAGE 9], thereby delegitimising Trump while connoting street-smart intelligence for himself [PAGE 9]. Ezhel’s counter-trap is more constrained: because direct naming risks arrest, he builds the elite through vague signifiers—“they,” “dictator,” “the system,” “arm protectors [police],” “newspapers,” “empty brains” [PAGE 10]—while constructing “the people” as “the public,” “children,” “mothers,” and “a victim” [PAGE 10]. The counter-trap succeeds only insofar as the audience can decode the veil; under heavy repression, the decoding itself becomes an act of dissident solidarity.

Clock. The discourse operates under a temporal constraint imposed by political events. Eminem’s “The Storm” is pegged to the 2017 BET Awards and the immediate post-election anti-Trump spike in hip hop [PAGE 4]. Ezhel’s timeline is tighter: the 2013 Gezi Park protests [PAGE 5], the 2016 coup attempt [PAGE 11], the 2018 arrest for “inciting drug use” [PAGE 5], and the 2020 official release during “further repression” [PAGE 5] create a closing window in which the artist must speak before the channel is shut. Because Ezhel lives in Berlin to escape repression [PAGE 5], the clock is existential rather than merely promotional.

Recursive theory of mind. Layer 1: the artist models the audience. Eminem addresses “any fan of mine who’s a supporter of his” and demands they decide to be “either for or against” Trump [PAGE 6], thereby forcing listeners to self-sort into “the people” or the elite. Ezhel includes himself in a “we” of dissident voices: “the only thing I want is justice, but it’s far from us” [PAGE 10, 15]. Layer 2: the audience models the artist. Authenticity is not intrinsic but “socially, historically and genre dependent,” ascribed by listeners as “sincerity” or “playing from the heart” [PAGE 2]. Layer 3: the artist models the audience’s model of the artist. Eminem’s Detroit opening and dated cars are attempts to overcome the “valuation of race” that might disqualify a white rapper from speaking for Black urban experience [PAGE 8]. Ezhel’s animated close-up and Time magazine cover are legitimacy proxies designed to make viewers “engage with him and his views” [PAGE 14–15]. Each layer recurses: the artist anticipates whether the audience will believe the artist’s claim to be the authentic voice of the people, and adjusts the multimodal signal accordingly.
