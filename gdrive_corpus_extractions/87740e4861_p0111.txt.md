To formalize Tolman's distinctions, we employ tools from Signal Detection Theory (SDT) and Information Theory, assigning rigorous SI and information-theoretic units.

**1. Differentiation-Threshold-Capacity Operator ($\mathcal{D}$):**
This operator quantifies the sensory discriminability of two stimuli, $S_1$ and $S_2$. It is defined by the non-centrality parameter or sensitivity index $d'$ from SDT [PAGE 92].
Let the internal response to stimulus $S_i$ be a random variable $X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_i, \sigma^2)$. Assume equal variance $\sigma^2$ for simplicity (Luce & Galanter, 1963).
$$
d'(S_1, S_2) = \frac{|\mu_1 - \mu_2|}{\sigma} \quad [\text{unitless}]
$$
For small differences $\Delta S = |S_1 - S_2|$ around a background level $S$, this relates to the Weber Fraction $W = \Delta S / S$. Using the linear approximation of the psychometric function near the threshold, we can define the *Differentiation Capacity* $C_D$ as the slope of the psychometric function at the point of subjective equality, which is proportional to $d'$. More fundamentally, we can relate it to the Fisher Information $I(S)$ for the mean $\mu(S)$:
$$
I(S) = \left( \frac{\partial \ln f(x;S)}{\partial S} \right)^2 = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \left( \frac{d\mu}{dS} \right)^2 \quad [\text{units: } S^{-2}]
$$
If we assume a power-law transduction $\mu \propto S^\beta$, then $I(S) \propto S^{2\beta-2}$. For constant Weber fraction ($\beta=1$), $I(S)$ is constant. The *threshold capacity* can be defined as the minimum detectable change $\Delta S_{min}$ such that $d' = d'_{crit}$ (typically ~1.0). Thus,
$$
\mathcal{D}(S) \equiv \frac{1}{\Delta S_{min}} \approx \sqrt{I(S)} \cdot \sigma \quad [\text{units: } S^{-1}]
$$
This represents the reciprocal of the just-noticeable difference, providing a direct measure of discrimination acuity.

**2. Expectational-Capacity Operator ($\mathcal{E}$):**
This operator is more complex, involving the integration of uncertain observations to form a hypothesis $H$ about the state of the world (e.g., "the correct goal arm is left"). We model this using Bayesian inference, consistent with predictive coding frameworks (Friston, 2010). Let $\theta$ represent the true environmental parameters (e.g., geometry, reward location). The animal maintains a posterior distribution $P(\theta | D_t)$ after observing data stream $D_t = \{x_1, ..., x_t\}$.
The *Expectational Capacity* is defined as the expected reduction in uncertainty about $\theta$ per unit time, given optimal Bayesian updating. This is essentially the mutual information between the true state and the accumulated evidence.
$$
\mathcal{E} \equiv \lim_{t \to \infty} \frac{1}{t} I(\theta ; D_t) = \lim_{t \to \infty} \frac{1}{t} \int p(\theta, D_t) \log \frac{p(\theta, D_t)}{p(\theta)p(D_t)} d\theta dD_t \quad [\text{units: bits/time}]
$$
In practice, for a stationary environment, this converges to the rate of information acquisition. If the sensory inputs are Markovian with transition matrix determined by $\theta$, this is related to the ergodic Shannon information rate of the process. A simpler, operational definition for a specific task (e.g., discriminating two maze configurations $H_A$ vs $H_B$) is the inverse of the number of trials $N_{conv}$ required to reach a target confidence level $p_{target}$ (e.g., 0.95) via sequential probability ratio testing (Wald, 1947).
$$
\mathcal{E}_{task} \approx \frac{D_{KL}[p(D|H_A) || p(D|H_B)]}{-\ln(\alpha) - \ln(\beta)} \quad [\text{units: bits/trial}]
$$
where $D_{KL}$ is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the distributions of outcomes under the two hypotheses, and $\alpha, \beta$ are error probabilities. This measures how efficiently the system can update its belief state.

**3. Linking $\mathcal{D}$ and $\mathcal{E}$: The Perspective Distortion Regime:**
Tolman emphasizes that perception involves expecting true properties despite distortions. Consider a geometric figure of size $L$ viewed at distance $z$. The retinal image size is $s = L/z$. A change in distance $\Delta z$ causes a change in $s$ that mimics a change in $L$. The system must disambiguate this.
Assume the animal has an estimate of distance $\hat{z}$ with variance $\sigma_z^2$ and an estimate of retinal size $\hat{s}$ with variance derived from $\mathcal{D}(s)$. The inferred size is $\hat{L} = \hat{s} \cdot \hat{z}$. By error propagation,
$$
\sigma_L^2 \approx (\hat{z})^2 \sigma_s^2 + (\hat{s})^2 \sigma_z^2
$$
A high differentiation capacity $\mathcal{D}(s)$ reduces $\sigma_s$, but if the depth cue $\mathcal{D}(z)$ is poor (large $\sigma_z$), the overall perceptual constancy fails. The expectational capacity $\mathcal{E}$ in this context is the ability to maintain a stable posterior $P(L | s, z)$ with low variance despite fluctuations in $s$ and $z$. Formally, this requires the sensory channels for $s$ and $z$ to provide sufficient mutual information to constrain $L$.

**Breakdown Regimes:**
*   **Regime 1: Sensory Saturation/Noise Floor.** If stimulus intensity approaches absolute threshold, $\mu \to 0$, $\sigma \to \text{const}$, so $d' \to 0$. Consequently, $I(S) \to 0$, and $\mathcal{D} \to 0$. In this regime, $\mathcal{E}$ collapses because no reliable information enters the system to update expectations. Performance degrades to chance levels regardless of cognitive sophistication.
*   **Regime 2: Cognitive Overload/Complexity.** If the environment's complexity (number of relevant parameters $\theta$) exceeds the integrative capacity of the organism (e.g., working memory limits), the posterior updates become inaccurate. Even with perfect $\mathcal{D}$, if the mental model cannot represent the joint distribution $P(\theta)$, the effective $\mathcal{E}$ remains low. This is a breakdown of the computational architecture supporting expectation formation.
*   **Regime 3: Pathological Prior Dominance.** In cases of severe neuropsychiatric conditions or artificial manipulations (e.g., lesions to prefrontal cortex), the prior $P(\theta)$ may become rigidly fixed. The system then ignores new sensory evidence, leading to a decoupling where $\mathcal{D}$ is high but $\mathcal{E}$ is effectively zero because beliefs do not update. This violates the normative Bayesian link between the two operators.

These equations provide a formal, quantitative bridge between Tolman's qualitative distinctions and modern neuroscientific metrics, allowing for precise hypothesis testing regarding the independence and interaction of sensory and cognitive faculties.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage of Tolman's dual-capacity framework extends back to the late 19th-century work of Gustav Fechner and Hermann von Helmholtz, who first grappled with the gap between physical stimulation and conscious perception. Helmholtz's concept of "unconscious inference" (1867) posited that perception is a hypothesis-testing process, a proto-Bayesian notion that anticipates Tolman's "expectational-capacities." Helmholtz used the example of judging distance: the visual system uses monocular cues (like relative size) in conjunction with binocular disparity, integrating them to infer metric properties. This is precisely the kind of "distorting factor" compensation Tolman alludes to. However, Helmholtz remained largely philosophical; his methods were introspective and human-centric.

The crucial shift came with the rise of experimental psychology and psychophysics in the early 20th century. Edward Titchener's structuralism attempted to decompose experience into elementary sensations, focusing intensely on what we now call differentiation-thresholds (just noticeable differences). Simultaneously, Wilhelm Wundt emphasized apperception—a higher-order selective attention process that shapes expectations. This dichotomy mirrors Tolman's later split. The behaviorist revolution, spearheaded by John B. Watson, violently rejected both introspection and mentalistic concepts like "expectation," reducing behavior to simple S-R bonds conditioned by reinforcement histories. In this paradigm, only the observable threshold responses mattered; the internal "map" was deemed metaphysical.

Tolman's work represents a pivotal retrocausal reaction against this reductive behaviorism. By introducing "purposive" behavior and covert variables like "expectancies," he sought to salvage the explanatory power of cognitive constructs while adhering to objective methodology. His famous "latent learning" experiments (Tolman & Honzik, 1930) demonstrated that rats exploring a maze without reinforcement suddenly performed optimally when a reward was introduced, implying they had formed a cognitive map (an expectational structure) during unrewarded exploration. This directly challenged Hull's S-R dominance theory, which predicted that learning required reinforcement.

The genealogical trap emerges when we trace this lineage forward to modern neuroscience. The neat division between "differentiation" (sensory) and "expectation" (cognitive) is increasingly seen as a heuristic rather than a hard ontological boundary. Contemporary theories of predictive coding (Rao & Ballard, 1999; Friston, 2010) propose that the brain is constantly generating predictions (top-down) and processing prediction errors (bottom-up) at every level of the cortical hierarchy. In this view, even early sensory areas (V1) are heavily influenced by contextual expectations. For instance, the perceived brightness of a patch depends on the surrounding luminance—a phenomenon known as simultaneous contrast, which cannot be explained by local differentiation thresholds alone.

Thus, Tolman's distinction, while historically vital, risks being misinterpreted as a modular separation of brain functions. A more accurate genealogical update would frame "differentiation-capacity" as the reliability of the bottom-up prediction error signals, and "expectational-capacity" as the precision-weighting and generative modeling done by higher-order areas. The two are deeply intertwined in a continuous loop. Historical studies on visual pattern discrimination in rats (Fields, 1928; Munn, 1929; Lashley, 1930), cited extensively by Tolman, primarily measured static discrimination thresholds (e.g., ability to distinguish vertical vs horizontal lines). These studies inadvertently validated the S-R model by focusing on performance asymptotes in simple tasks, potentially missing the dynamic, anticipatory nature of perception that Tolman championed. A truly comprehensive study would need to dissociate the influence of stimulus statistics (which shape priors) from inherent sensory limits, something difficult with the methodologies of the 1930s. Therefore, while Tolman correctly identified two critical components, their operational separation in empirical research remains an enduring challenge, revealing the persistent gray zone between sensation and cognition.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In analyzing Tolman’s framework through a recursive theory of mind lens, we must consider not just the animal's primary representations of the world, but its meta-representations—beliefs about its own capacities and the environment's reliability. The distinction between differentiation- and expectational-capacities becomes a subject of metacognitive monitoring. An agent (rat or human) operating in a complex, uncertain environment must develop a sense of "what I know" versus "what I can perceive." This leads to a hierarchical control problem.

Consider a rat navigating a maze where some landmarks are unreliable (e.g., movable walls). To succeed, it must not only form expectations about goal locations (first-order expectation) but also monitor the trustworthiness of its sensory inputs (second-order expectation). If the visual system's differentiation capacity is degraded (e.g., dim lighting), the rat should down-weight visual cues and rely more on proprioceptive or olfactory information. This adaptive weighting requires an internal model of the sensory channels themselves. This is a classic case of "precision weighting" in predictive coding: the brain estimates the variance (inverse precision) of incoming prediction errors to determine their influence on belief updates.

The crisis habit regression manifests when this metacognitive loop fails. Under extreme stress or fatigue, organisms tend to regress to simpler, habitual behaviors (Basaraba et al., 2010). In Tolmanian terms, this is a collapse of expectational flexibility. When the executive systems responsible for maintaining and updating cognitive maps are overloaded or compromised, the animal defaults to stimulus-bound, differentiation-driven responses. For example, a rat might repeatedly turn left at a junction because the distinctive cue associated with the right turn is faint (low differentiation signal), and the cognitive resource needed to override the habitual left-turn response is depleted. This is not a failure of the cognitive map per se, but a failure of the meta-control system that selects between map-based navigation and habitual routines.

Conversely, counter-trapping strategies emerge when an agent develops a theory of mind about another agent (human experimenter or predator). A prey animal might anticipate the hunter's strategy based on past encounters, forming an expectation not just about static landmarks but about dynamic threats. This requires modeling the other agent's expectations. If a rat believes the experimenter will move a landmark to confuse it, it must adjust its own reliance on that landmark. This creates a recursion: my expectation of your expectation of my action influences my action. In highly intelligent species, this can lead to deception tactics, where an animal intentionally violates its usual differentiation-threshold responses to mislead an observer.

The "clock" aspect refers to the temporal dynamics of these processes. Differentiation operates on fast millisecond timescales (neural firing rates), while expectation formation and updating occur over seconds to minutes (synaptic plasticity, consolidation). A mismatch in these timescales can lead to catastrophic failures. For instance, if an environment changes rapidly (e.g., flood washing away landmarks), the slow-updating expectational system may lag behind, causing the animal to persistently seek rewards in now-invalidated locations. Meanwhile, the fast-differentiating system detects novel cues but lacks the integrative framework to incorporate them quickly. This temporal decoupling highlights the need for mechanisms that accelerate learning under uncertainty, such as novelty bonuses or increased exploration driven by dopamine signaling (Dopamine as a precision modifier?).

Ultimately, a recursive theory of mind enriches Tolman's binary distinction by adding layers of self-monitoring and social cognition. The animal isn't just passively receiving stimuli and holding static expectations; it is actively managing its own cognitive resources, assessing the quality of its perceptions, and predicting the intentions of others—all while adapting to temporal constraints. This transforms the simple S-O-R model into a dynamic, multi-agent game played out in real-time, where each player's strategy is contingent on their assessment of their own and others' informational states.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Even the most sophisticated cognitive map and flawless expectational capacity can be nullified by physical, biomechanical, or environmental veto points. These lower-layer constraints act as hard filters on behavior, overriding higher-order intentions. Adjudicating these vetoes is crucial for validating any theory of purposive behavior, as failure to account for them leads to spurious attributions of cognitive deficits.

**1. Physical/Biomechanical Veto: Musculoskeletal Limitations.**
Consider a rat that has perfectly learned the spatial layout of a complex 3D maze (high expectational capacity) and can clearly see the goal platform from afar (high differentiation capacity). However, if the final approach to the platform requires traversing a narrow beam that is too wide for its center of mass to balance on, the rat will fail to reach the goal despite flawless cognition. Its motor system physically prevents execution. This is a veto based on effector dynamics. Another example: a rat with a leg injury may avoid certain paths not because they lead to punishment, but because the biomechanical cost of traversing them is prohibitive. Studies on locomotor patterns show that animals optimize for metabolic efficiency, which can mask underlying cognitive preferences (Kram & Taylor, 1990). Thus, observed path choices reflect an integration of cognitive goals and physical feasibility, mediated by basal ganglia circuits that compute movement costs.

**2. Biomechanical/Sensory Veto: Proprioceptive Conflict.**
When vestibular and visual cues conflict (e.g., rotating room during rotation), severe disorientation occurs. Even if the rat's visual system accurately differentiates the motion of stripes on the wall, the conflicting input from the otolith organs veto the formation of a coherent spatial expectation. The animal may exhibit nausea-like symptoms and freeze. This demonstrates that higher-order perceptual constancies (like recognizing a stationary object amidst self-motion) depend critically on the alignment of multiple sensory streams. If one stream is compromised (e.g., labyrinthectomy), the entire navigational framework can collapse, regardless of intact vision or memory.

**3. Weather/Environmental Veto: Olfactory Disruption.**
Rodents rely heavily on olfaction for many tasks, including social recognition and some forms of spatial navigation (using scent trails). High humidity or rain can wash away odor plumes or saturate the olfactory epithelium, drastically reducing differentiation capacity for chemical stimuli. In a maze where odors serve as critical cues, sudden weather changes (or simulated equivalents in lab settings, like airflow variations) can instantly invalidate the animal's cognitive map. The rat might enter a previously safe alley because the warning scent of a predator is no longer detectable. Here, the environmental condition directly vetoes the effectiveness of a stored expectation by corrupting the sensory channel upon which it depends.

**Proof of Veto Supremacy:**
To prove that these vetoes operate independently and can override cognition, one must design experiments where cognitive demand is high but success is made impossible by a physical constraint. For instance:
*   Train rats on a demanding spatial reversal task requiring flexible use of landmarks (high expectational load).
*   Introduce a temporary muscimol infusion into the red nucleus, impairing cerebellar-dependent motor coordination without affecting hippocampal memory retrieval.
*   Observe whether performance degradation correlates with motor impairment severity, even when post-task probe tests confirm intact memory of the new rule.
If animals with intact memories fail the task solely due to motor disruption, it confirms the veto status of the biomechanical layer. Similarly, applying a reversible desensitizer to olfactory receptors during a visually cued task shows minimal impact, but during an odor-cued task, performance plummets, demonstrating sensory-specific veto effects.

Such adjudications force us to refine Tolman's model. Expectational capacity is not monolithic; it comprises multiple, modality-specific subsystems whose outputs are gated by the integrity of corresponding sensory-motor loops. A complete account of purposive behavior must therefore include a hierarchical veto architecture, where lower-level viability checks continuously gate higher-level intentions before they are enacted. This prevents the system from pursuing futile goals and explains why seemingly irrational behaviors (like ignoring a visible reward) often stem from hidden physical constraints rather than flawed reasoning.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Reinforcement Learning Verification and Refutation (RLVR) Counterfactual Vetoes involve constructing plausible alternative scenarios (counterfactuals) where key assumptions of the RL model are violated, yet the same behavioral outcome is observed. This helps isolate whether behavior is truly driven by value maximization (learning from rewards/punishments) or by other factors like innate predispositions or sensory biases. We present three verifiable veto scenarios applicable to Tolmanian rodent studies.

**Veto Scenario 1: The Phantom Reward Illusion (Violation of Value Consistency).**
*   **Setup:** Train a rat in a T-maze where turning Left always yields a large sugar pellet (Reward A) and Right yields nothing. After extensive training, extinguish the reward (both arms empty). Then, introduce a novel, highly preferred reward (Reward B, e.g., chocolate milk) exclusively in the Right arm.
*   **Standard RL Prediction:** The rat should rapidly switch preference to Right, as B > A in value, driven by prediction error signaling in dopaminergic neurons upon unexpected delivery of B.
*   **Counterfactual Veto Condition:** Suppose the rat continues choosing Left for many trials despite repeated exposure to B in Right. Standard RL attributes this to strong habit persistence (model-free caching). However, we hypothesize a veto based on *differentiation-threshold limitations*. Assume Reward B has a very subtle taste profile that the rat's gustatory system struggles to discriminate from water (low $\mathcal{D}$ for B). The rat literally cannot register B as a significant positive reinforcer, hence no large prediction error is generated. The behavior appears stubborn, but it's actually a consequence of sensory insensitivity vetoing the learning signal.
*   **Verification Test:** Measure electrophysiological responses in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) or orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) to the delivery of B. If activity shows little phasic burst compared to delivery of A, it supports the veto: the lack of learning stems from failed sensory encoding, not poor valuation algorithms. Alternatively, administer a gustatory sensitizer drug and observe if switching accelerates.

**2. Veto Scenario 2: The Geometric Trap Paradox (Violation of Spatial Generalization).**
*   **Setup:** Rats navigate a square arena to find a hidden platform. They typically use geometric cues (wall lengths/corners) to reorient (Cheng, 1986). Now, reshape the arena into a rectangle while keeping the platform relative to one long wall. Rats usually make systematic errors, favoring corners congruent with the original square geometry.
*   **Standard RL/Cognitive Map Prediction:** Errors arise because the cognitive map generalizes the rectangular shape as a rotated version of the square, leading to incorrect extrapolation of the platform location. Updating the map requires overcoming this powerful geometric prior.
*   **Counterfactual Veto Condition:** Imagine the errors persist even when the rectangle is vastly elongated (aspect ratio 10:1), making the original square configuration physically implausible as a interpretation. The veto here comes from a *hardwired, evolutionarily ancient mechanism* for parity symmetry breaking in allocentric space (Shelton & Olton, 1994). This mechanism operates below the level of flexible cognitive mapping, generating a strong bias toward ambiguous symmetric solutions regardless of current evidence. It overrides the rational inference that "this room is definitely not a square."
*   **Verification Test:** Perform hippocampal lesions. If the lesion abolishes the geometric bias entirely (reducing performance to chance), it suggests the bias depended on hippocampal map-building. But if the bias persists post-lesion (perhaps manifesting as random but still corner-preferential choices), it implicates a subcortical veto system (e.g., entopeduncular nucleus or superior colliculus) that enforces geometric heuristics independent of declarative memory. Optogenetic inhibition of these subcortical regions during testing should selectively disrupt the bias without impairing basic spatial learning.

**3. Veto Scenario 3: The Temporal Discounting Mirage (Violation of Delay Independence).**
*   **Setup:** Offer a choice between a small immediate reward (SIR) and a larger delayed reward (LDR). Normally, animals discount LDR hyperbolically, preferring SIR when delay is short.
*   **Standard RL/Economic Model:** Choices reflect an internal calculation of discounted future utility $U = R/(1+kD)$. Changing the delay $D$ smoothly shifts preference according to the discount rate $k$.
*   **Counterfactual Veto Condition:** Introduce a salient distractor event (e.g., brief loud noise) during the waiting period for the LDR. Surprisingly, this can sometimes *increase* patience, making animals more likely to choose LDR, contrary to predictions of distraction increasing impulsivity. This anomalous effect is proposed to stem from a *metacognitive veto* involving anxiety or uncertainty monitoring. The distractor serves as a reminder of the passage of time or potential threat, activating a "wait-and-see" caution mode governed by the amygdala-prefrontal circuitry. This emotional state temporarily suspends the standard discounting algorithm, prioritizing risk avoidance over gain maximization.
*   **Verification Test:** Record neural activity in the lateral septum or basolateral amygdala during the delay. Correlate the magnitude of the "patience-inducing" effect of the distractor with the amplitude of specific oscillatory bands (e.g., theta-gamma coupling) indicative of anxiety states. Pharmacologically blocking corticotropin-releasing factor (CRF) receptors should abolish the paradoxical increase in patience, confirming the veto operates via the anxiety/stress pathway rather than altering the fundamental valuation of delay.

These counterfactual vetoes demonstrate that observed behavior in Tolmanian-style experiments cannot be naively equated with optimized decision-making. Lower-level sensory thresholds, hardwired geometric instincts, and affective states can all impose constraints that mimic or contradict economic rationality, necessitating careful disentanglement to reveal the true nature of "purposive" behavior.
