$$ \mathcal{L}_{meta} = \mathbb{E} \left[ d(\hat{J}_j^i, J_j)^2 \right] $$

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage of Knight's assertion traces back to the classical division of labor and culminates in modern information asymmetry theories. Historically, early economic thought (e.g., Smith) viewed the firm as a mechanism for coordinating physical tasks. Knight's innovation, rooted in his 1921 treatise, was to identify that coordination primarily involves managing *judgments* about the future, which are inherently uncertain [PAGE 292]. This genealogical line connects directly to the later works of Hayek on dispersed knowledge and Akerlof's "Market for Lemons," both of which rely on the premise that knowing what others know is harder than knowing the facts themselves.

The "multi-week trap" emerges from this genealogy. As organizations institutionalized these layers of meta-judgment, they created feedback loops where performance metrics began measuring the *appearance* of good judgment rather than its substance. Over weeks or months, employees learn to optimize their outputs to satisfy the evaluator's model of them ($\hat{J}_j^i$), rather than to address the real situation $S$. This is a retrocausal effect: the future evaluation criteria rewrite the past behavior, causing a decay in genuine situational awareness. The historical precedent for this is seen in large wartime bureaucracies or late-stage corporate monopolies, where internal communication focuses more on protecting turf (managing opinions of men) than on solving logistical problems (managing facts).

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In a high-pressure environment governed by Knight’s principles, an individual’s recursive theory of mind—their model of how others think about them—is constantly stressed. Under normal operations, a manager maintains a complex Bayesian network of subordinates’ competencies. However, during a crisis (e.g., sudden market volatility or a security breach), cognitive load spikes. The system undergoes "crisis habit regression." Instead of updating probabilistic beliefs about specific individuals based on new data, the manager reverts to primitive heuristics: tribalism (trusting only the in-group), deference to seniority, or suspicion of outsiders. 

This regression fundamentally alters the information flow. Subordinates, perceiving this shift, also regress, opting to conceal bad news or provide overly simplistic answers to avoid triggering the manager's negative priors. This creates a counter-trap: the very mechanisms designed to ensure "responsible control" by monitoring "errors in opinions of men" [PAGE 292] instead trigger a collapse into groupthink or paralysis. The clock ticks down on the external crisis while the internal focus remains locked on navigating the distorted social landscape, perfectly illustrating Knight's warning about the dominance of interpersonal judgment over factual assessment.
