Frequency was reported in 9/12 (66.7%), intensity in 6/12 (50%), time in 6/12 (50%), and type in 6/12 (50%) [PAGE 494]. Critically, of the six studies reporting intensity, “none of these six studies explained how this intensity was determined (i.e., as a percentage of one repetition maximum (1RM), or ratings of perceived exertion (RPE))” [PAGE 494]. This means the intensity operator mapping a participant to a prescribed load is a black box with no physical unit and no calibration standard. Hop 3: Dose—the actual volume performed and deviations from plan—was not reported in any study [PAGE 494, Table 4]. Without dose D, the fundamental exercise-oncology derivative ∂O/∂D is mathematically singular; outcome O cannot be attributed to a quantifiable exposure.

Gray-zone audit reveals near-singularities that compound the problem. First, two studies prescribed resistance training as “optional” (Befort et al., 2012; Hayes et al., 2018) [PAGE 494], which introduces a mixture distribution: the observed outcome is a convolution of exercisers and non-exercisers within the intervention arm, biasing intent-to-treat estimates toward the null. Second, rurality is heterogeneously operationalized: 6/12 studies included only regional/rural/remote participants, while 6/12 included mixed rural-urban samples with rural proportions ranging from 32% to 84% [PAGE 490]. This is cluster-level contamination; rural-specific effect sizes cannot be isolated from mixed-arm studies without individual-level rurality-stratified data, which was not separately reported. Third, completion rates ranged from 28.0% to 98.6% and uptake ranged from 0.6% to 85.8% [PAGE 494, Table 4], indicating extreme selection heterogeneity. Studies with uptake below 20% (Gallant et al., 2013; Hirko et al., 2021) [PAGE 498] may reflect delivery-method failure rather than intervention inefficacy, but the review cannot disentangle these because fidelity dose was never recorded.

The breakdown regime occurs when the fidelity operator F (adherence, dose, quality of delivery, participant responsiveness, differentiation) is partially null. Differentiation is universally reported (100%) [PAGE 494], but this is a descriptive label, not a mechanistic parameter. Quality of delivery is reported in 11/12 (91.7%) [PAGE 494], yet provider qualifications varied (exercise physiologists, oncology-certified trainers, graduate students) without a common competency metric. Thus, the review’s conclusion that reporting is “poor” [PAGE 487] is an understatement: the missing data are precisely the variables required to compute dose-response and to replicate the intervention in resource-constrained rural settings.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical lineage. The review anchors its reporting benchmarks to Hoffman (2002) for the six core exercise-training principles [PAGE 488, 491], to Carroll et al. (2007) for the implementation-fidelity framework (adherence, dose, quality of delivery, participant responsiveness, differentiation) [PAGE 488, 494–495], to Campbell et al. (2019) for exercise-oncology prescription guidelines [PAGE 488], and to Fairman et al. (2020) for transparent reporting of resistance-training dose, adherence, and tolerance [PAGE 488, 494–495]. Rowe et al. (2026) extends this lineage into regional, rural, and remote oncology, identifying a reporting-fidelity gap that parallels prior urban findings by Campbell et al. (2012), Neil-Sztramko et al. (2019a; 2019b), and Winters-Stone et al. (2014) [PAGE 488].

Multi-week traps. The eligibility criterion required interventions of two weeks or longer [PAGE 489]. This temporal floor captures the early neural-adaptation phase of resistance training but excludes the detraining and reversibility window that emerges after stimulus cessation. Only one study, Marker et al. (2025), reported reversibility via follow-up objective measures [PAGE 491], and only that study addressed diminished returns by comparing exercisers and non-exercisers at baseline and post-intervention [PAGE 491]. The remaining 11 studies lack longitudinal data beyond intervention end, creating a “clock mismatch”: intervention durations ranged from 10 to 52 weeks [PAGE 493], yet no study measured whether adaptations persisted, decayed, or reversed after the exercise stimulus was removed. In rural settings with limited follow-up infrastructure [PAGE 488], this temporal blind spot is acute because reversibility is the principle that determines whether a short-term rural exercise program yields durable survivorship benefit or merely transient improvement.

The intervention-duration clock also interacts with the cancer-treatment clock. Samples included patients across stage I–IV and phases from pre-treatment through survivorship (up to 3 years post-treatment) [PAGE 490, Table 1]. A 12-week resistance program delivered during active chemotherapy operates under a different biological constraint set (immunosuppression, fatigue, toxicity) than the same program delivered during survivorship [PAGE 490]. Because initial values were reported in only 4/12 studies [PAGE 491], the baseline physiological state relative to treatment phase is largely unknown, making it impossible to retro-causally attribute outcomes to the intervention versus disease trajectory or natural history.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. When rural oncology providers confront workforce shortages, equipment scarcity, long wait-times, and geographic dispersion [PAGE 488, 498], they regress to low-fidelity habits: prescribing resistance training as “optional” (Befort et al., 2012; Hayes et al., 2018) [PAGE 494], relying on unverified “moderate intensity” descriptors without %1RM or RPE anchoring [PAGE 494], and omitting dose tracking entirely [PAGE 494]. This regression is cognitively efficient under resource scarcity but epistemically destructive: it converts resistance exercise from a precisely dosed medical intervention into an ill-defined behavioral suggestion, forfeiting the ability to establish dose-response relationships in the population with the greatest access barriers.

Counter-trap. The reviewed studies exhibit a reporting counter-trap: differentiation was reported in 100% of studies and quality of delivery in 91.7% [PAGE 494], creating an appearance of fidelity rigor. However, these are the easiest fidelity domains to narratively describe (e.g., “telehealth + app,” “exercise physiologist delivered”) and require no quantitative dose data. The most consequential fidelity domain—actual dose—was reported in 0% of studies [PAGE 494]. This asymmetry suggests a Potemkin-fidelity facade: studies signal implementation quality through provider credentials and differentiation labels while concealing the mechanical truth of what participants actually lifted. A rural clinician reading these reports would infer high-quality delivery but remain unable to replicate load, volume, or progression.

Clock. The recursive theory of mind must reconcile three clocks: (1) the intervention-duration clock (10–52 weeks) [PAGE 493]; (2) the cancer-treatment clock (pre-treatment, during active treatment, up to 3 years post-treatment) [PAGE 490]; and (3) the biological adaptation clock, on which reversibility and diminished returns operate over timescales often exceeding the funded study window [PAGE 491]. Because only one study captured reversibility [PAGE 491], the adaptation clock is effectively stopped in the evidence base. Rural clinicians attempting to extrapolate from these trials must guess whether gains persist beyond the intervention period—a guess unsupported by the literature.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto layer. The included studies span cancer stage I–IV and phases from pre-treatment to survivorship [PAGE 490, Table 1]. Treatment-related toxicities (fatigue, pain, neuropathy, cardiotoxicity) impose a hard physiological veto on standard resistance loading. Without baseline initial values (reported in only 4/12 studies) [PAGE 491], there is no evidence that load was titrated to the participant’s treatment-phase capacity. The absence of reversibility reporting in 11/12 studies [PAGE 491] means the veto is never stress-tested during treatment interruption or detraining.

Biomechanical veto layer. Resistance modality (bands, free weights, body weight, machines) was reported in only 6/12 studies [PAGE 494], and exact exercises, repetitions, and sets were detailed in only one study (Hirko et al., 2021) [PAGE 491]. The volume operator 𝒱 = Σ(sets × reps × load) is therefore undefined for 11/12 studies [PAGE 493–494]. A biomechanical veto is triggered: the actual mechanical work performed cannot be reconstructed, so claims about muscular strength, hypertrophy, or functional-capacity outcomes cannot be causally linked to a specific resistance dose.

Weather/geographic veto layer. Rural and remote settings impose environmental vetoes: transport barriers, limited clinic infrastructure, reduced access to oncology-trained exercise professionals, and reliance on telehealth or home-based delivery [PAGE 488, 498]. Six of twelve studies were conducted in mixed rural-urban samples with rural representation from 32% to 84% [PAGE 490], introducing geographic confounding that prevents isolation of rural-specific effects. The veto is not merely logistical; it determines whether a protocol designed for supervised urban delivery can survive translation to unsupervised rural home settings.

Adjudication outcome. All 12 studies fail at least one veto layer; 10/12 failed to report more than two core principles [PAGE 491]; 12/12 failed to report dose [PAGE 494]. No study achieves full reporting fidelity across specificity, progression, overload, initial values, reversibility, diminished returns, all four FITT principles, and dose. The review’s verdict—that reporting is “inconsistent and of poor quality” [PAGE 497]—is the minimal defensible conclusion; the stronger conclusion is that the current literature is non-replicable and non-translatable for rural exercise oncology.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Veto Scenario 1: Replication failure from undefined dose.
Counterfactual: A rural exercise physiologist attempts to replicate Coletta et al. (2021) or Marker et al. (2025) using only the published FITT table [PAGE 493, Table 3].
Verification condition: The published tables do not specify sets, repetitions, or load for most exercises, and intensity is reported categorically (moderate/vigorous) without %1RM or RPE anchoring [PAGE 494].
Prediction: The replicated intervention will produce mechanical volume differing by >50% from the original (unbounded error), yielding either subtherapeutic stimulus (no adaptation) or excessive load (adverse event in immunocompromised patients). The dose-response coefficient ∂O/∂V cannot be estimated; replication is falsified a priori.

Veto Scenario 2: Intensity miscalibration in rural deployment.
Counterfactual: A clinician prescribes “moderate intensity” resistance training to a rural cancer survivor based on Wagoner et al. (2024), which reported “individualised” intensity without quantification [PAGE 494].
Verification condition: Zero of the six studies reporting intensity explained determination via %1RM or RPE [PAGE 494]. Without a reference metric, “moderate” has no SI physical unit (e.g., W or kg·m/s²) and is semantically empty.
Prediction: Prescribed load will deviate from true moderate intensity by an unbounded factor. For a frail, treatment-active survivor, overestimation risks musculoskeletal injury or cardiovascular stress; underestimation yields no hypertrophy stimulus. The veto is triggered because the intensity operator lacks both a physical unit and a calibration standard.

Veto Scenario 3: Meta-analytic ecological fallacy from missing dose.
Counterfactual: A systematic review pools the 12 included studies to estimate the effect of resistance training on cancer-related fatigue in rural populations.
Verification condition: Dose (actual volume performed and deviations from plan) was not reported in any study [PAGE 494, Table 4]. Adherence was reported in only 7/12 and often for the entire multimodal intervention, not the resistance component [PAGE 494].
Prediction: The pooled effect size will be an ecological average over unknown doses, with confidence intervals that do not capture the true dose-response curve. Heterogeneity (τ²) will be inflated and uninterpretable; the analysis cannot identify a minimum effective dose or optimal regimen for rural cancer care [PAGE 497–498]. The meta-analysis is vetoed because the exposure variable is missing for 100% of studies.
