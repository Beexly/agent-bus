<THINK_TRACE>
The paper proposes a semi-online multi-people tracker that reframes data association as a Multi-Label Markov Random Field (MLMRF) optimization over existing trajectories and detection hypotheses drawn from a bounded future window of τ frames. The core epistemological tension is between causal online filters—Kalman/particle Bayesian recursion (Fortmann et al. 1983; Hong-Yoon et al. 2016)—which are real-time but drift irreversibly under occlusion, and acausal offline batch methods (Milan et al. 2016; Dehghan et al. 2015) that optimize over the entire sequence but sacrifice real-time operation. The semi-online scheme inserts a lookahead buffer: at frame t, the optimizer sees active trajectories from the past plus hypotheses h^c_{t',k} for t' ∈ [t, t+τ], solves a labeling problem, commits to associations for frame t, then slides the window forward by one frame.

Each hypothesis receives exactly one label l ∈ L = L_e ∪ L_o ∪ L_f, where L_e indexes currently active targets, L_o spawns new targets, and L_f = 0 marks false positives. The energy function (Eq. 1) combines unary appearance/motion costs, pairwise space-time-view consistency, and a label-cost term λδ(l) that penalizes the cardinality of the label set. This is a re-identification objective: hypotheses are pulled toward existing labels through appearance cosine distance and motion IoU overlap, while the label-cost term discourages proliferation of new identities. The gray zone lies in the approximation: exact MLMRF inference is intractable, so the authors deploy α-expansion with QPBO, then a "fast" variant that restricts expansions to spatially/temporally nearby hypotheses. They report nearly identical objectives with large speedups (Fig. 8, [PAGE 9]), but cap expansion rounds at three. This creates a breakdown regime: if an identity switch requires more than three label-propagation rounds to resolve—e.g., a target re-emerges after a long occlusion beyond the trajectory memory π = 50 frames—the local minimum is stable but wrong.

A second gray zone concerns the appearance model. They fuse LOMO (26,960-D → PCA 256-D) with a 2-D size feature (2-D) into z ∈ R^{258}, feed it through an LSTM of hidden size 258, and apply mean pooling over all time steps to avoid the "fade" of early appearance in recurrent networks (cf. Maksai and Fua 2019 on exposure bias, cited at [PAGE 4]). Mean pooling assumes that early-frame appearance remains equally relevant late in a track; under rapid illumination change or clothing occlusion, this equal-weighting can dilute discriminative signal. The authors claim robustness to changing illumination (Fig. 2, [PAGE 1]), but the cosine-distance saturation regime is not characterized: if appearance distance A → 1 for all candidates, the unary term becomes uninformative and the optimizer collapses to motion-only or label-cost-only decisions.

Multi-camera view constraints (VV) assume pedestrians walk on a flat ground plane and use bottom-center bounding-box coordinates mapped via calibration to 3D ground-plane locations g ∈ R^3. The veto condition is explicit: any target not on the ground plane, or any non-rigid camera motion that invalidates the homography, breaks the 3D distance metric. The motion model similarly degrades: the paper states IoU-based association is "not a proper option" for fast-moving objects or moving cameras ([PAGE 6]), reverting to re-id-only for the current frame. Thus the system's physical domain of validity is bounded to pedestrian-scale velocities, approximately static or slowly moving cameras, and occlusions shorter than the effective memory horizon max(π, τ) ≈ 50 frames.

**Notation (Table 1, [PAGE 3]).**  
Let C be the number of cameras, I^c_t the frame at time t from camera c, and H^c_t = {h^c_{t,1}, ..., h^c_{t,m^c_t}} the detection hypotheses. The active trajectory of target i in camera c is T^c_{t,i}; the active target across views is T_i^t = {T^1_{t,i}, ..., T^C_{t,i}}. The label set L = L_e ∪ L_o ∪ L_f, with L_f = 0. The hypothesis-label random variable is l^c_{t',k}.

**MLMRF energy (Eq. 1, [PAGE 4]).**  
\[
E(\mathbf{l}) = \sum_{c',t',k} U\!\left(l^{c'}_{t',k}\right) + \sum_{(c',t',k),(c'',t'',j)\in\mathcal{N}} V\!\left(l^{c'}_{t',k}, l^{c''}_{t'',j}\right) + \lambda\,\delta(\mathbf{l})
\]
where **l** = {l^1_{t,1}, ..., l^C_{t+τ,m^C_{t+τ}}}, t',t'' ∈ [t, t+τ], λ is the label-cost parameter (dimensionless), and δ counts labels with at least one assigned hypothesis.

**Unary term (Eq. 2, [PAGE 4]).**  
\[
U\!\left(l^{c'}_{t',k}\right) = A\!\left(T^c_{t,i}, h^c_{t',k}\right) + M\!\left(T^c_{t,i}, h^c_{t',k}\right) + \mathbb{1}_{t=t'}\,\Lambda\!\left(T^c_{t,i}, h^c_{t',k}\right)
\]
Units: A and M are dimensionless distances in (−1, 1] and (−1, 0] respectively; Λ is a scalar re-identification bonus/penalty. The indicator activates only for hypotheses in the current frame.

**Appearance distance (Eq. 3, [PAGE 5]).**  
Feature vector z^c_{t',k} ∈ R^{258} (256 PCA-reduced LOMO + 2 size dimensions). Cosine distance:
\[
A\!\left(T^c_{t,i}, h^c_{t',k}\right) = 1 - \frac{z_T \cdot z_h}{\|z_T\|_2\,\|z_h\|_2} \in [-1, 1]
\]
Appearance model: LSTM hidden size 258 with mean pooling over the sequence (rather than last-step output), to preserve early-frame appearance under Liao et al. (2015) LOMO features.

**Motion term ([PAGE 6]).**  
Kalman filter predicts 2D velocity V^c_{t,i}; predicted position \(\hat{h}^c_{t,i}_{t'} = P(T^c_{t,i}, V^c_{t,i}, t')\). Motion distance via IoU overlap o:
\[
M\!\left(T^c_{t,i}, h^c_{t',k}\right) = -\eta^{\Delta t}\, o,\quad o = \frac{\text{Area}(\hat{h}\cap h)}{\text{Area}(\hat{h}\cup h)} \in [0,1],\quad \eta=0.98
\]
Range (−1, 0]. Decay factor η penalizes larger time gaps Δt = t' − t. Valid only for moderate motion; breaks down for fast-moving objects or moving cameras ([PAGE 6]).

**Re-identification threshold ([PAGE 7]).**  
\[
\Lambda = 
\begin{cases}
-\text{dist}(\hat{h}^c_{t,i}_{t'}, h^c_{t',k}) / \text{width}(T^c_{t,i}), & t = t' \\
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]
Spatially distant hypotheses may still match if appearance evidence is strong; threshold ϵ = 2 (units: pixels or meters depending on view) gates this relaxation.

**Pairwise term ([PAGE 7–8]).**  
\[
V = VS + VT + VV
\]
Spatial ([PAGE 7]): penalizes two overlapping hypotheses in the same frame/view taking the same existing or new label, with penalty ω.
Temporal ([PAGE 8]): appearance-based distance A_d between hypotheses at most Δt ∈ {−2, −1, 1, 2} frames apart.
View ([PAGE 8]): ground-plane 3D distance
\[
VV = \|g^{c''}_{t'',k} - g^{c'''}_{t''',j}\|_2,\quad g = (u, v, 0)
\]
Assumes flat ground and calibrated bottom-center mapping.

**Label cost ([PAGE 8]).**  
\[
\delta(\mathbf{l}) = \sum_{l\in L} \mathbb{1}\!\left[\exists h:\; l_h = l\right]
\]
Encourages re-identification to existing labels rather than new-track initialization.

**Optimization ([PAGE 8–10]).**  
α-expansion (Boykov et al. 2001) binarizes the MLMRF; higher-order label cost reduced to quadratic terms via auxiliary binary y_l (Delong et al. 2012); solved with QPBO. Fast α-expansion restricts each hypothesis to expand only into labels of nearby hypotheses, bounding QPBO graph size. Max iterations fixed at 3 ([PAGE 10]).

**Parameters ([PAGE 10–11]).**  
π = 50 frames (trajectory memory); ε ∈ {0.5 (PETS09), 1.0 (MOT17)} false-positive tradeoff; λ = 1; ϵ = 2; β = 0.5 (IoU overlap threshold); η = 0.98; τ = 10 frames (semi-online lookahead window). Running times: PETS09 S2.L2 291 s for 436 frames (1.5 fps); MOT17 27,099 s for 17,757 frames (0.7 fps) ([PAGE 13, Tables 6–7]).

**Breakdown / singularity regimes.**  
1. Occlusion duration > π = 50 frames: trajectory leaves L_e, forcing re-initialization (identity switch).  
2. Moving camera / fast target: IoU motion term invalid; system falls back to appearance only, which can fail under illumination change.  
3. QPBO nonsubmodular pairwise terms: approximate, possibly non-global optima.  
4. α-expansion capped at 3 rounds: stable local minima that may not resolve long-range identity switches.  
5. View constraint assumes planar ground; non-pedestrian targets or tilted ground planes invalidate VV.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper positions itself at the intersection of three historical tracking lineages. First, online Bayesian filtering—Kalman filtering, particle filtering, and Joint Probabilistic Data Association (JPDA, Fortmann et al. 1983)—operates causally on current and past frames only. These are suitable for real-time surveillance but suffer recursive drift that is difficult to correct ([PAGE 1–2]). Second, offline batch methods optimize data association over the entire sequence using min-cost flow, multicut, or higher-order motion models (Milan et al. 2016; Dehghan et al. 2015; Wen et al. 2016). These achieve higher accuracy but are computationally intensive and unsuitable for real-time use. Third, semi-online methods (Choi 2015; Lenz et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2017) delay decisions by a short buffer and exploit near-future hypotheses, attempting to capture offline robustness with online speed.

The paper's novel contribution is the first semi-online tracker explicitly cast as person re-identification. Prior semi-online work (e.g., Lenz et al. 2015) optimized memory-bounded shortest paths or local motion affinities without a mechanism for appearance variation under illumination or viewpoint change ([PAGE 2]). By contrast, this work integrates LOMO re-identification features (Liao et al. 2015) with an LSTM sequence-to-single architecture and mean pooling, directly addressing the weakness noted by Maksai and Fua (2019) regarding metric mismatch and exposure bias in recurrent trackers ([PAGE 4]).

Multi-week traps emerge when this frame-level architecture is deployed over long horizons. The label-cost term λδ(l) prevents unbounded identity proliferation but creates a merger trap: if two distinct pedestrians remain close and visually similar for longer than the temporal pairwise window (±2 frames), the optimizer may assign them the same label, producing a persistent identity switch that compounds over weeks of video. Similarly, the trajectory memory π = 50 frames defines a hard forgetting boundary; any target absent longer than 50 frames is dropped from L_e and must be re-initialized, generating a permanent ID switch in long-term deployments. The semi-online clock advances one frame per optimization step, so the effective identity horizon is bounded by max(π, τ) ≈ 50 frames. Beyond this, the tracker has no principled mechanism to recover identities after appearance drift (e.g., clothing changes, lighting shifts across days), which is the fundamental multi-week failure mode.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The tracker's recursive theory of mind operates at three levels: (1) it models each detection hypothesis as potentially belonging to an existing target, a new target, or clutter; (2) it reasons about pairwise hypotheses as same-or-different identity via space-time-view constraints; and (3) it anticipates future corrections because false positives at frame t can be relabeled as the window slides forward.

Crisis habit regression occurs under occlusion or illumination change. Figure 2 ([PAGE 1]) shows state-of-the-art methods failing under varying lighting: they lose Pedestrian 1 and 2, then initialize two brand-new trackers when the pedestrians reappear. This is the regression to "new target" (L_o) whenever association evidence drops below threshold—a crisis mode that fragments identities and inflates IDS.

The counter-trap is built into the unary and label-cost terms. The re-identification bonus Λ rewards matching a current-frame hypothesis to a nearby predicted target position, while λδ(l) penalizes opening new labels. Together they create an attractive basin toward existing L_e labels, resisting the reflex to spawn new trackers. The fast α-expansion propagates this preference across the hypothesis graph, but only within three expansion rounds and within the local neighborhood, so the counter-trap is spatially and temporally bounded.

The clock is the sliding semi-online window. At each step t, the optimizer decides labels for hypotheses up to t+τ but commits only to frame t. The window then advances: hypotheses at t+τ become part of the past trajectory set, and new future hypotheses enter. A false positive at t can be corrected at t+1…t+τ if future evidence contradicts it ([PAGE 4]). However, if an occlusion outlasts τ, or if a target leaves and re-enters beyond π, the clock expires on that identity. The tracker's theory of mind therefore has a finite recursion depth: it can hypothesize about the near future, but not about identities forgotten beyond the memory horizon.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto (single-camera): The spatial pairwise term enforces that one target cannot occupy two locations simultaneously in the same frame/view. It is implemented as a soft penalty ω for overlapping hypotheses sharing a label ([PAGE 7]). Veto condition: if two detections with non-negligible separation are forced to the same label by appearance similarity, the physical veto should trigger; because the constraint is soft, the veto can be overridden by strong appearance evidence, causing merge errors in crowded scenes.

Biomechanical veto (multi-camera): View constraints assume all targets walk on a flat ground plane and use bottom-center bounding boxes mapped to 3D ground coordinates g = (u, v, 0) ([PAGE 8]). Veto condition: any non-pedestrian target (e.g., a cyclist, a person on a mobility device, or an object thrown in the air) violates the planar-ground assumption. Under this veto, VV distances become geometrically meaningless, and cross-camera re-identification collapses to appearance-only matching.

Weather/illumination veto: The appearance model relies on LOMO features, claimed robust to illumination changes and partial occlusions ([PAGE 4]). Veto condition: extreme illumination regimes (night, glare, sudden shadow transitions) where LOMO discriminability collapses and cosine distance A saturates near 1 for all candidates. When A loses rank-order information, the unary term cannot distinguish targets, and the optimizer defaults to motion and label cost alone, which fails under occlusion.

Moving-camera veto: The motion model explicitly states IoU is "not a proper option" for fast-moving objects or objects in moving cameras ([PAGE 6]); the system then restricts motion association to the current frame and leans on re-identification. Veto condition: vehicle-mounted or handheld camera motion introduces parallax and invalidates the 2D Kalman velocity prediction, breaking M(t) and removing the primary cue for short-term association.

Adjudication hierarchy: Physical vetoes (space-time exclusion) are hard constraints softened by ω; biomechanical vetoes (ground plane) invalidate VV entirely; appearance vetoes (illumination) saturate A; motion vetoes (moving camera) nullify M. When multiple vetoes fire simultaneously—e.g., a moving camera filming a crowded, poorly lit street—the MLMRF has no unary or pairwise term left with discriminative power, and label cost dominates, causing either mass over-segmentation (new labels for every detection) or mass under-segmentation (everything forced into one label).
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Scenario 1 — Extended occlusion beyond memory horizon.  
Setup: A pedestrian in a PETS09 crowded sequence is fully occluded for 60 consecutive frames (> π = 50) and then reappears.  
Veto rule: The trajectory T^c_{t,i} is removed from L_e after 50 frames; upon reappearance, l_h can no longer bind to the old label and must either join L_o (new target) or L_f.  
Prediction: IDS increases by exactly one per such event; MT (mostly tracked) decreases; MOTA drops by approximately (FN + IDS)/GT relative to occlusions shorter than 50 frames. Verifiable on PETS09 S2.L2 where some pedestrians are occluded for whole sequences ([PAGE 11]).

Scenario 2 — Moving-camera parallax violating ground-plane assumption.  
Setup: MOT17 vehicle-mounted sequence with handheld camera motion; two pedestrians walk on a sidewalk that is not coplanar with the assumed ground homography.  
Veto rule: VV computes \|g − g'\|_2 from bottom-center coordinates under a fixed planar calibration. Parallax introduces systematic 3D distance error; cross-camera label propagation is vetoed.  
Prediction: Multi-camera MOTA degrades relative to static-camera sequences on the same dataset; IDS rises on vehicle-mounted subsets (MOT17 includes vehicle-mounted cameras, [PAGE 11–12]). The degradation is not explained by detector quality alone because all methods use identical SDP detections.

Scenario 3 — Close-proximity crossing exceeding temporal pairwise range.  
Setup: Two pedestrians walk side-by-side with high IoU overlap for 3+ consecutive frames, then cross and separate.  
Veto rule: Temporal pairwise VT only links hypotheses at most Δt ∈ {−2, −1, 1, 2} frames apart ([PAGE 8]). If the two targets remain overlapping for more than 2 frames, the optimizer cannot receive a temporal correction signal after they separate; spatial soft constraint ω may allow them to share a label.  
Prediction: Fragments (FM) and IDS increase specifically during crossing events; IDF1 drops on sequences with frequent pedestrian-on-pedestrian occlusion. This is isolable by comparing FM on high-density PETS09 sequences versus sparse MOT17 sequences at matched detector quality ([PAGE 12–13]).
