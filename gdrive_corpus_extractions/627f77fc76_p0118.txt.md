<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The line of scrimmage is an American football innovation that replaced rugby’s scrummage, formalized in the Walter Camp era of the 1880s and codified by 1915 in Spalding’s guide [PAGE 118]. The one-foot tolerance reflects a historical compromise between formal geometry and officiating discretion: early scrimmages permitted informal “hands on ground” alignments, and Rule IX converts that discretion into a bounded tubular predicate.

A multi-week trap emerges from officiating drift. If a crew tolerates linemen creeping inside the 1-ft boundary in weeks 1–4, a team that regresses to that comfortable alignment incurs a 5-yard penalty [PAGE 118] once league emphasis tightens in week 5. The “yard back” rule institutionalizes the quarterback’s depth; a coaching staff that slides the first receiver incrementally closer to the line creates a creeping-legal trap—legal under lax early-season enforcement, illegal under later scrutiny. Guard leg-locking [PAGE 118] is a load-bearing exception that unbalanced formations can mimic, producing a rules-lawyer trap that persists until crews adjust the adjacency-graph interpretation.

The page’s cross-reference to illustrations on pages 153–154 and Rule XVIII Sec. 7(a) [PAGE 118] constitutes an epistemic trap: without those pages, the biomechanical diagrams that would resolve stance ambiguity are missing, so any modern reconstruction of 1915 alignment mechanics is underdetermined by this source alone.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression operates when crowd noise, late-game urgency, or hostile stadium conditions cause offensive linemen to revert to pre-rule comfort zones: hands grounded closer than 1 ft, feet drifting forward during cadence, or guards testing the leg-locking boundary beyond the snapper-back interface. This is a first-order belief: the offense assumes the official will not resolve sub-foot deviations at the snap.

The counter-trap is second-order. Defensive coaches film-study the 1-ft boundary; if they identify systematic regression, they time their rush key on the offensive lineman’s hand or foot crossing inside the tolerance, drawing an offside penalty [PAGE 118] or forcing a reset. The defense relies on the offensive line’s stress-induced regression being detectable, not on probabilistic wagering.

The clock is the forcing function. End-of-half urgency shrinks the cadence window, reducing time to re-establish legal spacing and depth after shifts. A team trailing late may hurry to the line, increasing the probability that \(y_i<1\,\mathrm{yd}\) for a non-receiver backfield man [PAGE 118]. A third-order recursion follows: offense anticipates the defense’s counter-trap and deliberately sets legally but appears illegal, hoping to draw premature defensive movement. The clock collapses this recursion into a physical violation at the snap because the finite set-ready interval leaves no margin for correcting a 1-ft or 1-yd error after the ball is snapped.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: The line of scrimmage is a marked geometric object on turf or dirt with nonzero width \(w_{\text{line}}\). The predicate \(d\le1\,\mathrm{ft}\) presupposes a measure-zero line. If \(w_{\text{line}}\) approaches or exceeds the inspection resolution of the official, the operator \(\mathrm{ONLINE}(i)\) is ill-posed; the physical layer vetoes enforcement and the play must be whistled down or the formation reset rather than penalized on an unverifiable boundary.

Biomechanical layer: A lineman in a two-point stance sets feet but may move hands during cadence; \(\mathrm{ONLINE}(i)\) must hold at the snap, so a hand rising above 1 ft before snap voids the predicate. The snapperback’s head and hands may occupy \(y>0\) [PAGE 118], but the torso must remain \(y\le0\); the legal configuration space is a constrained manifold with a discontinuity at the snap instant. Torso-ahead snap is an offside veto.

Weather layer: Rain or mud reduces static friction, permitting post-set foot slip that violates spacing or ONLINE between ready-for-play and snap. Snow accumulation obscures \(L\), making \(d(\mathbf{r},L)\) unobservable. Wind alters snap trajectory but not alignment directly.

Veto proof: All three layers must pass verification. Physical layer failure (line unobservable) defaults to an official timeout rather than a team penalty because the measurement operator is undefined. Biomechanical layer failure (torso ahead, hand high, foot inside spacing) yields the 5-yard loss specified in [PAGE 118]. Weather-induced foot slip after the ready signal is treated as a movement infraction if attributable to the player, but line obscurity from snow shifts the burden to the officials.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
