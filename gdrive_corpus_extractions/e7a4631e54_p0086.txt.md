<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Three layers veto the pure rational-apportionment model. Biomechanical layer: human working memory and executive function cannot stably maintain a one- to five-year resource apportionment; cognitive load and decision fatigue impose an effective horizon T_eff = min(T, T_cog) with T_cog ≪ 5 years under stress. Physical layer: the budget constraint Σ p_{i,t} x_{i,t} ≤ R is a hard resource veto; an income shock that lowers R forces evaluation of B(x) at a lower x, potentially stranding the agent on the negative side of the x* singularity. Weather/exogenous-shock layer: macroeconomic or supply disruptions function as common shocks η that strike all agents simultaneously; they are the physical analogue of the advertising common component in footnote 1 [PAGE 1], pushing ρ → 1 and voiding the law-of-large-numbers rescue [PAGE 1].

Adjudication: the model is valid only in the regime where (i) T ≤ T_cog, (ii) ρ < 1 sufficiently that (1−ρ²)/N dominates ρ²σ_η², and (iii) x remains distant from x* so that no abrupt sign flip is triggered. The veto proof is that in crisis all three conditions fail simultaneously: T collapses, ρ approaches unity through coordinated marketing and macro shocks, and R shocks push x across x*. The exact singularity/breakdown regime is x = x* with ρ = 1 and T > T_cog: there, B has no well-defined continuous interior root, the subgradient 0 ∈ ∂B(x*) is not approached smoothly, and corner solutions with hysteresis obtain. Under these veto conditions the text's own "approximate rationality" [PAGE 1] is inoperative, and the balance never cleanly strikes; instead the system exhibits discrete allocation switches and path dependence.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1 — Correlated-impulse veto. Counterfactual: remove the common advertising/salesmanship shock so ρ = 0; actual: ρ > 0 per footnote 1 [PAGE 1]. Prediction: with ρ = 0, \bar{ξ}_N \xrightarrow{p} 0 and aggregate demand converges to the rational apportionment as N grows; with ρ > 0, lim_{N→∞} Var(\bar{ξ}_N) = ρ²σ_η² > 0, so aggregate demand retains a systematic, non-vanishing bias toward impulse goods even in large markets. Verifiable implication: the cross-sectional variance of impulse-good expenditure should not decline with market size once advertising correlation is present.

Scenario 2 — Horizon-truncation veto. Counterfactual: agents sustain T = 5 years; crisis truncates T to weeks. Prediction: under the long horizon, marginal utility is equalized across time (∂u_t/∂x_{i,t} equalized via λ p_{i,t}); under truncation, demand for durable and long-lived goods collapses disproportionately while perishable/immediate goods spike, because the agent liquidates before the balance can approach x* from the positive side [PAGE 1]. Verifiable implication: the durable-to-nondurable expenditure ratio should drop discontinuously when credit or planning horizons shorten, without a corresponding smooth price adjustment.

Scenario 3 — Singularity-crossing veto. Counterfactual: B(x) is smooth and single-crossing at x*; actual: Knight's "suddenly becomes negative" discontinuity at x* [PAGE 1]. Prediction: if smooth, a small price decrease restores positive balance continuously; if discontinuous at x*, demand exhibits hysteresis—once x > x* and the balance turns negative, restoring x below x* requires a price change larger than the initial trigger, so price cuts below threshold fail to re-engage purchase until a secondary threshold is crossed. Verifiable implication: consumption bundles around the comfort/subsistence threshold should show path dependence; identical prices can sustain different purchase states depending on whether the agent previously crossed x*.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
