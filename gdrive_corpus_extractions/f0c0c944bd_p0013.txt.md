<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from Glosten and Milgrom (1985) and Madhavan and Smidt (1991), both named in [PAGE 13], through Kyle (1985) continuous-auction informed trading and Easley and O’Hara (1992) order-flow informativeness, into the present patent’s modular architecture. The critical historical insight inherited by US 7,599,876 B1 is that order flow is a stochastic signal of private information, and that the market maker must invert that signal via Bayesian learning rather than post static quotes. The EMM patent compresses this into three modules—Quality Control, Inventory Control, Speculation—ruled by an Arbitration Module that trades off profit against market quality through tunable weights.

Multi-week trap: In live operation, market microstructure regimes drift over weekly horizons (volatility clustering, earnings cycles, changes in informed-trader composition, regulatory or venue rule changes). Because the patent prefers that the EMM be “trained” with simulated or experimental data [PAGE 13], the weights w_m and the discrepancy threshold τ are calibrated to a historical or synthetic distribution that may be stale by the time of deployment. The “learning cycle” performance quotient—aggregated over stored protocols and compared to historical data—creates a lookback vulnerability: a short window overfits to the most recent regime; a long window averages over obsolete regimes. After a regime shift, Test 425 consensus failures increase in frequency, invoking block 430 parameter expansion (increasing limits/ranges). If that expansion is monotonic and uncapped, the system slowly drifts toward a permissive state in which consensus is easy to manufacture because almost any recommendation falls within widened bands, eroding the adverse-selection discipline that the original Bayesian model was designed to enforce. This is a multi-week drift trap: the control law’s remedy for disagreement is precisely the mechanism that degrades the very discrimination the information-based model requires.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Each module embeds an implicit theory of the others. The Speculation Module, when it forecasts a price rise and recommends a higher long position [PAGE 13], models the Inventory Module as a mean-reverting restraint that will later trim exposure. The Inventory Module, conversely, models the Speculation Module as a momentum-chasing signal that may overshoot after positive order imbalance. Under crisis conditions, both regress to habit: Speculation doubles down on the perceived trend (momentum ignition), while Inventory slashes risk (forced liquidation), producing the exact conflict the patent describes—Speculation recommends increasing inventory while Inventory recommends the opposite [PAGE 13].

Counter-trap: The consensus gate at Test 425 creates a second-order incentive. If modules learn that disagreement triggers block 430 parameter relaxation (wider limits/ranges), a module can profitably issue extreme recommendations not because they reflect its private model, but to force the arbitration layer into a more permissive regime. The meta-controller therefore faces a recursive manipulation: its dispute-resolution rule subsidizes strategic disagreement. The equilibrium outcome may be a “consensus” reached only after limits have been inflated so far that the test no longer binds, which is consensus in name only.

Clock: The learning-cycle interval T_learn sets the adaptation clock. If T_learn is shorter than the market regime’s autocorrelation time, the system chases noise and over-adjusts weights; if longer, it misses the regime change and the performance quotient Q(T) = (1/N)Σ_{i=T-N}^{T} profit_i continues to be benchmarked against a historical distribution that no longer applies. Under stress, crisis habit regression dominates: the system reverts to pre-set default weights rather than exploring new configurations, locking in a suboptimal response exactly when the information environment has changed most.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
