**1. Outer Measure Definition**
Given a premeasure \(\mu\) on a ring \(\mathcal{R}\) of subsets of \(X\), the outer measure \(\mu^*: \mathcal{P}(X) \to [0,\infty]\) is defined for any \(E \subseteq X\) by:
\[
\mu^*(E) = \inf \left\{ \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(A_n) : A_n \in \mathcal{R}, E \subseteq \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n \right\}.
\]
Units: Same as \(\mu\) (e.g., length, area, volume, probability mass).

**2. Measurable Cover Existence Construction**
[PAGE 66] For a given \(E \subseteq X\) with \(\mu^*(E) < \infty\), there exists a sequence \(\{F_n\}\) in the σ-ring of measurable sets \(\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{R})\) such that:
\[
E \subseteq F_n \quad \text{and} \quad \mu(F_n) < \mu^*(E) + \frac{1}{n}.
\]
Define \(F = \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty F_n\). Then \(F \in \mathcal{S}(\mathcal{R})\), \(E \subseteq F\), and:
\[
\mu(F) \leq \mu(F_n) < \mu^*(E) + \frac{1}{n} \quad \forall n \implies \mu(F) \leq \mu^*(E).
\]
By the monotonicity of outer measure, since \(E \subseteq F\), \(\mu^*(E) \leq \mu^*(F) = \mu(F)\) (as \(F\) is measurable). Hence:
\[
\mu(F) = \mu^*(E).
\]

**3. Uniqueness of Measurable Covers (Theorem D)**
[PAGE 66] Let \(E \subseteq X\) and suppose \(F_1, F_2 \in \mathcal{S}(\mathcal{R})\) are measurable covers of \(E\), i.e.,
\[
E \subseteq F_i, \quad \mu(F_i) = \mu^*(E) \quad \text{for } i=1,2.
\]
Then their symmetric difference has measure zero:
\[
\mu(F_1 \Delta F_2) = 0.
\]
*Proof Sketch:* Since \(E \subseteq F_1 \cap F_2 \subseteq F_1\), we have \(F_1 - (F_1 \cap F_2) \subseteq F_1 - E\). Because \(F_1\) is a measurable cover, any measurable subset of \(F_1\) disjoint from \(E\) must have measure zero (otherwise, removing it would yield a smaller measurable cover). Thus \(\mu(F_1 \setminus F_2) = 0\). Symmetrically, \(\mu(F_2 \setminus F_1) = 0\), so \(\mu(F_1 \Delta F_2) = 0\).

**4. σ-Finiteness Preservation (Theorem E)**
[PAGE 66] If \(\mu\) on \(\mathcal{R}\) is σ-finite, then the induced measure \(\mu\) on \(\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{R})\) and its completion \(\mu\) on \(\mathcal{S}\) are also σ-finite.
*Assumptions:* There exist \(\{A_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subseteq \mathcal{R}\) such that \(X = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\) and \(\mu(A_n) < \infty\) for all \(n\).
Since \(\mathcal{R} \subseteq \mathcal{S}(\mathcal{R})\), these same sets lie in \(\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{R})\) and satisfy \(\mu(A_n) < \infty\). Thus \(\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{R})\) is σ-finite. For the completion \(\mathcal{S}\), any \(B \in \mathcal{S}\) can be written as \(B = M \cup N\) with \(M \in \mathcal{S}(\mathcal{R})\), \(N \subseteq Z\) for some \(Z \in \mathcal{S}(\mathcal{R})\) with \(\mu(Z)=0\). Since \(Z\) is covered by the σ-finite collection, \(\mathcal{S}\) remains σ-finite.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The development presented in Halmos's text is the culmination of early 20th-century efforts to rigorously define integration and measure. Its direct antecedents are Lebesgue's integral (1902) and Carathéodory's extension theorem (1914). Lebesgue introduced the idea of measuring sets via outer approximations by intervals, defining measurability through the ability to split any interval additively. Carathéodory generalized this by starting from an arbitrary premeasure on a ring (not just intervals on ℝ) and defining measurability via the splitting condition for *all* subsets, leading to a σ-algebra of measurable sets. Halmos systematized and streamlined this approach, emphasizing the role of rings and σ-rings over algebras and σ-algebras, making the theory applicable to broader contexts (e.g., locally compact groups with Haar measure).

The concept of a "measurable cover" arises naturally in this lineage. Given the outer measure, one seeks the smallest measurable set containing a given set E. In Lebesgue measure on ℝ, this is straightforward: take an open set G containing E with m(G) close to m*(E), then let F be a Gδ set inside G still containing E. In the abstract setting, Halmos constructs F as the intersection of a decreasing sequence of measurable sets (from the σ-ring) whose measures approach µ*(E). This mirrors the topological notion of taking intersections of open neighborhoods.

Historically, multi-week traps in understanding this material often involve conflating different notions of "approximation." Students might confuse:
1. **Outer approximation by open sets** (topology-dependent, requires regularity).
2. **Existence of a measurable cover** (always true in the Carathéodory extension, but not necessarily open).
3. **Inner approximation by closed/compact sets** (requires finite measure or σ-finiteness plus regularity).
Another trap is assuming that the measurable cover is unique as a set, rather than unique up to null sets. The proof of Theorem D clarifies this: any two covers differ by a null set, meaning they represent the same element in the measure algebra (sets modulo null sets). This equivalence relation is fundamental for defining Lp spaces and stochastic processes.

Furthermore, the preservation of σ-finiteness (Theorem E) is not merely a technical detail; it ensures that many powerful theorems (Fubini-Tonelli, Radon-Nikodym) remain applicable after extension. If σ-finiteness were lost, counterexamples abound: e.g., product measures may fail to be uniquely determined, or conditional expectations might not exist. Historically, the focus on σ-finiteness stems from its necessity in proving the completeness of the measure space (adding all subsets of null sets) without introducing pathological behavior.

The genealogy also includes the transition from "content" on a ring (finite additive) to "measure" on a σ-ring (countably additive). Halmos’s work sits at the intersection of functional analysis (via Daniell-Stone integration) and classical real analysis. Understanding this context reveals why the seemingly dry exercises on measurable covers are pivotal: they guarantee that the completed measure space is rich enough to contain all relevant subsets (for probability) yet tame enough to avoid contradictions (like Vitali sets becoming measurable with non-zero measure).
