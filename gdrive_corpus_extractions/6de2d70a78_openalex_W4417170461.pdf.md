The experimental group’s motor-decision gain (p = 0.017) indicates the counter-script improved identification of where and how to respond, but the motor-execution null (p = 1.00) reveals that the counter-script remained dissociated from the physical act of striking [PAGE 9-10].

Three clocks govern the design. (1) Intervention clock: 4 weeks, 16 sessions, home-based, unsupervised [PAGE 3, 7]. (2) Retention clock: 28–31 days post-intervention [PAGE 3], long enough for forgetting or competing seasonal demands to interfere, yet short enough that no formal physical practice intervened [PAGE 3]. (3) Season clock: recruitment and testing straddle the 2020–2021 season onset [PAGE 3], so retention performance may reflect in-season match exposure rather than pure training retention, although the absence of on-field practice during the study partially controls this [PAGE 3].

Verbal report introduces a recursive metacognitive layer: the player must not only decide but articulate the decision (“call out the zone”) [PAGE 5]. The authors note this is artificial for youth players who may lack the technical skill to execute intent, hence verbal reports capture intended rather than realized behavior [PAGE 5]. The interaction for verbal decision is significant (p = 0.009) but the pairwise experimental improvement is not (p = 0.085 after Bonferroni) [PAGE 8-9], suggesting the metacognitive layer either dilutes the trained signal or hits a ceiling/floor effect in this age group. The counter-trap, therefore, is that video training can install a superior decision model without installing the motor policy to execute it; under crisis conditions, the player regresses to the habitual motor policy despite knowing the optimal zone.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and environmental veto proof.

Physical layer. The on-field test was performed indoors under controlled conditions, so meteorological variables (wind, temperature, precipitation) are not operative and constitute a controlled constant rather than a veto. Relevant physical invariants are specified: regulation net height 2.24 m [PAGE 5]; Mikasa V020W size 5 ball [PAGE 5]; match ball speed in excess of 100 km/h [PAGE 3]; 66 ms occlusion before ball contact [PAGE 4], which is below the threshold for conscious deliberation and forces reliance on implicit pickup of advance cues; field sizes from 6 × 4.5 m to 9 × 9 m with 6 or 9 zones [PAGE 4, Table 1]; and 40 trials per participant with no scheduled rest [PAGE 5-6]. The authors report no obvious signs or records of fatigue [PAGE 6], and no other participant was present during an individual’s on-field performance [PAGE 6], eliminating social-distraction confounds.

Biomechanical layer. This is the decisive veto domain. Motor execution requires perception-action coupling: feedforward predictive control and feedback error correction during the actual strike [PAGE 10-11]. Video-based training, by design, uncouples perception from action—the participant presses a keyboard or states a zone rather than striking a ball [PAGE 4-5]. Observational practice alone is insufficient to transfer to motor execution (Hayes et al., 2012; Maslovat et al., 2010; Foster et al., 2023) [PAGE 10-11, refs 24-26]. Consequently, motor execution is vetoed: no significant main or interaction effect, ηp² = 0.04 [PAGE 9-10]. Motor decision survives the veto because identifying “where and how” to respond is a perceptual-cognitive judgment that does not require the effector loop [PAGE 10]. Verbal decision is also vetoed, not by biomechanics but by statistical and ecological artificiality: Bonferroni correction suppresses the pairwise gain (p = 0.085), and verbalizing a motor plan is rarely practiced in actual volleyball [PAGE 8-9, 10]. Far transfer is vetoed by experimental scope: the on-field test replicated the video scenarios exactly (near transfer), so generalization to novel attacking sequences or match play remains untested [PAGE 10-11].

Adjudication summary. Surviving causal claim: video-based training improves and retains near-transfer motor decision in youth volleyball (ηp² = 0.49, pairwise p = 0.017) [PAGE 8-9]. Vetoed claims: motor execution (physical-practice/perception-action-coupling veto), verbal decision (Bonferroni/artificiality veto), and far transfer (design-scope veto). The implied remediation is intermittent physical practice to recouple perception and action [PAGE 10-11].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable reinforcement-learning-with-verifiable-rewards (RLVR) counterfactual veto scenarios and predictions.

Scenario 1 — Retention-interval compression. Counterfactual condition: administer the retention test 7 days after intervention instead of 28–31 days [PAGE 3], holding all other design features identical. Prediction: motor execution remains statistically null (interaction p > 0.05; ηp² < 0.175), because the veto is absence of physical practice/perception-action coupling, not memory decay [PAGE 10-11]. Motor decision should remain significant or strengthen due to reduced forgetting. Verification: run a matched cohort (N = 16, 8 per group) with identical video training and a 7-day retention interval; conduct mixed ANOVA on motor execution. Falsification criterion: if motor execution interaction becomes significant (p < 0.05, ηp² ≥ 0.175) at 7 days but not at 28–31 days, the null is attributable to decay rather than coupling, overturning the current biomechanical veto.

Scenario 2 — Intermittent physical-practice injection. Counterfactual condition: the experimental group adds one on-field session per week (4 sessions total) alongside the 16 video sessions, while the control group remains video-only placebo [PAGE 7]. Prediction: motor execution interaction becomes significant (p < 0.05, ηp² ≥ 0.175) because physical practice re-engages feedforward and feedback processes [PAGE 10-11]; verbal decision remains non-significant after Bonferroni correction due to its artificial metacognitive demands [PAGE 8-9, 10]. Verification: two-group mixed MANOVA on the three on-field measures; planned contrast on motor execution. Falsification criterion: if motor execution remains null despite added physical practice, the uncoupling explanation is insufficient and another motor-learning bottleneck (e.g., insufficient technical volume) dominates.

Scenario 3 — Novel-sequence far transfer. Counterfactual condition: the on-field test uses attacking sequences never presented during video training (novel blocker alignments, non-standard passes, unformed blocks) rather than the exact 6x6 matches used in the study [PAGE 5, 10-11]. Prediction: the experimental group’s motor-decision advantage attenuates to null (group × phase interaction p > 0.05, ηp² near 0), because the current benefit is scenario-matched near transfer [PAGE 10]. Verification: administer novel-sequence on-field pre/post tests to a new sample; compare against the historical near-transfer effect (ηp² = 0.49) [PAGE 8-9]. Falsification criterion: if the experimental group retains a significant motor-decision advantage on novel sequences (p < 0.05, ηp² ≥ 0.175), the training produced abstract decision principles rather than situation-specific scripts, invalidating the near-transfer boundary condition.
