The source is a repeated-measures laboratory study with ten male current or former football players and three pad conditions: no pad, standard pad, and prototype pad. The central measurement is not force, power, or injury risk; it is a maximum reach coordinate in three axes after the trunk and head are restrained. The paper states: “A Three-Way Repeated Measures Analyses of Variance (ANOVA) was performed on the dependent variable maximum reach distance (cm)” [PAGE 4]. The design is therefore a within-subject condition × movement × trial experiment, with movement nested in the protocol and trial used as a reliability check. The first key derivation is that the paper’s “reach distance” is an extremum operator over time, not an average posture: the maximum X, Y, and Z coordinates are extracted for every position, trial, and condition. That matters because a maximum is sensitive to brief excursions, marker visibility, and stabilization quality. The second derivation is that trial was not a significant factor: “The differences in reach distance between the three trials were not statistically significant” [PAGE 4]. The authors then collapsed across trials. This is defensible only if trial order effects, fatigue, and learning are negligible; with three trials and no warm-up, those assumptions are not fully proven, although the reported coefficients of variation of 6.3%, 8.4%, and 8.4% support moderate repeatability [PAGE 5].

The third derivation is the condition effect. The standard pad reduced extension reach in X by 9.0 cm versus no pad, reduced horizontal extension by 11.4 cm, reduced horizontal flexion in Y by about 12.6 cm, reduced flexion in Z by 17.3 cm, and reduced abduction in Z by 25.2 cm [PAGE 4]. The prototype pad was closer to the no-pad condition: in Z flexion the prototype exceeded the standard pad by 10.3 cm, and in Z abduction by 23.1 cm [PAGE 1, PAGE 4]. The strongest summary is: “There were no significant differences found between the no pad condition and the prototype shoulder pad condition for all directions” [PAGE 4]. That is a useful design signal, but it is not proof of on-field catch improvement. The paper itself limits that inference: “On-field performance was not quantified or assessed in this study” [PAGE 6]. A practical threshold is offered because “the diameter of a standard size football is approximately 17 cm,” which is less than the 23.1 cm prototype-minus-standard abduction difference [PAGE 5]. That is a geometric threshold, not a validated catch probability.

The gray-zone audit has several layers. First, the sample is small and homogeneous: ten male players, mean height 183.3 cm and body mass 107.1 kg [PAGE 2]. The paper admits “sample size and sample characteristics must be considered when interpreting and applying the results” [PAGE 5]. Second, the analysis is right-hand only after a three-participant sub-analysis showed no left-right differences [PAGE 6]. Third, the questionnaire was developed for this study and the paper recommends future reliability assessment [PAGE 7]. Fourth, several comfort outcomes are binary counts with exact-looking P values, for example nine of ten versus three of ten for neck-collar discomfort [PAGE 4]. Fifth, the prototype is proprietary: “Images and specific details of the prototype pads cannot be shared at this time due to the proprietary nature of the design in the marketplace” [PAGE 2]. Sixth, the study was static and lacked warm-up: “Since this study was completed using relatively static tasks without a warm-up period, further assessments of shoulder pad breathability during dynamic tasks after warming up, or after play, may provide valuable design-related data” [PAGE 6]. Seventh, measurement uncertainty was not negligible in some comparisons: “The mean uncertainty values in some cases for flexion and horizontal flexion in the X direction were as much as 1 cm” [PAGE 6]. The correct audit conclusion is that the study supports a shoulder-isolated, lab-static reach advantage for the prototype over the standard pad, with aligned subjective comfort, but it does not establish dynamic catch radius, thermal performance in play, or protective noninferiority.

The tracked marker coordinate is \(\mathbf{x}_{i,c,m,t}(s)\in\mathbb{R}^3\) in metres; the paper reports centimetres. The source says: “The maximum value in the X (anterior), Y (medio-lateral), and Z (vertical) directions were determined for every position, trial, and condition” [PAGE 3]. \[
r^{(d)}_{i,c,m,t}(s)=\mathbf{e}_d^{\top}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i,c,m,t}(s)-\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ref}}\right),
\]

\[
R^{(d)}_{i,c,m,t}=\max_{s\in[0,T_{i,c,m,t}]} r^{(d)}_{i,c,m,t}(s).
\]

Units: \(R\) is in metres, or centimetres after scaling. The Z coordinate is taken relative to the ground, while X and Y are relative to the stabilizing-apparatus marker. The maximum operator is not a mean posture; it is a reach-envelope extremum. Its breakdown regimes include marker occlusion, camera synchronization failure, calibration drift, and any head or trunk motion that changes the reference frame. The paper claims “the participants’ heads did not move in any direction during the reach distance protocol” [PAGE 3], but no numerical tolerance is reported.

The condition effect is the mean contrast

\[
\Delta_{c_1,c_2}^{(d,m)}=\bar R_{c_1}^{(d,m)}-\bar R_{c_2}^{(d,m)},
\]

with units of cm. A confidence interval at level \(1-\alpha\) is

\[
\mathrm{CI}_{1-\alpha}\left(\Delta_{c_1,c_2}^{(d,m)}\right)
=
\Delta_{c_1,c_2}^{(d,m)}
\pm
t_{1-\alpha/2,\nu}\,
\frac{s_{\Delta}}{\sqrt{n}},
\]

where \(s_{\Delta}\) is the sample standard deviation of within-participant differences in cm, \(n\) is the number of paired observations, and \(\nu\) is the appropriate degrees of freedom. The standardized within-participant effect is

\[
d_z=\frac{\bar\Delta}{s_\Delta},
\]

which is dimensionless. Its singularity is exact when \(s_\Delta=0\) or when \(n=1\). The paper reports several mean differences and P values, but not full confidence intervals or \(d_z\): examples include 9.0 cm for no-pad minus standard in X extension, 7.0 cm for prototype minus standard in X extension, 11.4 cm for no-pad minus standard in horizontal extension, 12.6 cm for no-pad minus standard in Y horizontal flexion, 17.3 cm for no-pad minus standard in Z flexion, 10.3 cm for prototype minus standard in Z flexion, 4.7 cm for prototype minus standard in Z extension, and 25.2 cm and 23.1 cm for no-pad and prototype versus standard in Z abduction, respectively [PAGE 4]. These are geometric reach contrasts, not probabilities of catching.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage here runs from protective-equipment research dominated by impact attenuation toward performance and comfort evaluation. The paper states that the three main focuses are protective capability, player performance, and comfort and fit, but that force attenuation “has dominated this field of study to date” [PAGE 1]. The immediate ancestors are Frayne et al. on hockey goalie reach envelopes and Virani et al. on hockey shoulder-pad comfort. The paper criticizes the Frayne protocol because “participants were sitting while they completed the reach envelope protocol, and their trunk was not secured” [PAGE 1]. That criticism is the retrocausal hinge: if trunk motion is unconstrained, a pad effect can be masked or confounded by trunk compensation. The present study’s stabilizing apparatus is therefore designed to force the measured reach to be shoulder-joint-dominated. The paper says: “A stabilizing apparatus was utilized that limited head and trunk movement (Figure 1) to isolate the contribution of the shoulder joint to players’ reach distances during the reach distance protocol” [PAGE 2].

The design lineage also includes comfort-compliance research. Brisbine et al. is cited for the claim that players may opt out of recommended equipment if it is uncomfortable or not breathable [PAGE 2], and the discussion states that “athletes will opt out of wearing important pieces of safety equipment because they feel that the equipment negatively impacts performance (i.e., is too restrictive)” [PAGE 5]. This creates a causal chain: pad geometry affects reach and discomfort; discomfort affects compliance; compliance affects real-world protection; therefore a comfort or reach improvement cannot be evaluated in isolation from protection.

The multi-week traps are identifiable. Trap one: treating a static lab reach extreme as a direct proxy for game catch radius. The paper warns that “On-field performance was not quantified or assessed in this study” [PAGE 6]. Trap two: ignoring the protection trade-off. The prototype’s most common dislike was that “participants were worried that these pads would not provide as much protection as the standard pad they were used to wearing” [PAGE 6], and the paper states that “How improved range of motion during typical use interacts with the protective capabilities of shoulder pads remains to be determined” [PAGE 6]. Trap three: overgeneralizing from ten male players to all football positions, sizes, and levels. Trap four: treating an unvalidated comfort questionnaire as a calibrated instrument; the paper itself recommends future assessment of questionnaire reliability [PAGE 7]. Trap five: ignoring multiple endpoints across movements, axes, and conditions. Trap six: treating the proprietary prototype as a reproducible design when its images and details cannot be shared [PAGE 2]. Trap seven: omitting dynamic warm-up, sweat, heat, and repeated-play effects. The paper explicitly notes that static tasks without warm-up limit breathability inference [PAGE 6]. These traps are not speculative; they are stated limitations or direct consequences of the published design.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The player’s adoption decision is recursive because it depends on the player’s own comfort, the player’s belief about protection, the player’s belief about what coaches and medical staff will approve, and the player’s belief about how peers or opponents will perceive the equipment. A compact formal representation is

\[
U_i(c)
=
\lambda_P \hat P_i(c)
+
\lambda_R \hat R_i(c)
+
\lambda_C \hat C_i(c)
+
\lambda_F \hat F_i(c)
+
\lambda_S \hat S_i(c)
+
\epsilon_{i,c},
\]

where \(\hat P_i(c)\) is perceived protection, \(\hat R_i(c)\) is perceived reach or non-restrictiveness, \(\hat C_i(c)\) is comfort, \(\hat F_i(c)\) is familiarity, and \(\hat S_i(c)\) is perceived social or staff approval. The weights are not measured in the paper. The source gives qualitative evidence for the terms: the prototype was “not restrictive” for eight participants, “comfortable” for five, and “light” for four, while standard-pad dislikes included “Neck Collar Tight” for five, “Bulky” for four, “Restrictive” for four, and “Uncomfortable” for four [PAGE 5, PAGE 6]. The recursive layer appears in the protection worry: the prototype’s most common dislike was “Worried Pads are Not as Protective,” reported by five participants [PAGE 6]. A player who expects coaches or medical staff to prioritize protection may discount the reach benefit even if the player personally feels less restricted.

Crisis habit regression is the predicted shift under fatigue, heat, fear of injury, or high cognitive load: the familiarity weight \(\lambda_F\) rises and the reach weight \(\lambda_R\) falls, so the player reverts to the familiar standard pad despite measured reach loss. The source cannot test this directly, but it is consistent with the compliance literature cited by the authors. The counter-trap is optimizing comfort or reach alone: a pad that improves reach and breathability but reduces impact attenuation could increase net risk if players wear it more often but are less protected per impact. The paper’s own restraint is decisive: “How improved range of motion during typical use interacts with the protective capabilities of shoulder pads remains to be determined” [PAGE 6]. The clock layer is time: the lab measures a short static protocol, while players experience warm-up, sweat, repeated overhead reaching, and pad migration over a practice or game. The paper notes that the standard pad’s “relatively stiff shell” may have shifted upward to the neck during overhead reach [PAGE 6], which is a time- and movement-dependent fit failure. A single static snapshot cannot resolve whether the prototype remains comfortable after thirty minutes of sweating or whether its holes improve breathability under real heat. The correct recursive conclusion is that player perception is not a simple preference score; it is a belief-weighted adoption model with a protection counter-trap and a duration clock. The study measures some inputs to that model, but not the full decision or its long-horizon dynamics.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer is partially adjudicated by the football-diameter comparison. If the measured prototype-minus-standard reach difference in Z abduction is 23.1 cm and a standard football diameter is about 17 cm, then the geometric reach envelope changes by more than one ball diameter in that direction [PAGE 5]. That supports a physical plausibility argument for catch or deflection geometry, but it does not prove a dynamic catch. The veto condition is

\[
V_{\mathrm{catch}}
=
\mathbf{1}\left\{
\Delta R_{Z,\mathrm{abd}}^{\mathrm{dynamic}}<D_{\mathrm{ball}}
\right\},
\]

with \(D_{\mathrm{ball}}\approx0.17\ \mathrm{m}\). If a free-trunk, dynamic catch-radius test shows a difference smaller than the ball diameter, or a confidence interval that includes zero, then the static practical claim is vetoed. The source already states that “On-field performance was not quantified or assessed in this study” [PAGE 6], so the physical layer is not closed.

The biomechanical layer is governed by the stabilizing apparatus. The inference that reach differences are shoulder-joint-specific depends on the claim that “the participants’ heads did not move in any direction during the reach distance protocol” [PAGE 3] and that the trunk was restrained. A formal veto is

\[
V_{\mathrm{trunk}}
=
\mathbf{1}\left\{
\max_t\left\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{head}}(t)-\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{head}}(0)\right\|>\epsilon_h
\;\lor\;
\max_t\left\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{trunk}}(t)-\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{trunk}}(0)\right\|>\epsilon_t
\right\},
\]

where \(\epsilon_h\) and \(\epsilon_t\) are pre-registered tolerances in metres. The paper does not report numerical tolerances, so the veto is only partially proven. If the head strap loosens, the hip strap slips, or the participant learns to recruit trunk motion, the shoulder-isolation claim breaks. The standard pad’s reported upward shell migration into the neck during overhead reach is another biomechanical veto signal: it suggests that fit is not static and that the pad can change position under movement [PAGE 6]. The prototype’s flexibility may reduce that migration, but the paper does not quantify pad displacement.

The weather and thermal layer is not adjudicated at all. The paper measured breathability by questionnaire and reported that the prototype was “significantly more breathable” [PAGE 4], with a proposed mechanism: “many holes in the material of the prototype shoulder pad that the standard shoulder pad did not have” [PAGE 6]. But no ambient temperature, relative humidity, WBGT, skin temperature, microclimate humidity, sweat rate, or airflow was reported. The study admits that it used “relatively static tasks without a warm-up period” and recommends future dynamic assessment after warming up or after play [PAGE 6]. A thermal veto is

\[
V_{\mathrm{thermal}}
=
\mathbf{1}\left\{
\Delta T_{\mathrm{micro}}\ge0
\;\land\;
\Delta \mathrm{RH}_{\mathrm{micro}}\ge0
\right\},
\]

where \(T_{\mathrm{micro}}\) is microclimate temperature in degrees Celsius and \(\mathrm{RH}_{\mathrm{micro}}\) is relative humidity in percent. If the prototype fails to lower microclimate temperature or humidity during intermittent exercise in heat, the breathability advantage is vetoed as a play-condition claim. Finally, the protective layer is explicitly unresolved: “How improved range of motion during typical use interacts with the protective capabilities of shoulder pads remains to be determined” [PAGE 6]. Any net-safety claim requires impact attenuation testing under standardized shoulder impacts; without it, the cross-layer adjudication permits a reach-and-comfort claim only, not a protection claim.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
For each counterfactual, the verifiable reward is a pre-registered endpoint that can be recomputed from raw data. The veto fires when the endpoint crosses the stated boundary. This is not a subjective review; it is a falsification test.

Scenario 1: dynamic free-trunk catch-radius counterfactual. Design: randomized crossover with the same three conditions, but the trunk is free, the task is a dynamic overhead catch or deflection, and three-dimensional motion capture measures hand reach at the moment of ball contact. The primary endpoint is \(\Delta R_{Z,\mathrm{abd}}^{\mathrm{dynamic}}\) in cm, with a 95% confidence interval. Prediction: the prototype will remain less restrictive than the standard pad, but the advantage will shrink relative to the static 23.1 cm because trunk compensation and task dynamics add variance. Veto prediction: if the 95% confidence interval for prototype minus standard includes zero, or if its upper bound is below the 17 cm football-diameter threshold, then the static catch-geometry claim is vetoed. The paper’s own words support this test: “On-field performance was not quantified or assessed in this study” [PAGE 6].

Scenario 2: protective noninferiority counterfactual. Design: standardized shoulder-impact testing with the standard and prototype pads, measuring peak linear acceleration in \(g\) and peak rotational acceleration in \(\mathrm{rad}/\mathrm{s}^2\), with a pre-registered noninferiority margin. Prediction: the prototype’s greater flexibility and reduced bulk may improve reach but may or may not preserve impact attenuation. Veto prediction: if the prototype exceeds the noninferiority margin for either peak linear or peak rotational acceleration, then any claim that the prototype is a net performance improvement is vetoed. The source explicitly leaves this unresolved: “How improved range of motion during typical use interacts with the protective capabilities of shoulder pads remains to be determined” [PAGE 6]. This scenario is verifiable because the raw acceleration time series and the pre-registered margin determine the binary outcome.

Scenario 3: thermal and fit-duration counterfactual. Design: intermittent exercise in an environmental chamber at a fixed dry-bulb temperature and relative humidity, with warm-up and at least thirty minutes of repeated overhead reaching. Endpoints: microclimate temperature in degrees Celsius, microclimate relative humidity in percent, skin temperature, sweat rate, and pad displacement in cm. Prediction: the prototype’s holes will lower microclimate humidity and temperature relative to the standard pad, but the advantage may attenuate as sweat accumulates and fabric wets out. Veto prediction: if the prototype shows no improvement in microclimate humidity or temperature, or if it shows greater pad migration into the neck than the standard pad, then the breathability-and-fit claim is vetoed for play conditions. The paper’s static limitation is the direct justification: “Since this study was completed using relatively static tasks without a warm-up period, further assessments of shoulder pad breathability during dynamic tasks after warming up, or after play, may provide valuable design-related data” [PAGE 6]. Each scenario yields a recomputable reward: pass, fail, or indeterminate due to missing calibration.
