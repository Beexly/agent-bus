Counter-trap: an opposing tactical pattern can exploit this habit. Dumping the puck into board-occluded bottom halves of the defensive or offensive zones—where accuracy is below 60% [PAGE 5]—creates a region where the model's visual branch receives no signal and its attention branch may be misled by player positions away from the puck. Similarly, during line changes or neutral-zone traps, players congregate away from the puck; the attention module then biases localization toward empty ice, corrupting both the puck output and the event head, because Fo is shared between tasks [PAGE 4][PAGE 5]. The Advance event, which is already confused with Play and Shot (F1 = 41.9) [PAGE 7], becomes a gradient-conflict source in L_multi, potentially dragging localization features away from accurate puck estimates.

Clock: the network's temporal clock is rigidly bounded by l = 2 s and s = 1 s [PAGE 6]. Crisis events such as breakaways, shots, or rapid turnovers can evolve within or below this window. Because the model was trained on trimmed 2 s clips with approximate annotations, it lacks sub-clip temporal resolution; at s = 0.125 s the trajectory is "extremely noisy," while s = 1 s gives the most accurate but temporally coarse estimates [PAGE 6][Fig. 9]. Inference at 5 fps [PAGE 6] imposes a 200 ms minimum update period, which is slower than puck flight time across the rink at high velocity. Thus the model's internal clock cannot resolve fast state transitions, and recursive theory of mind must treat the network as a lagging estimator that regresses to context when the clock ticks faster than its receptive field.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto: hockey pucks are small, travel at high velocity, and are occluded by players and opaque boards [PAGE 1]. The rink boards are physically opaque; when the puck is behind the boards in the bottom halves of the defensive and offensive zones, no visual information reaches the broadcast camera [PAGE 5]. Any hard localization output in those zones is physically unverifiable and must be vetoed or rendered as a high-entropy distribution rather than a point estimate.

Biomechanical veto: player detection runs on the middle frame only, assuming player locations remain approximately constant over 2 s [PAGE 4]. Body checks, line changes, and rapid transitions violate this stationarity. If detected player displacement within a clip exceeds the heatmap σ_p (tested at 15, 20, 25 ft/pixel equivalents; σ_p = 15 performed best [PAGE 7][Table 5]), the player branch output becomes a decoy, and the attention mechanism's Hadamard product \(F_o = F_a \otimes F_v + F_v\) propagates the error into shared features [PAGE 4]. A veto should suppress player-branch gain when optical-flow or motion-energy in the video branch contradicts player-heatmap concentration.

Weather/environmental veto: indoor arena lighting, broadcast compression, and ice-resurfacing cycles alter exposure and motion blur. The paper explicitly cites motion blur due to high puck velocity as a core challenge [PAGE 1]. Because no explicit blur or exposure model is in the architecture, frames exceeding a motion-blur threshold must trigger veto of frame-level puck inference; temporal context alone cannot recover location when the optical signal is destroyed at the sensor.

Adjudication rule: the current architecture produces \(p_w \in \mathbb{R}^{200}\) and \(p_h \in \mathbb{R}^{85}\) but has no learned abstention or confidence-veto layer. Cross-layer veto requires that if (a) board occlusion is detected via depth or board segmentation, (b) player-heatmap entropy exceeds a stationarity threshold, or (c) frame motion-blur exceeds backbone tolerance, then the soft spatial outputs are flagged as low-confidence and the event head should not condition its gradients on those features. Without such a veto, the multi-task loss \(L_{\text{multi}}\) forces the event head to consume corrupted puck-location signals, degrading Faceoff and Advance F1 [PAGE 7][Table 7].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Scenario 1 — Board occlusion counterfactual: Suppose the puck is physically behind the opaque boards in the bottom half of the offensive zone during a dump-in (Advance event). Verifiable condition: camera line-of-sight to the puck is blocked by the boards; ground-truth annotation places puck in occluded zone. Veto rule: the model must output diffuse probability over the board region or abstain; it may not emit a high-confidence point estimate in open ice. Prediction: under veto enforcement, localization AUC in bottom-half zones remains below the observed <60% [PAGE 5], and Advance F1 stays below 0.5 [PAGE 7][Table 7]; failure to veto produces confidently wrong coordinates and cascades into incorrect Advance event classification.

Scenario 2 — Player-density decoy counterfactual: Suppose players screen near the boards while the puck is actually in the neutral zone on an Advance play. Verifiable condition: Faster R-CNN detects high player density in one zone, but video-branch motion energy indicates puck in another. Veto rule: when player-heatmap attention and video-branch motion saliency disagree beyond a learned threshold, suppress the player-branch contribution to \(F_o\) [PAGE 4]. Prediction: adversarially shifting the player heatmap should reduce overall AUC by more than the 3.2% drop observed when removing random frame sampling [PAGE 7][Table 6], and event-recognition F1 for Advance should decline further below 41.9 because the shared features are corrupted by a false spatial prior.

Scenario 3 — Temporal aliasing counterfactual: Suppose a slap shot carries the puck from the neutral zone into the offensive zone within 0.4 s. Verifiable condition: inter-window puck displacement exceeds the tolerance t = 5 ft while the sliding-window stride remains s = 1 s. Veto rule: if estimated puck speed implies displacement > t between consecutive windows, the output is flagged as temporally aliased; the system must either reduce stride or mark the sequence unreliable. Prediction: at s = 0.125 s the trajectory is already "extremely noisy" [PAGE 6][Fig. 9], and at s = 1 s fast events are undersampled; enforcing a veto below an effective temporal Nyquist rate should reduce false-positive Shot and Faceoff detections while increasing abstention rate, preserving AUC but lowering raw coverage on untrimmed video [PAGE 6].
