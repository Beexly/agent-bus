1. Probability calibration and Brier/QPS. For binary outcome \(Y_t\in\{0,1\}\) and forecast probability \(p_t\in[0,1]\),
\[
\mathrm{QPS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}(p_t-Y_t)^2,
\qquad \mathrm{QPS}\in[0,1].
\]
Units: probability squared, dimensionless. The source states: “The quadratic probability score (QPS) as also known as the Brier Score can be used to evaluate probability forecasts when the relative frequencies of the outcomes are known” [PAGE 7]. Calibration is
\[
\mathbb{E}[Y_t\mid p_t=p]=p,
\]
with units of probability. For binned calibration, \(C_k=\hat f_k-p_k\), where \(\hat f_k\) is the ex post relative frequency in bin \(k\). Singularity/breakdown: if a bin has \(n_k=0\), \(\hat f_k\) is undefined; if \(p_t=0\) or \(1\) in small samples, calibration is unstable; if the outcome coding is altered, QPS changes meaning. The log score has a singularity at \(p_t=0\) when \(Y_t=1\); QPS remains finite under \(p_t,Y_t\in[0,1]\).

2. Unbiasedness regression. The source gives
\[
A=a+bF+e,
\]
where \(A\) is actual value and \(F\) is forecast [PAGE 12]. Units: \(A,F,e\) in points, runs, or goals depending on sport; \(a\) in the same units; \(b\) dimensionless. The null is \(H_0:a=0,\ b=1\). Breakdown regimes: \(\operatorname{Var}(F)=0\) makes \(b\) unidentified; perfect collinearity or missing regressors bias \(a,b\); heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation invalidate ordinary standard errors; structural breaks change the mapping from \(F\) to \(A\). The source’s own caution is that “the explanatory power of the equation is usually low, indicating that there is considerable unexplained variation” [PAGE 12]. Low \(R^2\) is not by itself evidence of bias.

3. Score-difference regression. The source’s second regression uses the same intercept-slope null for actual score difference against the market’s score-difference forecast [PAGE 12]. I relabel the source’s score-difference forecast as \(S_t\) to avoid importing domain-specific notation:
\[
D_t=a+bS_t+e_t,
\qquad H_0:a=0,\ b=1.
\]
Units: \(D_t,S_t,e_t\) in points; \(a\) in points; \(b\) dimensionless. Breakdown: if \(S_t\) is stale relative to \(D_t\), \(b\) is attenuated or shifted; if \(S_t\) is measured with error, \(b\) is attenuated; if physical state changes after \(S_t\) is formed, the residual contains an omitted layer.

4. Market aggregation as a belief fixed point. Let \(p_i\) be forecaster \(i\)’s probability and \(w_i\) a proper weight. A log-loss aggregator solves
\[
p^*=\arg\min_q \sum_i w_i\,\ell(q,p_i),
\qquad
\ell(q,p_i)=-p_i\log q-(1-p_i)\log(1-q),
\]
which yields \(p^*=\sum_i w_i p_i\) under standard conditions. Units: probability. Breakdown: if weights are biased, if information is not common, if some agents have private state, or if the market is nonstationary, the fixed point is not the true probability. The source’s claim that “the market price is the best predictor of the event because the market aggregates all the information that is relevant to the event” [PAGE 27] is therefore conditional on relevant information actually entering the aggregation.

\[
Y_t\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_t),
\qquad
\log\lambda_t=\mu+\alpha_{\text{home}}+\beta_{\text{attack},i}+\gamma_{\text{defense},j}+\delta t.
\]
Units: \(Y_t\) goals per match; \(\lambda_t\) goals per match; \(\mu,\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\) dimensionless log-scale parameters. Breakdown: overdispersion requires negative-binomial extension; zero-inflation or structural breaks violate the Poisson mean-variance relation; nonstationary team strength requires dynamic updating, as the source notes: “Since the abilities and performance of teams can change over time, some models have become dynamic to capture these effects” [PAGE 21].

6. Power-score operator. A latent-strength model can be written
\[
\widehat D_{ij}=\theta_i-\theta_j+\eta H_{ij},
\]
where \(\widehat D_{ij}\) is predicted score difference in points or goals, \(\theta_i,\theta_j\) are latent team strengths, and \(H_{ij}\) is a home indicator. Units: points or goals for \(\widehat D,\theta\); \(\eta\) in points or goals; \(H\) dimensionless. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive problem is that forecasters forecast other forecasters’ forecasts. Let \(B_i^{(k)}(p)\) be agent \(i\)’s \(k\)-th order belief about probability \(p\), and let \(M\) be the market-implied probability. A simple recursive updating scheme is
\[
B_i^{(k+1)}
=
\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\!\left(
\alpha_i B_i^{(k)}
+
(1-\alpha_i)M
+
\beta_i R_t
\right),
\]
where \(R_t\) is recent outcome information, \(\alpha_i\in[0,1]\) is an anchoring weight, and \(\beta_i\) is a recency-loading coefficient. Units: probabilities for \(B,M\); \(\alpha,\beta\) dimensionless. This is not a claim that the source estimated this equation; it is a compact way to represent the source’s behavioral evidence. Under crisis, \(\beta_i\) rises: agents overweight the latest result, producing hot-hand and recency errors [PAGE 15][PAGE 16]. Under high uncertainty, \(\alpha_i\) rises: agents anchor on prior beliefs and underweight new information. The source notes that the majority of evidence favors overreaction, but also reports situations where recent information is given too little weight [PAGE 26]. So the recursive map can land in either failure mode.

Crisis habit regression is the first trap. When uncertainty is high, forecasters regress to simple habits: “the team won big, so it will win again,” “the high seed always wins,” “the expert must know.” The source’s hot-hand discussion [PAGE 16] and overreaction discussion [PAGE 15] are evidence of habit regression. The counter-trap is to overcorrect against the market. If a model disagrees with the market, the analyst may assume the market is biased and the model is informed. But the source says “There is no evidence that either statistical systems or experts consistently outperform the market” [PAGE 27], and it reports model/expert parity in football [PAGE 26]. The counter-trap is therefore to fade the aggregate without out-of-sample calibration. The disciplined counter is combination: “combining forecasts usually improves accuracy” [PAGE 17]. That does not mean equal weights; it means testing whether the model adds information after the market is included.

The clock is the third operator. Every forecast must have an ex ante timestamp, and evaluation must respect that timestamp. Opening quotations are less accurate than closing quotations [PAGE 13], so mixing them creates look-ahead bias. Dynamic models are needed because team ability changes [PAGE 21], but the update clock must be fixed before outcomes are observed. The recursive theory of mind therefore has three adjudication rules: (1) crisis habit regression is vetoed when the forecast is merely recency or hot-hand extrapolation; (2) counter-trap is vetoed when the model claims market-beating skill without out-of-sample calibration and bias testing; (3) clock violation is vetoed when opening, closing, and outcome information are not separated. The market is a fixed point of many beliefs, but it is not omniscient; the model is an information source, but it is not automatically superior. The correct recursive stance is calibrated humility: combine, timestamp, and test.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is the claim that physical, biomechanical, or weather state can invalidate a forecast even when the market, model, and experts agree. The source does not measure wind speed, precipitation, ground reaction force, or joint load, so this is an external layer, not a source-reported operator. The formal proof is an omitted-variable argument. Suppose the true score difference is
\[
D_t=\alpha+\beta S_t+\gamma W_t+u_t,
\]
where \(S_t\) is the market score-difference forecast in points, \(W_t\) is an unmodeled physical/environmental vector, and \(u_t\) is noise. Units: \(D_t,S_t,u_t\) in points; \(W_t\) in SI units such as m/s for wind, mm/h for precipitation, °C for temperature, N for ground reaction force, and N·m for joint load; \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) carry units that make the equation dimensionally consistent. If the analyst estimates \(D_t=a+bS_t+e_t\), the linear-projection slope is
\[
b=\beta+\gamma\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(S_t,W_t)}{\operatorname{Var}(S_t)},
\qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_t)>0.
\]
The intercept is
\[
a=\alpha+\gamma\mathbb{E}[W_t]-(b-\beta)\mathbb{E}[S_t].
\]
Proof: substitute the true equation into the projection of \(D_t\) on \(S_t\); the projection error contains \(\gamma W_t\), and the covariance term follows directly. Veto conditions are exact: if \(\operatorname{Var}(S_t)=0\), \(b\) is unidentified; if \(\gamma\neq0\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(S_t,W_t)\neq0\), the regression is biased and calibration fails; if \(\gamma\neq0\) but \(\operatorname{Cov}(S_t,W_t)=0\), the slope is unbiased but residual variance is inflated by \(\gamma^2\operatorname{Var}(W_t)\), so QPS and confidence intervals are miscalibrated. If \(W_t\) changes regime within the forecast horizon, a static model is stale even if its historical calibration was good.

Physical veto examples: wind speed changes projectile motion and passing accuracy; precipitation changes traction and handling; temperature changes ball elasticity and fatigue; biomechanical load changes injury risk and sprint output. The source’s dynamic-model warning applies: “Since the abilities and performance of teams can change over time, some models have become dynamic to capture these effects” [PAGE 21]. But ability change is only one layer. If \(W_t\) is private and not aggregated, the market-aggregation claim on [PAGE 27] does not rescue the forecast, because the relevant information never entered the aggregate. If \(W_t\) is public but the model omits it, the model is structurally misspecified. If \(W_t\) is measured after the forecast timestamp, using it is a clock violation. Therefore the cross-layer veto is not a refutation of the source; it is a boundary condition on the source’s market-aggregation and dynamic-model operators. The veto is proof-based: omitted physical state either biases the slope, inflates the residual, or breaks the calibration map. A forecast that ignores the layer can be accurate on average and still fail catastrophically in the tail.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
