**1. POMAC game tuple.** The problem is “a Partially Observable Multi-Agent Cooperative Game” governed by \(\langle I,S,O_I,A_I,R_I,T,Z\rangle\), with \(o_{i,t}=o_{i,t}(s_t)\), joint observation \(\vec{o}_t=\langle o_{1,t},\dots,o_{I,t}\rangle\), joint action \(\vec{a}_t=\langle a_{1,t},\dots,a_{I,t}\rangle\), transition \(T(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,\vec{a}_t)\), and observation function \(Z(\vec{o}_{t+1}\mid s_{t+1},\vec{a}_t)\) [PAGE 3]. Units: \(S\) and \(O\) are raw-pixel tensors plus pose vectors (pixels; meters; degrees); \(A\) is a discrete set of eleven actions [PAGE 5]; \(R\) is dimensionless. Assumption: the environment is a true POMDP with stationary transition and observation kernels. Breakdown: if the simulator’s rendering pipeline is non-stationary across episodes (e.g., texture randomization changes the observation kernel \(Z\)), the POMDP assumption fails and the learned policy is not identifiable across episodes.

**2. Global objective.** The cooperative goal is
\[
\max_{\pi_1,\dots,\pi_I}\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi_1,\dots,\pi_I}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R_{i,t}\right] \tag{1}
\]
[PAGE 3]. Units: \(R_{i,t}\) dimensionless; \(T\) in frames; \(I\) a count. Assumption: common-payoff cooperation, no adversarial agents. Breakdown: the sum is unweighted, so a camera with a permanently unreachable target contributes a constant negative offset that cannot be improved; the gradient signal for that camera vanishes and the optimizer reallocates capacity to easier cameras.

**3. Direction reward (Eq. 2).** With \(\alpha,\beta,\lambda\) the pitch, yaw, and roll Euler angles [PAGE 4],
\[
R_{d,t}=
\begin{cases}
1-\dfrac{\Delta\alpha_t}{\alpha_{\max}}-\dfrac{\Delta\beta_t}{\beta_{\max}}, & \text{(a) target visible},\\[1.2ex]
0, & \text{(b) target occluded},\\[0.8ex]
-1, & \text{(c) target outside view},
\end{cases} \tag{2}
\]
where \(\Delta\alpha_t,\Delta\beta_t\) are absolute pitch and yaw errors in degrees and \(\alpha_{\max},\beta_{\max}\) are the maximum control bounds in the same units. Assumption: \(\alpha_{\max},\beta_{\max}>0\) and the two angular errors are additively separable. Singularity: \(\alpha_{\max}\to0\) or \(\beta_{\max}\to0\) makes the ratio diverge. Breakdown regime: for \(\Delta\alpha_t/\alpha_{\max}+\Delta\beta_t/\beta_{\max}\ge2\), the pre-clip reward is \(\le -1\), colliding with case (c); the piecewise definition is therefore not injective on the visible set.

\]
Units: \(\xi\) and \(\Delta\xi_t\) dimensionless; \(R_t\) dimensionless. Assumption: \(\xi_{\max}\) is the maximum admissible scale error, so \(R_{z,t}\in[0,1]\). Pre-clip range: \(R_t\in[-1,2]\). Breakdown: clipping is non-injective on \(R_t\le -1\); all states in that tail receive identical zero gradient, so the policy cannot distinguish “slightly outside view” from “catastrophically misaligned.”

\text{Mean Error}=\frac{1}{I}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\Delta\alpha_{i,t}+\Delta\beta_{i,t}}{2} \tag{4}
\]
[PAGE 6]. Units: degrees. Assumption: pitch and yaw errors are equally weighted and commensurable. Breakdown: the metric is unbounded above (a camera can accumulate \(\Delta\alpha\to180^\circ\)); it is undefined for \(I=0\) or \(T=0\); and equal weighting is unjustified for anisotropic fields of view, since a wide-FOV camera tolerates larger yaw error at fixed image displacement.

\text{Success Rate}=\frac{1}{I}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{i,t},\qquad D_{i,t}\in\{0,1\},
\]
with \(D_{i,t}=1\) iff the target is within camera \(i\)’s perspective [PAGE 6]. Units: dimensionless fraction in \([0,1]\). Breakdown: the indicator ignores centering and angular error, so it is gameable by a policy that keeps the target barely inside the frame while failing Eq. (4).

The third strand is end-to-end reinforcement-learning active tracking: Luo et al. 2018/2019 and Zhong et al.’s AD-VAT/AD-VAT+ [PAGE 8] replaced the two-stage detect-then-control pipeline with a Conv-LSTM policy trained in augmented virtual environments.

The pose-sharing design also anticipates later communication-efficient multi-agent RL, where agents exchange compact messages rather than raw observations; the paper’s claim that “our method only needs to share the poses and the conditions of switcher” [PAGE 2] is an early statement of the low-bandwidth coordination thesis.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The system implements a shallow recursive theory of mind with three levels. Level 0 is the target’s latent state. Level 1 is the camera’s self-model: the switcher estimates \(P(g_i=1\mid\psi_{i,t})\), i.e., “am I tracking?” Level 2 is the camera’s model of others: the pose encoder \(f_p(l_{1:I},g_{1:I})\) estimates “where are the successful cameras pointing?” The paper does not implement level 3 — no camera models another camera’s model of itself — so the recursion depth is two. This is sufficient for behavior consistency but insufficient for adversarial or correlated failure.

**Crisis habit regression.** When occlusion occurs, the system enters a crisis and regresses to its habit: the pose-based controller’s learned prior over the consensus pose. Because the switcher is a hard binary gate, the regression is discrete, not graded; there is no blended policy that gradually decreases trust in vision as the target becomes partially occluded. The habit is also temporally sticky: the LSTM/GRU state \(\psi_{i,t}\) carries history, so once the switcher flips to pose mode, it may remain there for several frames after the target reappears. Define the switching latency \(L_s\) as the number of frames between occlusion onset and \(g_i\) flipping to 0, and the recovery latency \(L_r\) as the number of frames between reappearance and \(g_i\) returning to 1. The paper reports neither. In a crisis, \(L_s\) and \(L_r\) determine whether the habit helps or harms; a long \(L_r\) means the camera keeps pointing at the old consensus while the target has moved.

**Counter-trap.** The recursive trust structure creates a counter-trap: camera \(i\) trusts camera \(j\)’s \(g_j=1\), but \(g_j\) is itself a classifier output, and camera \(j\) may be confidently wrong. Because the Bi-GRU aggregates all labels and poses, a single false positive can pull the consensus toward a wrong pose, and the failed camera will rotate toward it. The trap is self-reinforcing if the wrong pose happens to improve the false-positive camera’s own apparent alignment, creating a feedback loop. The counter-measure is not more recursion but robust aggregation: a trimmed mean or median over the poses of cameras with \(g=1\), plus a calibrated switcher with an abstention region, would bound the influence of any single label. The paper’s learned Bi-GRU has no such guarantee; it is a soft attention mechanism without an outlier rejection proof.

**Clock.** The clock operates at three scales. At the frame scale, the LSTM/GRU introduce temporal credit assignment: the reward at time \(t\) depends on actions taken several frames earlier, and the paper’s fixed episode length \(T\) means late recovery is heavily penalized by the time average in Eq. (4) and the Success Rate. At the training scale, phase one’s “10 \(\sim\)15 minutes” [PAGE 5] is a clock-stamped artifact: the pose controller is frozen at that moment and cannot adapt to the switcher’s evolving distribution in phase two. At the evaluation scale, the reported averages hide the per-frame switching dynamics; a system with 85% success could be succeeding in long stable stretches and failing catastrophically in short crisis windows, and the aggregate cannot distinguish these. The recursive theory of mind is therefore temporally shallow: it models others at the current step but not their trajectories, and it models itself through a sticky recurrent state whose latency is unmeasured.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is a condition under which a lower-level physical constraint overrides the learned policy and invalidates the reported metric as a measure of tracking competence. Three layers are relevant.

**Physical/actuator veto.** The POMAC transition \(T(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,\vec{a}_t)\) contains no actuator dynamics. Real PTZ cameras have finite maximum angular velocity \(\omega_{\max}\) (deg/s), finite angular acceleration, and communication/actuation latency \(\tau\) (s). If the required correction satisfies \(|\Delta\alpha|/\Delta t>\omega_{\max}\) or \(|\Delta\beta|/\Delta t>\omega_{\max}\), the motor saturates and the executed rotation is smaller than commanded. The reward in Eq. (2) then attributes the residual error to policy error, but the error is hardware-induced. This is a model-reality gap: the learned policy is optimal for a fictitious plant with infinite slew rate. A second physical veto is optical: the pose vector omits focal length and zoom scale, so the mapping from commanded angle to image displacement is state-dependent; at \(\xi=3.3\) the same angular command produces a different pixel displacement than at \(\xi=1\), and the pose controller cannot compensate because it never observes \(\xi\).

**Biomechanical veto.** The target is a walking person, but the simulator uses “random velocity and trajectories” [PAGE 5], not a gait model. Human walking has a stride frequency near 1.8 Hz, a preferred speed near 1.4 m/s, nonholonomic turning limits, and self-occlusion of limbs and face. A policy trained on white-noise trajectories can over-react to direction reversals that a real pedestrian cannot execute, and can under-react to the periodic vertical bob that a real gait produces. The veto condition is that the target’s trajectory correlation time exceeds the policy’s effective planning horizon: under that condition, the RL policy’s action is systematically phase-lagged relative to the target, and the reported Mean Error underestimates the real-world error. Self-occlusion is a second biomechanical veto: the object mask used to train the switcher may treat a self-occluded limb as background, producing label noise that is correlated with gait phase.

**Weather veto.** Garden and Urban City are outdoor scenes with “illumination variation” [PAGE 6], but the paper randomizes illumination, not precipitation, fog, or wind. Rain adds scattering and lens droplets; fog reduces contrast and shifts the appearance distribution; wind moves foliage, creating non-target motion that can trigger the appearance encoder and corrupt the mask. The switcher is calibrated on Random Room textures drawn from the Kylberg dataset [PAGE 5, PAGE 8]; out-of-distribution foliage under wind shifts the score distribution and violates the calibration assumption. The veto proof is a signal-detection argument: let \(s\) be the switcher score, and suppose the target-present and target-absent densities are \(p_1(s)\) and \(p_0(s)\). Calibration fixes a threshold \(\tau\) such that \(P(s>\tau\mid\text{absent})\) is small on the training distribution. Under wind, \(p_0\) shifts right, so the false-positive rate at fixed \(\tau\) rises; if it exceeds the true-positive rate, the pose consensus inverts and the pose-based controller actively steers away from the target. In that regime, higher collaboration makes the system worse, which is the strongest form of cross-layer veto.

Adjudication rule: if any of these veto conditions holds, the reported 9.39° mean error and 84.92% success rate [PAGE 7] are not transferable estimates; they are conditional on a plant with ideal actuators, a gait-free target, and a weather-free appearance distribution.
